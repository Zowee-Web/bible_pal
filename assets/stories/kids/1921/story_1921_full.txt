The Queen Who Came to See

Far, far away, in a land called Sheba, a queen sat in her palace and listened. Travelers came from distant places, and they all had the same story to tell. They spoke of a king named Solomon, who lived in a land far beyond the deserts.

"He is the wisest man in all the world," they said. "God himself made him so wise. Ask him any question — any riddle, any puzzle — and he knows the answer. His palace is beautiful. His table is full. And his people are glad and content."

The queen listened, and listened, and her eyes grew wide. The stories sounded almost too good to be true. How could one king be so wise? How could one land be so glad? Surely the travelers were stretching it just a little.

And yet — what if it was true?

The more the queen heard, the more one thought grew inside her, bright and certain as a morning star: "I cannot just sit here and wonder. I must go and see for myself."

So that is exactly what she did.

Now picture this, because it is the part to remember. A great caravan of camels set out from Sheba, stepping slowly across the wide, golden desert. Camel after camel after camel, swaying along under the hot sun, their bells ringing softly. Some carried baskets of sweet-smelling spices. Some carried heavy bags of shining gold. And right in the middle rode the queen herself, sitting tall, looking far ahead toward the distant land of King Solomon.

She was coming such a very, very long way — over deserts and faraway roads — just to find out if the wonderful stories were really true.

Day after day, the caravan traveled on. The sand stretched out before them, and the sun rose and set, rose and set. But the queen did not turn back. She kept her eyes on the road ahead. And at last, after many miles, the long journey ended. The towers of Solomon's city rose up before her. The queen of Sheba had arrived.

Then it was time for her questions — and oh, she had brought so many! Every riddle she could think of. Every puzzle. Every hard, tricky, knotted-up question she had been saving for years and years. She must have wondered, deep down: Will this king really know? Or are the stories just stories after all?

So she began to ask. One question. Then another. Then another.

And one by one, King Solomon answered them. Every single one. He never frowned, and he never said, "That one is too hard." There was nothing she could ask that he did not understand. Nothing in all her questions was too hard for him.

The queen could hardly believe it. So she looked around her. She walked through Solomon's beautiful palace, with its halls and its high windows. She saw how wisely and kindly he ruled. She watched his servants and his people, and saw how happy and well cared for they all were. Everywhere she turned, there was something good, something wise, something more wonderful than she had pictured back home.

And the queen of Sheba stood very still, and her heart filled all the way up to the brim with wonder.

"It is true," she said at last, almost in a whisper. "It is all true — and even more than true. They told me wonderful things about you, but the half was not even told to me! What I see with my own eyes is far greater than anything I ever heard."

And then the queen did the most beautiful thing of all. She did not only praise the king. She praised the God who had made him so wise — the God whose goodness was even bigger than the stories.

She had come a very, very long way, all because she wanted to see for herself.

And what she found at the end of that long desert road was greater, and brighter, and more wonderful than every story that had ever traveled all the way to Sheba.
