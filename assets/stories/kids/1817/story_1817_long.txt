The Loving Father

One day, Jesus told a story about a father who had two sons, and who loved them both with all his heart.

The younger son was restless. He did not want to stay home anymore. He wanted to go far away and see the whole wide world for himself. So one day he came to his father and asked to take his share of all that the family had, so he could leave.

It made the father very sad. But he did not hold his son back, and he did not lock the gate. He gave the boy what he asked for. And then he stood at the gate and watched him go, down the long road, getting smaller and smaller — until he was only a tiny speck in the distance, and then he was gone.

The young man traveled to a land far, far from home. At first it was wonderful. He had plenty of money, and plenty of friends, and plenty of fun. But little by little, he spent it all. Every last coin slipped through his fingers, until one day he reached into his pockets and found nothing there at all. And his new friends, who had laughed and feasted with him while the money lasted, were nowhere to be found now that it was gone. He was all alone in a strange land, with empty pockets and an empty tummy.

Now he was hungry. He was tired. And he was very, very lonely. The only work he could find was feeding pigs in a muddy field. He was so hungry that even the pigs' food began to look good to him. And there, sitting all alone among the pigs, he began to think about home — about his father, and the warm bread on the table, and the door that was always, always open.

"What am I doing here?" he said to himself. "Back home, even the workers have more than enough to eat — and here I am, starving. I will get up. I will go back to my father. And I will tell him, 'Father, I am so sorry. I have done wrong. I am not good enough to be called your son anymore.'"

So he stood up, and he turned toward home, and he began the long, long walk back. It was a long way, and his feet grew sore, and a worried thought followed him the whole way: What if his father was angry? What if there was no place for him anymore? But still he kept walking, step after step after step, toward home.

Now, here is the most wonderful part of the whole story. Every single day that his son had been gone, the father had walked out to the gate and looked down that road — hoping, and watching, and waiting. He had never stopped watching. And on this day, while his son was still far, far away, no more than a small and dusty shape on the horizon, the father knew him at once. He would know his own boy anywhere.

And the father's heart leaped for joy.

He did not wait at the gate. He did not stand with his arms crossed. He did not frown, or make his son walk the whole way alone. No — the old father gathered up his robe and he ran. He ran down the road as fast as he could go, all the way to his tired, ragged, dusty son. And he threw his arms around him, and held him close, and would not let go.

The son began the speech he had practiced. "Father," he said, "I am so sorry. I have done wrong. I am not good enough to be called —"

But the father would not even let him finish. He was laughing and crying all at once, hugging his boy tighter, too happy for words about anything except this: his son had come home. "Quick!" he called out to everyone. "Bring the very best coat for my son! Put a ring on his finger! My boy was gone, and now he is home again. Tonight, we are going to have a feast!"

For the father had been far happier to see his son come home than he ever was interested in hearing him explain. He did not love his son because the boy was perfect. He loved him because he was his.

And so they did. The best food was cooked, and music filled the house, and everyone celebrated together — not because the son had done everything right, but simply because he had come home, and he was loved. And there was such great joy in that house that night, with music and good food and laughing, that it lasted all the evening long.
