Penny saw the wrapper hit the ground before anyone else did.

It was lunch recess. The yard was full of kids — some on the swings, some on the basketball court, some sitting on the low wall eating their lunches. Penny was on the bench by the bike rack, which was her favorite spot because it was far enough from the noise to think but close enough to see everything.

She was eating an apple and watching. That's what Penny did at recess most days. She watched.

A boy from the grade above — his name was Derek, he was tall, he had a loud voice and was always surrounded by four or five friends — had just finished his granola bar. He was walking past the big trash can by the water fountain. Without looking, he tossed the wrapper sideways. It landed on the concrete. Six inches from the trash can. Right next to it. Close enough that he obviously knew where the can was. He just didn't bother.

His friends kept walking. Derek kept walking. The wrapper sat on the ground, shiny and crinkled, the wind nudging it slightly.

Nobody said anything. There were at least fifteen kids within ten feet of that wrapper. Nobody bent down. Nobody called out.

Penny looked at the wrapper. She looked at the trash can. She looked at Derek's back getting farther away.

It wasn't her wrapper. It wasn't her problem. She could finish her apple and go inside when the bell rang and the wrapper would either blow away or a janitor would get it after school.

She stood up.

Her legs did the thing they always did when she was nervous — the trembly, watery feeling, like her knees were made of something that wasn't quite solid. The same feeling she got before oral reports. The same feeling she got when the doctor said "this will pinch."

She walked over to the wrapper. She bent down, picked it up, and dropped it in the trash can. The metal lid clanged.

Derek turned around. He was fifteen feet away. His friends stopped too.

"What are you doing?" Derek asked.

"You missed," Penny said.

"I didn't miss."

"The trash can's right there." She pointed at it. Her voice came out thinner than she wanted, but it came out. She kept her hand pointed at the can.

Derek looked at her. He was a full head taller. His friends had their arms crossed. One of them smirked.

"I didn't miss," Derek said again, slower this time. "I just didn't care."

"It was six inches away."

There was a pause. The kind of pause where anything could happen. Penny's heart hammered in her ears. Her fingers tightened around her apple.

Then Derek laughed. Not a mean laugh — a surprised one, like he hadn't expected a fourth-grader on a bench to say anything to him at all. "Okay, trash police." He shook his head and walked away. His friends followed.

Penny went back to the bench. She sat down. Her heart was still going fast. She looked at her apple — there was a bruise on the side where she'd been squeezing it.

She sat there for a minute, not eating, just breathing.

A girl she didn't recognize — maybe second grade, small, with a blue headband — walked past the bench. She stopped. She bent down, picked up a straw wrapper that was lying near the water fountain, and carried it to the trash can. She dropped it in.

Then she looked at Penny. A small, quiet look. She nodded once.

Penny nodded back.

The bell rang. Kids started moving toward the doors. Penny picked up her backpack and took a bite of her apple. It was a little bruised on one side, but it still tasted fine.
