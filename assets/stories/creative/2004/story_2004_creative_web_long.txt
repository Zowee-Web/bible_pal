Elara lived in a small cottage at the edge of a wide meadow. The cottage had a round blue door and a chimney that puffed soft smoke in the mornings. Wildflowers grew along the stone path that led from the door to the garden gate. Bees hummed around the lavender bushes, and the air always smelled sweet there. Her grandmother kept a birdhouse on a post near the garden, painted yellow with tiny white dots. Elara loved to sit on the porch steps and listen to the birds sing their morning songs.

The meadow stretched out behind the cottage like a green blanket spread over the earth. Tall grass swayed when the wind blew, making a soft whispering sound. A little creek ran along the far side, where smooth stones sat in the shallow water. Butterflies drifted from flower to flower in no particular hurry at all. Sometimes a rabbit would hop through the grass and pause to wiggle its nose. Elara thought the meadow was the most beautiful place she had ever known.

One morning, Elara walked out to the garden to water the tomato plants. She carried a small green watering can that her grandmother had given her last spring. The soil was warm under her bare feet, and dew still clung to the leaves. As she bent down near the rosemary bush, she heard a tiny chirping sound. She looked carefully and found a small brown bird sitting very still on the ground. Its feathers were ruffled, and one wing stuck out at an odd angle.

Elara set down her watering can and knelt in the soft dirt. The bird looked up at her with round dark eyes that blinked slowly. It chirped again, a quiet little sound like a question being asked. She could see its small chest rising and falling with each quick breath. Elara cupped her hands gently around the bird without squeezing at all. She could feel the warmth of its tiny body and the flutter of its heartbeat against her palms.

She carried the bird inside and found her grandmother making oatmeal at the stove. Her grandmother looked at the small bird and smiled in a knowing way. Together they lined a shoebox with a soft cotton cloth and set it on the kitchen table. They placed the bird inside, and it nestled into the cloth right away. Her grandmother showed Elara how to offer water from a tiny dropper, one drop at a time. The bird opened its beak and drank, and Elara felt her whole heart fill up with tenderness.

Over the next few days, Elara cared for the bird every single morning. She woke early and tiptoed to the kitchen before the sun had fully risen. She gave the bird water and tiny bits of seed from her grandmother's pantry. She talked to the bird in a soft voice, telling it about the meadow and the creek. The bird would tilt its head and listen, as if it understood every word she said. Its ruffled feathers began to smooth out, and the wing that had stuck out slowly tucked back in.

By the fourth day, the bird hopped around inside the shoebox with energy. It flapped its wings and made the cotton cloth ripple beneath its feet. Elara laughed and clapped her hands softly whenever it tried a little jump. She named the bird Pip because of the small sounds it made each morning. Her grandmother watched from the doorway and nodded with a warm, quiet expression. Elara began to imagine Pip staying with her forever, perched on her shoulder while she watered the garden.

On the fifth morning, Pip flew out of the shoebox and landed on the windowsill. The window was open, and a breeze carried the scent of honeysuckle into the kitchen. Pip looked outside at the meadow, where other birds were singing in the tall grass. It spread its wings wide and then folded them back again. Elara watched and felt something tighten in her chest, just a little bit. She reached out and gently stroked Pip's feathers with one finger.

Her grandmother came and sat beside her at the table with two cups of warm tea. She wrapped her hands around her cup and looked at Pip on the windowsill. "That little bird has gotten so strong," her grandmother said quietly. Elara nodded but didn't say anything for a moment. She took a sip of tea that tasted like chamomile and honey. "I want Pip to stay here with me," Elara whispered, looking down at her cup.

Her grandmother reached over and placed a warm hand on top of Elara's. "When I plant seeds in the garden, I water them and care for them every day," she said. "But I don't hold the flowers closed when they are ready to bloom." She smiled and squeezed Elara's hand gently. Elara looked back at Pip, who was chirping toward the open window. The morning air was fresh and full of birdsong from the meadow beyond the garden gate. Elara felt the warmth of her grandmother's hand and the warmth of the tea all at once.

That afternoon, Elara carried Pip outside to the garden on her open palm. The sun was high and golden, and the lavender bushes hummed with bees. Pip looked around at the wide open sky and fluffed its feathers. Elara lifted her hand up slowly, feeling the bird's small feet grip and then relax. "You can go, Pip," she said softly, and her voice was steady and kind. Pip spread its wings, pushed off from her palm, and rose into the air in a smooth, gentle arc.

Elara watched Pip fly over the rosemary bush and past the garden gate. The little bird swooped low over the tall meadow grass and then climbed higher. It joined two other birds near the creek, and they sang together in bright, clear notes. Elara's grandmother stood behind her and rested a hand on her shoulder. Elara could still feel the light press of Pip's feet on her palm, like a tiny memory held in her skin. She smiled and leaned back against her grandmother, listening to the birdsong that filled the whole meadow.

The next morning, Elara sat on the porch steps with her watering can beside her. The garden smelled like rosemary and damp earth after a light rain in the night. She heard a familiar chirp and looked up at the yellow birdhouse on the post. Pip was sitting right on top of it, head tilted, looking straight at her. Elara laughed and waved, and Pip chirped twice before flying off toward the creek. She picked up her watering can and walked into the garden, humming a small tune that matched the rhythm of the birds.
