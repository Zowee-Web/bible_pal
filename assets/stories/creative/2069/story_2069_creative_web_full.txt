The potatoes were still warm.

Margaret set the bowl on the table between the green beans and the rolls, same as every Sunday for thirty-one years. Four place settings. She had laid four before catching herself, standing with the extra plate pressed flat against her chest, the white ceramic cool through her blouse.

"Mom." Rachel touched her elbow. "Sit down."

Margaret returned the plate to the cabinet. She closed the door carefully, the way a person closes something they don't want to hear shut. She did not look at the chair.

Tom said grace. His voice held steady for the first eight words and then it cracked like a stick underfoot. He pressed through. He said amen. Nobody reached for a serving spoon. The silence had weight, the kind that settles into furniture and walls.

Rachel served herself potatoes and passed the bowl to her left, toward the empty space, then caught herself and reversed direction, passing it right to her father. The scrape of serving spoons. The clink of ice settling in glasses of water. These were the only sounds for a long while.

Outside, October wind stripped the last leaves from the maple that Danny had climbed when he was nine. He had fallen from the third branch and broken his left arm, and Margaret had driven him to the hospital with the radio on because he asked her to, because he said the music helped. She could not remember the song. She had tried.

"These rolls are good," Tom said.

"Store-bought," Margaret said.

"Still good."

The chair sat three feet from the table's edge. Oak, with a spindle back, same as the other three. Tom had built them in his shop the winter before Rachel was born, sanding each spindle by hand until it was smooth enough to run a silk cloth over without catching. A small dent marked the top rail where Danny had tipped it backward onto the tile floor one Thanksgiving, twelve years old and laughing at his own joke. Margaret had been furious. She had pointed at the dent and told him that wood doesn't forget.

She would give nearly anything to hear that crack again.

Rachel ate slowly. She watched her mother cut a green bean into four precise pieces and eat none of them.

"He loved your potatoes," Rachel said.

Margaret set her knife down. "I know."

"He said they were better than any restaurant. Every single time we sat here. He said it every time, even when he was—" Rachel stopped. She pressed her napkin to her mouth and breathed through it.

"Even at the end," Margaret finished.

Tom reached across the table and covered Margaret's hand with his. His palm was rough from forty years of woodworking, warm from holding his coffee before dinner. She turned her wrist beneath his grip and held on. Rachel placed her hand on top of theirs. Three hands stacked on the pine table, the grain dark with age beneath them.

The furnace clicked on somewhere below the floor. Warm air moved through the vent near the window, stirring the white curtain. Beyond the glass, the stripped maple stood against a sky turning violet at its lowest edge, the branches sharp and bare and reaching.

A car passed on the street. Its headlights swept across the dining room wall and vanished.

Margaret picked up her fork. She put a small bite of potato in her mouth and chewed. The salt and butter were right. She had made them the way Danny liked, with the skins still on, because that was the only way she knew how to make them now.

Rachel poured more water into her mother's glass. Tom squeezed Margaret's hand once and let go, returning to his plate. The three of them sat in the quiet of the room, the October wind pressing leaves along the sidewalk outside, the empty chair holding its silence among them.

On the kitchen counter behind Margaret, a photograph in a wooden frame caught the overhead light. A boy in a baseball cap, nine years old, his left arm in a cast, grinning so wide his eyes nearly closed. The frame was oak. Tom had made it from the same wood as the chairs.

Margaret took another bite. Then another. The furnace hummed beneath them, pushing warmth up through the floor and into their shoes and through the soles of their feet, steady and unseen.
