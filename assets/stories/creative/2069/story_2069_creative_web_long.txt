The potatoes were still warm.

Margaret set the bowl on the table between the green beans and the dinner rolls, same position as every Sunday for thirty-one years, the white ceramic centered on the same faded potholder Rachel had sewn in a home economics class a lifetime ago. Four place settings. She had laid four before catching herself. She stood at the counter holding the extra plate against her chest, the cool ceramic pressing through her blouse into her sternum, and she did not move for a long time.

"Mom." Rachel was in the doorway. "Sit down."

Margaret returned the plate to the cabinet. She placed it on the stack with the others, aligning the rim. She closed the door the way a person closes something they don't want to hear shut. She smoothed her blouse. She walked to the table and sat in her chair and folded her hands and did not look at the chair across from her.

Tom said grace. His voice held steady for eight words. Then it broke, a clean fracture right through the middle of a sentence about thankfulness. He pressed through. He said amen. His chin was down. Nobody reached for a serving spoon.

The silence had weight. Not the comfortable kind that sometimes settled over Sunday dinners when everyone was content and full, but the other kind. The kind that presses into furniture and walls and stays.

Rachel served herself potatoes and passed the bowl to her left, toward the empty space, a motion so automatic her arm was already extended before she caught it. She pulled the bowl back and passed it right, to her father. Tom took it. Neither of them mentioned it.

The scrape of a serving spoon against ceramic. The clink of ice settling in glasses of water that no one had touched. These were the only sounds for what felt like minutes.

Outside, October wind stripped the last leaves from the maple in the front yard. Danny had climbed that tree when he was nine. Third branch up, the one that angled over the driveway. He had reached for a branch that wasn't strong enough and fallen eight feet onto grass and gravel, and Margaret had found him sitting cross-legged on the lawn holding his left arm with his right, not crying, just looking at the odd angle of his wrist with scientific curiosity.

She drove him to the hospital with the radio on because he asked her to. He said the music made his arm hurt less. She had tried many times to remember what song was playing. She could not. She remembered his sneakers on the dashboard, the left one untied, and the way he hummed along to whatever it was, and that was all.

"These rolls are good," Tom said.

"Store-bought," Margaret said.

"Still good."

Tom buttered a roll and set it on the edge of his plate. He picked up his fork, put it down, picked it up again. His hands were large and square and capable. They looked wrong holding a fork with nothing to do.

The chair sat three feet from the table's edge. Oak, with a spindle back, same as the other three. Tom had built all four in his workshop the winter before Rachel was born. He'd spent three weekends sanding each spindle by hand until the wood was smooth enough to run a silk cloth across without catching a single fiber. A small dent marked the top rail of Danny's chair, a crescent-shaped divot in the oak where he'd tipped it backward onto the tile floor one Thanksgiving. Twelve years old. Laughing at his own joke about cranberry sauce. The chair hit the tile with a sound like a gunshot and Danny scrambled up still laughing while Margaret pointed at the dent and said, Wood doesn't forget, Daniel.

She would give almost anything to hear that crack again. The joke too. She couldn't remember the joke.

Rachel ate slowly, moving her fork in small precise motions, cutting her chicken into pieces far smaller than necessary. She was watching her mother. Margaret could feel it.

"He loved your potatoes," Rachel said.

Margaret set her knife across her plate. "I know."

"He said they were better than any restaurant. Every single time. Even the time you burned them, he said they were better with the crispy parts." Rachel almost smiled. The almost was worse than if she hadn't tried. "He said it every time, even when—"

She stopped. She pressed her napkin to her mouth and breathed through the cloth.

"Even at the end," Margaret said.

"Yeah."

The word sat between them, thin and final.

Tom reached across the table and covered Margaret's hand with his. His palm was rough from forty years of working wood, callused across the base of each finger, warm from holding his coffee mug before dinner. Margaret turned her wrist beneath his grip and held on. She could feel his pulse in his thumb. Or maybe it was hers. She couldn't tell.

Rachel placed her hand on top of both of theirs. Three hands stacked on the old pine table, over a water ring Danny had made with a glass of lemonade fifteen summers ago. The grain was dark with age. Margaret stared at their hands and thought of nothing. She was grateful for the nothing. Thought was the thing she couldn't afford tonight.

The furnace clicked on beneath the floor. Warm air moved through the iron vent near the window, stirring the white curtain. The curtain swayed once and settled. Beyond the glass, the stripped maple stood against a sky turning violet at its lowest edge, the branches sharp and bare and reaching upward into the darker blue above.

A car passed on the street. Its headlights swept across the dining room wall, over the framed photographs and the clock that needed a new battery and the pencil marks on the doorframe where Margaret had measured both children every January first. The light moved across these things and vanished and the room was warm and dim again.

Margaret picked up her fork. She put a small bite of potato in her mouth and chewed. The salt and butter were right. She had made them the way Danny liked, with the skins left on and a little too much pepper, because that was the only way she knew how to make them anymore. Her hands had learned his preference and would not unlearn it.

"Good potatoes, Mom," Rachel said.

"Thank you."

Rachel poured more water into her mother's glass from the blue pitcher. The water caught the overhead light as it fell. Tom squeezed Margaret's hand once more and released it, returning to his plate. He ate a bite of chicken. He ate a roll. He chewed with the slow deliberation of a man performing a task because it must be performed.

On the kitchen counter behind Margaret, a photograph in a small wooden frame caught the edge of the light. A boy in a red baseball cap, nine years old, his left arm in a white cast already covered in signatures, grinning so wide his eyes had nearly disappeared. The frame was oak. Tom had made it from the same board he'd used for the chairs, a scrap piece he'd kept in a coffee can of cutoffs on his workbench.

The furnace hummed beneath them, pushing warmth up through the floor, through the tile and into their shoes and through the soles of their feet. Margaret took another bite. Then another. She chewed and swallowed and the food was warm going down.

Rachel stood and carried her plate to the sink. She ran the water. She began to wash the serving bowls, and the sound of it filled the kitchen — water on ceramic, the soft brush of the sponge, the clink of a bowl set in the drying rack. Normal sounds. Sunday sounds.

Tom brought his plate to the counter. He picked up a dish towel and stood beside his daughter and dried what she handed him. Margaret watched them from the table. Rachel's hair was pulled back with a clip. Tom's shoulders were rounded forward. They moved in a rhythm they didn't have to discuss.

Margaret looked at the empty chair. She looked at it fully, for the first time that evening. The spindle back. The dent in the rail. The seat worn smooth from years of use. She reached across the table and rested her hand on it, her fingers curling over the edge of the seat where his legs had hung as a child, where he had sat as a man.

The wood was cool beneath her palm.

At the sink, water ran. Tom folded the towel over the oven handle. Rachel dried her hands on her jeans. Outside, the wind moved through the bare maple and the last leaf on the lowest branch let go and turned twice in the air and landed on the porch step, settling there, resting against the welcome mat in the yellow square of light that fell through the window from the kitchen where the three of them stood together, still standing, the dinner plates clean and stacked, the table not yet cleared of its empty chair.
