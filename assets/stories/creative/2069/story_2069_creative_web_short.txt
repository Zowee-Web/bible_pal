The potatoes were still warm.

Margaret set the bowl on the table between the green beans and the rolls, same as every Sunday. Four place settings. She had laid four before catching herself, and then she stood at the counter holding the extra plate against her chest like something fragile she'd found in the road.

"Mom." Rachel touched her elbow. "Sit down."

Margaret put the plate back in the cabinet. She did not look at the chair.

Tom said grace. His voice was steady for the first eight words and then it wasn't. He pressed through. He said amen. Nobody moved for a moment.

Rachel served herself potatoes and passed the bowl to her left, toward the empty space, and then pulled it back and passed it right instead. The scrape of serving spoons. The clink of ice in glasses. Outside, October wind stripped the last leaves from the maple that Danny had climbed when he was nine and broken his arm falling from.

"These rolls are good," Tom said.

"Store-bought," Margaret said.

"Still good."

The chair sat three feet from the table's edge. Oak, with a spindle back, same as the others. A small dent in the top rail where Danny had tipped it backward onto the tile floor one Thanksgiving. Margaret had been furious. She would give almost anything to hear that crack again.

Rachel ate slowly. She watched her mother cut a green bean into four pieces and eat none of them.

"He loved your potatoes," Rachel said.

Margaret nodded. Her fork rested on the rim of her plate.

"He said they were better than any restaurant." Rachel's voice was thinner now. "He said that every time."

"He did."

Tom reached across the table and covered Margaret's hand with his own. His palm was rough and warm. She turned her wrist and held on.

The furnace clicked on somewhere beneath the floor. Warm air moved through the vent near the window, shifting the white curtain. Beyond the glass, the stripped maple stood against a sky turning violet at its edges.

Margaret picked up her fork. She put a small bite of potato in her mouth and chewed. The three of them sat in the quiet, the wind outside pressing leaves along the sidewalk, the empty chair holding its silence among them like a guest who had simply arrived before the others and was waiting, patient and still.
