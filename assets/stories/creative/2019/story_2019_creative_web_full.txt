The summer Corinne turned twelve, her grandmother taught her to make bread without measuring.

She hadn't thought about that in years. But now, sitting in the teachers' lounge with a stack of ungraded essays and a resignation letter half-typed on her laptop, the memory arrived like a guest who doesn't knock. It simply walked in and sat down.

Her grandmother's hands, dusted with flour. The wooden bowl with the crack along the rim. The kitchen that always smelled like yeast and coffee and the particular warmth of a stove that had been on since dawn. The way her grandmother would say, "You'll know when the dough is ready because it'll push back at you. Like it's got something to say."

Corinne had loved that — the idea that something you were making could talk back to you, could tell you when it was ready to become what it was meant to be.

The lounge door opened. DeShawn Walker, the music teacher, dropped into the chair across from her with his brown lunch bag and a tired smile. He had chalk dust on his sleeve, though he taught music and had no reason for chalk dust. That was DeShawn. He helped wherever he wandered.

"You look like I feel," he said.

"That bad?"

"I lost three students from jazz band this week. Budget cuts took the after-school funding." He unwrapped a sandwich — turkey, no mustard, same as every day. "You?"

Corinne looked at the blinking cursor on her screen. The letter was two paragraphs long. She'd written "I regret to inform you" and stalled, because she didn't regret it, not exactly. She was just tired. Eighteen years of tired.

"I'm thinking about quitting," she said.

DeShawn chewed slowly. He didn't flinch or lean forward or rush to talk her out of it. He just chewed and looked at her with his steady, dark eyes, giving the words room to exist.

"When I was twenty-four," he said, "I played a gig at a nursing home. Community outreach thing. Worst piano they'd ever bought — an old upright with half the keys sticking. The sustain pedal was gone. I wanted to walk out before the first song." He took a sip of water. "But there was this woman in the front row, silver hair, hands folded in her lap. She closed her eyes during the first song and didn't open them until the last. Twenty minutes, eyes closed, just listening."

Corinne waited.

"Afterward she wheeled herself over and said, 'Young man, that piano hasn't sounded that good in years.'"

He smiled. "The piano was terrible. Objectively terrible. But she heard something through it. Something the instrument couldn't ruin, no matter how broken it was."

The lounge was quiet. The fluorescent lights hummed. Someone's coffee maker gurgled on the counter.

Corinne looked down at the stack of essays. Jayla Robinson's was on top, the handwriting uneven, the margins full of small drawings — birds, mostly, and one careful tree. Jayla, who'd come to her after class last week with her backpack sliding off one shoulder and asked, shyly, if she could write an extra essay. Not for credit. Just for practice.

"The dough pushes back," Corinne said quietly.

DeShawn raised an eyebrow. "What?"

"Nothing. Something my grandmother used to say. About bread."

He nodded as if this made perfect sense, which, between the two of them, it somehow did.

Corinne closed the laptop without saving. The resignation letter disappeared without protest, like a breath on a cold window. She picked up Jayla's essay, uncapped a red pen, and began to read.

The handwriting was messy. The thesis was buried in the third paragraph. But somewhere in the middle of the page, Jayla had written a sentence so clear and true that Corinne read it twice and felt the dough push back.
