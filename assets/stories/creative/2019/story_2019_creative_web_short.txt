The summer Corinne turned twelve, her grandmother taught her to make bread without measuring.

She hadn't thought about that in years. But now, sitting in the teachers' lounge with a stack of ungraded essays and a resignation letter half-typed on her laptop, the memory arrived like a guest who doesn't knock.

Her grandmother's hands, dusted with flour. The wooden bowl. The way she'd say, "You'll know when the dough is ready because it'll push back at you. Like it's got something to say."

The lounge door opened. DeShawn Walker, the music teacher, dropped into the chair across from her with his lunch bag and a tired smile.

"You look like I feel," he said.

"That bad?"

"I lost three students from jazz band this week. Budget cuts." He unwrapped a sandwich. "You?"

Corinne looked at the blinking cursor on her screen. "I'm thinking about quitting."

DeShawn chewed slowly. He didn't flinch or rush to talk her out of it.

"When I was twenty-four," he said, "I played a gig at a nursing home. Worst piano they'd ever bought. Half the keys stuck. I wanted to walk out." He took a sip of water. "But there was this woman in the front row. She closed her eyes during the first song and didn't open them until the last. Afterward she said, 'Young man, that piano hasn't sounded that good in years.'"

Corinne waited.

"The piano was terrible," DeShawn said. "But she heard something through it. Something real."

Corinne looked down at the essays. Jayla Robinson's was on top, the handwriting uneven, the margins full of small drawings. Jayla, who'd come to her after class last week and asked if she could write an extra essay, just for practice.

"The dough pushes back," Corinne said quietly.

"What?"

"Nothing. Something my grandmother used to say."

She closed the laptop without saving. The resignation letter disappeared like a breath on a window. She picked up Jayla's essay and a red pen and began to read.
