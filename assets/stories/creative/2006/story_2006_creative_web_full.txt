The morning Suki rowed out, the fog sat so thick on the harbor that she couldn't see the bow of her own skiff. She was seventeen, small-boned, with calloused hands that looked like they belonged to someone twice her age. Her father had taught her to fish these waters before his back gave out, and now she went alone most mornings, setting nets along the familiar line of buoys that marked the safe fishing grounds. The other fishers in Calder's Cove respected the buoys. Everyone did. Past the last one, the currents turned unpredictable, and the seafloor dropped into a trench so deep the old-timers said it had no bottom.

She was hauling in her second net, picking through a modest catch of mackerel, when she heard it — a sound that didn't belong to the ocean. It was a high, broken cry, almost like a child, carried from somewhere beyond the fog. Suki stopped moving. The cry came again, weaker. She wiped her hands on her canvas pants and looked toward the last buoy, a rusted red barrel bobbing forty yards east. The sound was coming from past it.

Her hands tightened on the oars. She thought about her father sitting at the kitchen table, waiting for her to come home with enough fish to sell at the afternoon market. She thought about the rent due on Friday and the engine part they still couldn't afford. Every practical thing in her life pointed back toward shore.

But the crying didn't stop.

Suki rowed past the last buoy. The water changed almost immediately — darker, colder, the current pulling at the hull with a new insistence. She kept her strokes even and steady, the way her father had taught her when the sea got rough. "Don't fight it," he always said. "Just keep your rhythm and let the boat do its work."

Three hundred yards out, she found the source. A young harbor seal was tangled in a mess of discarded netting and plastic line, the kind that drifted in from commercial trawlers farther out. The animal had wrapped itself tighter with every struggle. One flipper was pinned against its body, and the netting had cut into the skin near its neck. Its dark eyes were glassy with exhaustion.

Suki brought the skiff alongside and leaned over the gunwale. The seal thrashed weakly, and the boat tipped hard enough that cold water sloshed over her boots. She grabbed the netting with both hands and started cutting with her bait knife, working carefully around the wounds. The seal snapped at her twice. She didn't pull away. She just waited each time, then went back to cutting.

It took twenty minutes. Her arms ached and her fingers were numb by the time the last strand came free. The seal slipped beneath the surface and vanished. Suki sat back in the skiff, breathing hard, her knife still in her hand. The fog had started to thin. She could see the outline of the coast now, the dark shape of the breakwater, the faint cluster of houses above the harbor.

She'd lost most of the morning. Her nets still needed hauling, and the mackerel she'd already caught were warming in the hold. It would be a short sale at the market, maybe not enough for the rent.

Suki picked up the oars and started rowing back toward the buoy line. Halfway there, a school of bluefish broke the surface around her boat — dozens of them, more than she'd seen in one place all season. They churned the water silver. She grabbed her casting net and threw it in one clean motion, the way she'd practiced a thousand times on the dock as a kid.

When she hauled it in, the net was so heavy she had to brace her feet against the hull.

That evening, her father looked at the catch spread across the sorting table and then looked at her. "How'd you manage all this?"

Suki hung her jacket on the hook by the door. "I rowed out a little farther than usual."
