The morning fog sat low on the harbor, thick enough that Suki could taste the salt of it on her lips. She was seventeen, and she'd been pulling nets since she was nine. Her skiff was small — twelve feet of sun-bleached wood with oars that creaked like old doors.

The last buoy was painted orange, faded to almost pink. Every fisher in Calloway Bay knew to turn back there. Past that marker, the currents ran unpredictable. The seafloor dropped off into channels nobody had mapped. Her father had told her once, plainly: "Nothing good waits past the orange."

But she heard it — a low, rhythmic slapping against the surface. Not a fish. Something heavier, more deliberate. She shipped her oars and listened. There it was again, maybe forty yards beyond the buoy. A sound like an animal struggling to keep its head above water.

Suki gripped the oars. Her hands were already raw from the morning's work, and the fog made everything past ten feet a guess. She looked back toward the harbor. The other boats were gray shapes, barely visible, heading in.

She pulled hard on the left oar and swung the bow past the orange buoy.

The current grabbed the skiff immediately, tugging it sideways. She corrected, rowing steady, counting her strokes. At twenty-two, the fog thinned just enough. A young seal was tangled in a mess of netting and plastic line, wrapped so tight around its flippers that it could barely move. Its dark eyes tracked her as she approached.

"Easy," she whispered. "Easy now."

She reached over the gunwale with her bait knife and began cutting. The seal thrashed once, and cold water sloshed over her knees. She held firm, sawing through the last loop of line. The netting fell away into the dark water.

The seal slipped free and dove. For a moment there was nothing — just Suki alone in her skiff past the last marker, soaked, breathing hard, hands shaking.

Then the seal surfaced three feet from her bow. It watched her for a long breath, its whiskers dripping. It barked once, sharp and clear, and disappeared beneath the surface.

Suki wiped her hands on her jacket and picked up the oars. The current was still pulling, but she knew the way back.
