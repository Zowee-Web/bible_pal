Wanda couldn't make herself go inside the house.

She sat in the driveway with the engine off and the windows down, listening to the neighborhood — a lawnmower two streets over, the Hendersons' dog barking at nothing, the particular silence of a Wednesday afternoon when everyone else's life is continuing normally.

Ray had been gone four months. A hundred and nineteen days, though she'd stopped counting aloud because people looked at her strangely when she said the number. The house was the same house. Same brick, same crooked mailbox post he'd always meant to fix, same azaleas he'd planted the spring before, now leggy and overgrown because she couldn't bring herself to prune them. His hands had shaped them. Cutting them felt like erasing.

But going inside meant walking through rooms shaped by his absence — the indentation in his side of the couch, the coffee mug she couldn't put away, the reading glasses on the nightstand. Today she didn't have it in her.

She got out of the car and walked. No destination. Just away from the front door.

She ended up at the laundromat on Hatch Street, six blocks from home. The air inside was warm and humid, thick with detergent and hot cotton. Machines tumbled and sloshed — washers swishing, dryers turning with their soft, steady thump.

A girl sat on the folding counter by the back wall, maybe ten years old, legs swinging. She was reading a book with a cracked spine, her chin propped on her fist. Her braids were tied with yellow ribbons that matched her sneakers.

Wanda sat in a plastic chair near the window. She didn't have laundry. She didn't have a reason to be there. She just sat and let the warm air settle around her.

The girl looked up. "You waiting for a machine?"

"No. Just sitting."

The girl considered this. "My grandma does that sometimes. Just sits somewhere and breathes for a while."

"Does she?"

"Yeah. She says sometimes your legs bring you where you need to be, and you just have to trust them."

Wanda looked at the girl. Her eyes were serious and clear, the way children's eyes are when they're repeating something they believe completely, the way you believe in gravity or rain.

A dryer buzzed. The girl hopped off the counter and began pulling warm towels into a basket, folding them with practiced hands. She worked without rushing. Each towel came out neat and square. Pull, shake, fold, place. The rhythm was steady and unhurried.

Wanda watched. There was nothing remarkable about it. A child folding towels on a Wednesday. But something about the steadiness of her hands, the unselfconscious care she took with each one, moved through the humid air like a heartbeat. It was the same care Ray had taken — the slow way he'd dried dishes, the patience in his hands.

The tightness behind Wanda's eyes softened. She didn't cry. She just breathed. And for the first time in weeks, the breathing didn't hurt. It just felt like breathing — air in, air out, the body doing its faithful work.

The girl finished her stack. She patted the top towel and looked over with a smile — not pitying, not knowing, just open and uncomplicated.

"Bye," the girl said, hefting the basket against her hip.

"Bye, sweetheart," Wanda said.

The door swung shut. The machines kept turning. The warm air kept holding.

Wanda sat a while longer, hands resting in her lap, letting her legs trust that they'd brought her exactly where she needed to be.
