Wanda couldn't make herself go inside the house.

She sat in the driveway with the engine off and the windows down. A lawnmower two streets over. The Hendersons' dog barking at nothing. The particular silence of a Wednesday afternoon when everyone else's life is continuing normally.

Ray had been gone four months. The house was the same house. Same brick, same crooked mailbox, same azaleas he'd planted the spring before. But going inside meant walking through rooms shaped by his absence.

She got out of the car and walked. No destination. Just away from the front door.

She ended up at the laundromat on Hatch Street. The air inside was warm and humid, thick with detergent and hot cotton. Machines tumbled and sloshed in their patient rhythms.

A girl sat on the folding counter, maybe ten, legs swinging, reading a book with a cracked spine.

Wanda sat in a plastic chair near the window. She didn't have laundry. She just sat.

The girl looked up. "You waiting for a machine?"

"No. Just sitting."

"My grandma does that sometimes. Just sits."

"Does she?"

"Yeah. She says sometimes your legs bring you where you need to be, and you just have to trust them."

A dryer buzzed. The girl hopped down and began pulling warm towels into a basket, folding them with practiced hands. Pull, fold, stack. Each one neat and square.

Wanda watched. There was nothing remarkable about it. But something about the steadiness of those small hands, the unselfconscious care, made the tightness behind her eyes soften.

She didn't cry. She just breathed. And for the first time in weeks, the breathing didn't hurt.

The girl finished. She smiled — not pitying, not knowing, just open.

"Bye," the girl said, carrying the basket toward the door.

"Bye," Wanda said.

The machines kept turning. The warm air kept holding. She sat a while longer, letting her legs trust that they'd brought her where she needed to be.
