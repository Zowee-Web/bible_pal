Marta knelt in the strip of dirt between the sidewalk and the street, the same strip the city had paved over twice and she'd broken open twice. Her knees ached against the ground. Seventy-eight years had made kneeling a negotiation with her own body, but she'd brought the foam pad from her kitchen and she managed.

The sapling was a red oak, barely taller than her mailbox. She worked the roots into the hole with her fingers, pressing soil around them the way she'd press a blanket around a child's shoulders. Three houses down, the maple she'd planted eleven years ago now shaded half the Rodriguez family's front yard. Their kids did homework under it in September when the house got stuffy.

A jogger slowed and watched her. "That's going to be a big tree someday."

"That's the idea," Marta said without looking up.

"You'll have great shade."

She laughed, a short warm sound. "Honey, I won't be here for that. This one's for whoever moves in after whoever moves in after me."

The jogger stood there a moment, then kept going.

Marta patted the soil firm and sat back on her heels. Her hands were black with dirt, her lower back singing a familiar complaint. She poured water from a repurposed milk jug, slow, letting the ground drink before she added more.

Across the street, old Mr. Keene sat on his porch beneath a pear tree that someone else had planted decades before either of them lived on this block. He raised his coffee mug toward her. She raised her muddy hand back.

She thought about her grandmother, who had built the stone wall behind the family garden — not for herself, but to keep the hillside from sliding onto whatever came next. The wall still stood. Marta had eaten raspberries against it as a girl, warm from the sun the stones held.

She pushed herself up, one hand on her knee, and brushed the dirt from her pants. The sapling's few leaves caught a breeze and trembled. Marta gathered her milk jug and her foam pad, tucked them under one arm, and walked inside to wash her hands.
