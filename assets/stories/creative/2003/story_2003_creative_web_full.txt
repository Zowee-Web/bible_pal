Marta Solis had bad knees and a good shovel. Every Tuesday morning, she loaded her wheelbarrow with saplings from the nursery behind the community center and pushed it down Elm Creek Road, which hadn't had an actual elm on it for twenty years. The sidewalks were cracked and pale in the heat. Most of the yards along the road belonged to renters who came and went with the seasons, and nobody had planted anything permanent in a long time.

She started with the strip of city land between the sidewalk and the curb, the part everyone walked past and nobody claimed. The first Tuesday, she put in a red maple. The soil was mostly clay and gravel, and she had to break it up with a pickaxe before she could even use the shovel. A man sitting on his porch across the street watched her for a while, then called out, "You know that thing won't give shade for fifteen years." Marta wiped her forehead with her sleeve. "Then it better get started," she said.

By the fourth week, she'd planted a row of five saplings along the road. Each one was barely taller than a broomstick, held upright with a wooden stake and a strip of old cloth. She watered them from a jug she carried in the wheelbarrow. Her daughter, Lucia, drove by one afternoon and pulled over. "Mamá, what are you doing out here? It's ninety-two degrees." Marta pointed at the newest sapling, a young sycamore with leaves the size of her palm. "Watering," she said simply. Lucia shook her head but came back the next Tuesday with a second jug and a wide-brimmed hat for her mother.

The neighborhood noticed in its own slow way. A teenager named Devon, who cut through the strip on his way to the bus stop, started checking the stakes after storms to make sure none had blown loose. He never said anything to Marta about it, but she found the stakes straightened and the cloth ties retied in neat knots she didn't recognize. The woman who ran the laundromat two blocks over left a bag of mulch at the base of the red maple one morning with a note that just said "for the tree."

Marta didn't keep a list of what she planted or where. She didn't take pictures. When the pastor at her church mentioned her project during announcements, she looked down at her hands and said she wasn't running a project, she was just putting trees in the ground. After the service, three people asked if they could help the following Tuesday. She told them to bring gloves.

By October, there were fourteen saplings along Elm Creek Road. Most of them had dropped their leaves already, and they looked fragile standing there, thin and bare against the cooling sky. Marta knew some of them wouldn't survive the winter. She'd lose two or three, maybe more. That was how it worked. But the red maple had put on a full inch of new growth since spring, and its root system was already spreading beneath the surface in ways she couldn't see but trusted were happening.

On the last Tuesday before the first frost, Lucia found her mother kneeling beside a newly planted dogwood at the far end of the road. Marta's knees were muddy and her breathing was heavy, but her hands moved steadily, pressing the soil firm around the base. Lucia knelt beside her without a word and held the trunk straight while Marta tied it to its stake.

When they finished, they stood together looking down the road at the thin line of saplings stretching into the distance. A few of them still had leaves — gold and rust, catching what was left of the afternoon. Devon walked past on his way home, paused at the dogwood, and tucked the cloth tie a little tighter against the stake. He nodded at Marta. She nodded back.

Lucia drove her mother home. On the passenger seat between them sat the empty wheelbarrow handles, the shovel, and a bag with two more saplings for next Tuesday.
