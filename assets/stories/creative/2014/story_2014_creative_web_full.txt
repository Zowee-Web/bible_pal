Willa lived on a short street with seven houses in a row. Each house had a different colored door. Her own door was painted the color of ripe blueberries. Next door, old Mr. Hagan had a door the color of marigolds. Across the way, the Reyes family had a bright red one. Every morning, Willa woke up before the rest of her family and tiptoed to the kitchen. Warm toast and honey smells drifted from the counter where her papa had left breakfast. She ate quickly, then pulled on her boots and slipped outside.

Behind Willa's house, a shallow creek ran along the edge of a meadow. Smooth pebbles lined the creek bed in all sorts of colors. Some were gray with white stripes. Others were speckled brown or pale pink. Willa had a small canvas bag she carried with her each morning. She would crouch by the water and choose seven pebbles, one for every house on her street.

This had started three months ago, on a cool autumn day. Willa's mama had been sick with a bad cough for two weeks. Mr. Hagan brought soup every evening without being asked. Mrs. Reyes walked Willa to school each morning so her papa could stay home. The Kowalski twins shared their lunch snacks with her every single day. Willa wanted to say thank you, but words felt too small. So she found a smooth creek pebble and set it on Mr. Hagan's doorstep. She placed one on the Reyes family's welcome mat too. And one on the Kowalski porch, right between two clay flower pots.

Now her mama was well again. But Willa kept collecting pebbles every morning. She had added every house on the street to her route. Even the Palmers at the end, who were quiet and kept to themselves. Even young Miss Linden, who had just moved in last month. Willa would walk from door to door in the early light, placing a pebble gently on each step. She never knocked. She never left a note.

One Tuesday, Mr. Hagan was already outside watering his geraniums when Willa came by. He looked down at the pebble she placed near his boot. "Another one," he said, smiling. His voice was rough but kind. "I've got a whole jar of these on my windowsill now." Willa grinned and kept walking. At the Reyes house, little Sofia was pressing her nose against the front window. She waved with both hands. Willa waved back and set a speckled pebble beside the doormat.

By Thursday, something new happened. Willa found a small acorn sitting on her own blueberry-colored doorstep. It was placed right in the center, like someone had thought carefully about where it should go. She picked it up and held it in her palm. It was smooth and cool. She tucked it into her pocket and smiled all the way to school.

The next morning, a tiny pinecone sat on her step. The morning after that, a buttercup pressed flat and dry. Willa started keeping these gifts in a jar on her own windowsill, right next to her hairbrush and her favorite book about birds. She did not know who was leaving them. She did not try to find out.

On Saturday, Willa's mama came outside to help her pick pebbles at the creek. They crouched side by side, their boots sinking a little in the soft mud. "You've kept this up a long time," her mama said. Willa nodded and turned a pale pink stone over in her fingers. "It makes my morning feel good," she said. Her mama picked up a gray pebble with a white ring around it. "Can I leave one too?" she asked. Willa handed her the canvas bag.

Together they walked the street. Willa took the left side. Her mama took the right. They moved quietly from porch to porch. At the Palmers' house, Mr. Palmer opened his door just as Willa's mama bent down. He looked at the pebble, then at her, then at Willa across the street. He gave a slow nod and said, "Thank you. I keep every one."

That evening, Willa sat on her front step counting the gifts in her jar. Three acorns, two pinecones, one dried buttercup, one small white feather, and a tiny wooden bead. Her papa came and sat beside her. He smelled like sawdust and soap. "Busy jar," he said. Willa held up the wooden bead. "Someone carved this," she whispered. Her papa leaned closer to look. "That's nice work," he said.

Down the street, porch lights clicked on one by one. Mr. Hagan's marigold door glowed warm and bright. Sofia's face appeared again in the Reyes window, and she waved goodnight. Willa waved back, then set her jar carefully inside the front door. She left one pebble on her own step, just because it felt right. Her papa put his arm around her shoulder, and they walked inside together.
