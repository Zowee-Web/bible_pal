Rue lived in a small village where the houses sat close together. Each home had a painted door and a little front step. Some doors were blue. Some were red or yellow or soft green. The steps were made of flat stone, worn smooth from years of feet coming and going. Morning dew still clung to the grass when Rue woke up each day.

Her bedroom window looked out over the lane. She could see the rooftops from her pillow if she turned her head just right. A curtain with tiny embroidered flowers hung beside the glass. When the early light came through, it made small bright shapes on her quilt.

She kept a basket by her bed. Inside were smooth pebbles she had gathered from the creek behind her house. Some were gray with white stripes. Others were speckled brown or pale pink. A few were so small they fit on her thumbnail. Every evening, she sat on the edge of her bed and picked her favorites. She turned each one slowly, feeling its weight. Then she placed them in the basket for the next morning. The basket was woven from dried reeds, and it made a soft rustling sound when she set the pebbles inside.

Before breakfast, Rue would slip on her shoes and carry the basket outside. The front door creaked a little when she opened it. Cool air touched her face and arms. She could hear birds waking up in the hedge along the fence. She walked quietly from door to door, her feet making soft sounds on the packed dirt path. At each step, she set down one pebble. She placed it right in the center where someone would see it. She used both hands sometimes, adjusting the stone until it sat just so. Each pebble was her way of saying thank you.

Old Mr. Calloway always waved to her from his garden. His garden had tall tomato plants tied to wooden stakes with strips of cloth. He wore a wide straw hat and brown boots with the laces untied. She left him a round white one. She set it near the edge of his step where his boot would almost touch it when he came back inside.

Mrs. Linden baked honey rolls and shared them on Saturdays. Her door was the yellow one with a brass handle shaped like a leaf. A small clay pot of herbs sat beside her step, and the smell of rosemary drifted up whenever the breeze moved. Rue chose a pink pebble for her. She placed it right next to the herb pot, leaning it gently against the clay.

The twins next door let her play with their wooden train set. Their gate had a latch that clicked when it opened. Rue could see their rain boots lined up on the mat, one pair red and one pair green. She left two speckled stones, side by side. She made sure they were touching, just barely, so they looked like they belonged together.

She did this every morning. Some days the air was warm and still. Other days a light breeze carried the smell of cut grass or someone's laundry drying on a line. Rue never hurried. She liked the quiet of the lane before everyone was fully awake. She liked the way each doorstep looked with a small stone resting on it, like a little gift waiting to be found.

One Tuesday morning, Rue opened her front door and stopped. Her hand stayed on the door handle. A small pile of pebbles sat on her own step. They were different from hers. These were smoother and darker, some with tiny flecks of gold that caught the light. There were seven or eight of them, gathered into a neat little mound right in the center of her step.

She knelt down slowly. She picked one up and turned it over in her hand. It was warm from the early light. The golden flecks sparkled when she tilted it. Another pebble was dark gray, almost black, with a single white ring around its middle. She held it between her thumb and finger and looked at it closely.

She looked down the lane. Mr. Calloway was already watering his tomatoes. Water dripped from the spout of his green watering can and splashed softly on the soil. He glanced up and smiled. His hat tilted a little to one side.

Mrs. Linden stood at her window, holding a cup of tea. Steam curled up from the rim. She gave a little nod. Her fingers tapped the windowsill twice, gently.

The twins peeked out from behind their gate. One held the gate with both hands. The other stood on tiptoe to see over. Both were grinning. One of them wiggled his fingers in a small wave.

Rue held the pebbles close to her chest. She could feel the cool, smooth shapes pressing against her palms. Her throat felt full, the way it does when something good catches you by surprise. She blinked a few times and took a breath.

She sat down on her step. The stone was cool beneath her. She set her basket beside her knee and began arranging the new pebbles in a circle around it. She placed each one carefully, spacing them out evenly. The dark ones and the gold-flecked ones alternated in a ring. When she finished, she sat back and looked at the pattern. Her basket sat in the middle like the center of a flower.

The morning air smelled like wet clover and bread baking somewhere nearby. A robin landed on the fence post across the lane. It tilted its head once, then twice, then sang three bright notes. Rue wiggled her toes inside her shoes and laughed softly. The sound was small and easy, like a bubble popping.

She whispered a quiet thank you. Not to anyone she could see, but to the wide, kind morning itself. The words were so soft they barely made a sound. She folded her hands in her lap for a moment and just sat.

Then she stood up. She brushed off her knees. She tucked one golden-flecked pebble into her pocket and patted it once through the fabric. She picked up her basket and felt its familiar weight. It felt lighter today. Her steps felt easy.

The lane stretched ahead, familiar and good. She could see each painted door in a row. A cat sat on a windowsill three houses down, washing its paw. Somewhere, a screen door opened and closed with a gentle tap.

At the first house past the twins, she placed a smooth gray pebble on the step. She nudged it with her fingertip until it sat straight. At the next house, she chose a small brown one with a white dot on top. She set it beside a pair of muddy garden gloves someone had left out to dry.

She kept walking. Her basket swung gently at her side. She hummed a little tune, just three notes, matching the robin's song from earlier. The hem of her dress brushed against her knees as she walked.

At the last house on the row, she placed a pale blue stone on the step. It was the color of a robin's egg. She patted it once with her palm, pressing it lightly into place. The step was warm beneath her hand.

Then she turned back toward home. The lane looked different going this direction. She could see her own front door, still open a crack. The circle of pebbles glinted on her step. Her basket of new gifts waited there, ordinary and wonderful.

She walked slowly, her pocket heavy with the golden-flecked pebble bumping softly against her leg. A breeze lifted her hair for just a moment. She tucked it behind her ear and kept going. Her front step was close now. She could see the dark and light pebbles arranged in their careful ring.

Rue sat back down beside them. She rested the basket in her lap and ran her fingers over the tops of the new stones. Each one was different. Each one was smooth and real and placed there on purpose, just for her. She pressed her palm flat against the cool step and smiled.

Down the lane, a door opened. Then another. The village was waking up. Somewhere, Mr. Calloway's watering can clinked against a fence post. Somewhere, the twins laughed. And Rue sat on her step, her basket full, her pocket warm, her morning just beginning.
