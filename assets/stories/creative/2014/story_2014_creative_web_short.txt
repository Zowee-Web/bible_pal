Rue lived in a small village where the houses sat close together. Each home had a painted door and a little front step. Morning dew still clung to the grass when Rue woke up each day.

She kept a basket by her bed. Inside were smooth pebbles she had gathered from the creek behind her house. Some were gray with white stripes. Others were speckled brown or pale pink. Every evening, she picked her favorites and placed them in the basket for the next morning.

Before breakfast, Rue would slip on her shoes and carry the basket outside. She walked quietly from door to door. At each step, she set down one pebble. She placed it right in the center where someone would see it. Each pebble was her way of saying thank you.

Old Mr. Calloway always waved to her from his garden. She left him a round white one. Mrs. Linden baked honey rolls and shared them on Saturdays. Rue chose a pink pebble for her. The twins next door let her play with their wooden train set. She left two speckled stones, side by side.

One Tuesday morning, Rue opened her front door and stopped. A small pile of pebbles sat on her own step. They were different from hers — smoother, darker, some with tiny flecks of gold. She picked one up and turned it over in her hand. It was warm from the early light.

She looked down the lane. Mr. Calloway was already watering his tomatoes. He glanced up and smiled. Mrs. Linden stood at her window, holding a cup of tea. She gave a little nod. The twins peeked out from behind their gate, grinning.

Rue held the pebbles close to her chest. Her throat felt full, the way it does when something good catches you by surprise. She sat down on her step and arranged the new pebbles in a circle around her basket.

The morning air smelled like wet clover and bread baking somewhere nearby. A robin landed on the fence post and sang three bright notes. Rue wiggled her toes inside her shoes and laughed softly.

She whispered a quiet thank you — not to anyone she could see, but to the wide, kind morning itself. Then she stood up, tucked one golden-flecked pebble into her pocket, and started her walk again. Her basket felt lighter. Her steps felt easy. The lane stretched ahead, familiar and good.

At the last house on the row, she placed a pale blue stone on the step and patted it once. Then she turned back toward home, where her basket of new pebbles waited in a circle of ordinary, wonderful gifts.
