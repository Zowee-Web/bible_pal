Claudette was standing at the bus stop on Seventh Avenue when the woman with the red scarf fell.

It was a Thursday afternoon, overcast, the kind of day when the city felt like it was holding its breath. Claudette had just come from the dentist and her jaw ached and she wanted to go home.

The woman in the red scarf was crossing the street, carrying two paper grocery bags pressed against her chest. She stepped off the curb and her ankle turned.

Her body pitched forward. The bags flew from her arms. Oranges hit the asphalt and rolled in every direction. A carton of eggs cracked open. A bag of rice split and sprayed white grains across the road.

The woman went down on both knees and one hand. Her scarf fell across her face.

Claudette stood at the bus stop with seven other people. Nobody moved. The man in the gray suit looked at his phone. Two teenagers glanced over and looked away. A woman in a green coat tightened her grip on her purse strap.

Then a kid — eleven, maybe twelve, a skateboard tucked under his arm — dropped the board on the sidewalk and ran into the street.

He didn't check for traffic. He didn't look to see if anyone else was going. He just went.

He knelt beside the woman and started picking up oranges. His hands moved fast, scooping the fruit off the wet road and putting them back into the torn bag. He held the bottom with one hand so nothing else would fall out.

A car came around the corner and honked. The kid didn't flinch. He looked up at the driver and held one hand out — wait — and kept gathering groceries with the other.

The woman pushed her scarf out of her face. She was maybe sixty. Her knee was scraped through her stockings. She looked at the boy and her face changed — not just relief, but something else. The look you give someone who did the thing no one else would do.

He helped her stand. She tested her ankle and winced.

"You okay?" the boy asked.

She nodded. "Thank you, sweetheart."

He handed her the bag of oranges. The rice was gone — scattered across the road. The eggs were finished. But the oranges were saved, and the woman was on her feet.

The kid walked back to the sidewalk, picked up his skateboard, and tucked it under his arm. He didn't look at the eight people who had been standing there watching.

The bus arrived. Everyone got on. Claudette sat by the window.

She was still thinking about it three stops later. Not the fall — falls happen every day. She was thinking about the boy. The way he had dropped his skateboard without hesitation. The way his feet had carried him into the street while every other pair of feet at that bus stop — including hers — had stayed planted on the sidewalk.

Claudette looked at her hands in her lap. They were perfectly capable hands. Hands that could have picked up oranges.

The bus turned onto her street. She pulled the cord and stood up. As she stepped off, she thought: that is what courage looks like. It doesn't announce itself. It drops its skateboard and runs into the street while everyone else stands still.
