Claudette was standing at the bus stop on Seventh Avenue when the woman with the red scarf fell.

It happened fast. One moment the woman was stepping off the curb. The next, her ankle turned and she went down hard — hands out, groceries scattering, oranges rolling into the gutter.

Nobody moved. There were eight people at the bus stop. Claudette counted them. A man in a suit looked at his phone. Two teenagers glanced over and looked away. An older woman tightened her grip on her purse.

Then a kid — eleven, maybe twelve — dropped his skateboard and ran into the street. He knelt beside the woman and started picking up her oranges. He didn't say anything clever. He just gathered the fruit into the torn bag and held it steady while she got to her feet.

A car honked. The kid didn't flinch.

The woman stood, tested her ankle, winced. The boy handed her the bag. "You okay?"

She nodded. "Thank you, sweetheart."

He picked up his skateboard and went back to the sidewalk. The eight people at the bus stop had watched the whole thing. Nobody said anything to the boy. The bus came. Everyone got on.

Claudette sat by the window. She was still thinking about it — not the fall, but the boy. How his body had moved before his mind caught up. How his feet had carried him into the street while everyone else's had stayed on the concrete.

She had stood there like the rest of them. She had watched. She had not moved.

The bus turned the corner. Claudette looked at her own hands in her lap and thought: that is what courage looks like. It does not announce itself. It drops its skateboard and runs.
