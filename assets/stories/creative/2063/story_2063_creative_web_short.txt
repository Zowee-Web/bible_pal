Nora had been carrying the science project since Monday and her arms were giving out.

It was a model of the solar system — nine planets (she included Pluto, and she would fight anyone who said otherwise) made from painted styrofoam balls hung on wire from a wooden frame. The whole thing was three feet wide and wobbled when she walked.

She had stayed up past midnight finishing it. She had painted Jupiter twice because the first one looked like a dirty snowball. She had glued Saturn's rings on four times.

Now it was Friday, presentation day, and she was walking the six blocks from the bus stop to school because her mom had an early shift and couldn't drive her. The frame dug into her fingers. Pluto swung wildly on its wire. Neptune had already fallen off once and she'd caught it with her chin.

At block four, the frame slipped. The whole solar system tilted sideways. Mars popped off its wire and bounced on the sidewalk. Nora scrambled after it, holding the frame with one arm, scooping Mars with her free hand.

She stood there on the sidewalk, breathing hard, the project listing to one side, Mars in her fist, her backpack sliding off one shoulder.

A crossing guard named Mr. Eddie was standing at the corner. He was tall and wore an orange vest. He watched Nora struggle for about three seconds.

Then he walked over. He didn't ask if she needed help. He just took one side of the frame and lifted.

"Which school?" he asked.

"Lincoln. Two more blocks."

They walked together. Mr. Eddie held his side steady. The planets stopped swinging. Nora stuck Mars back on its wire as they walked.

At the school entrance, he handed his side back to her. "Good Jupiter," he said.

"Thanks. I painted it twice."

She carried the project through the front doors. Her arms still ached. But the solar system was in one piece.
