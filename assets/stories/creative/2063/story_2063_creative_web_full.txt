Nora had been carrying the science project since Monday and her arms were ready to quit.

The project was a model of the solar system. Nine planets — she included Pluto because Pluto was a planet when her grandfather learned about space and that was good enough for her — made from styrofoam balls painted by hand and hung on thin wire from a wooden frame. Mercury was the size of a marble. Jupiter was the size of a grapefruit. The whole thing was three feet wide and wobbled every time she took a step.

She had started two weeks ago. Planned it carefully, drawn a diagram, made a supply list. But the painting took longer than expected because styrofoam doesn't hold paint well. She painted Jupiter twice — the first version looked like a dirty snowball. She glued Saturn's rings on four separate times because the glue kept failing.

Thursday night she stayed up past midnight attaching the last wires. Her alarm went off at six-fifteen.

Now it was Friday morning. Presentation day. Nora was walking six blocks from the bus stop to Lincoln Elementary because her mom had an early shift at the hospital and couldn't drive her.

The wooden frame dug into her fingers. Her backpack pulled at her shoulders. Pluto, on the longest wire, swung back and forth like a tiny wrecking ball.

Block two. Her right hand went numb. She shifted the frame to her left.

Block three. Neptune fell off its wire. Nora caught it against her chest with her chin and pressed it back on with her teeth while walking.

Block four. The frame slipped.

The whole solar system tilted sideways. Mercury swung into Venus. Jupiter knocked against Earth. Mars popped off its wire and bounced on the sidewalk — a small red ball rolling toward the gutter. Nora lunged, holding the frame with one arm outstretched, and scooped Mars off the ground before it reached the drain.

She stood on the sidewalk. The project hung at a thirty-degree angle from her left hand. Mars was clenched in her right fist. Her backpack had slid off one shoulder. A strand of hair was stuck to her lip.

Two more blocks.

A crossing guard stood at the corner of Oak and Fifth. Tall, gray-haired, orange vest, holding a stop sign on a pole. His name tag said MR. EDDIE.

He didn't ask if she needed help. He just set his stop sign against the pole, walked over, and took the right side of the frame. He lifted it level.

"Which school?"

"Lincoln. Two blocks."

"Let's go."

They walked together. Mr. Eddie held his side steady. The planets stopped swinging. The frame was level for the first time since the bus stop. Nora stuck Mars back on its wire as they walked.

"Pluto," Mr. Eddie said, nodding at the small blue ball on the far wire. "Good for you."

"It's a planet."

"No argument from me."

They reached Lincoln's front entrance. Mr. Eddie stopped at the bottom of the steps and carefully transferred his side of the frame back to Nora.

She adjusted her grip. The project was heavy but it was whole. Everything still hung where it belonged.

"Good Jupiter," Mr. Eddie said.

"Thanks. I painted it twice."

He nodded once, turned, and walked back toward his corner.

Nora carried the solar system through the front doors. Her arms burned. But all nine planets swung gently on their wires — Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune, and Pluto — and none of them fell.
