The cafeteria was loud. Trays clattered against the metal rails. Chairs scraped across tile. Voices bounced off the walls like rubber balls thrown hard at concrete.

Marco carried his tray with both hands, gripping the edges because the bottom was slick. He stepped carefully between backpacks dumped on the floor and a puddle of spilled juice nobody had cleaned up yet.

He was new. Five days new. His shoes were still clean and his backpack still had the tags inside.

He scanned the room the way you scan a parking lot for your car — hoping something familiar would just appear, knowing it probably wouldn't.

Tables full of kids laughing, elbowing each other, trading chips for cookies. A group near the door comparing shoes, each boy lifting his foot onto a chair. Two girls whispering behind cupped hands, their eyes darting. A table of kids playing some game with folded paper.

Every seat looked taken. Every seat looked like it belonged to someone.

And then there was a table near the window where one boy sat alone.

The boy had dark hair that fell across his forehead. He was eating a sandwich slowly, pulling it apart more than biting it, looking down at the table like it had something interesting written on it. It didn't. Just a scratch shaped like a check mark and someone's old pen doodle of a star.

Marco stood still in the middle of the aisle. A kid bumped past him with a tray. "Watch it," the kid muttered, not mean, just moving.

Marco didn't move.

He knew that look on the boy's face. He'd worn it himself at his old school, the week after his best friend Devon had moved away. Three lunches alone. Three days of staring at food and pretending the table was fascinating. On the fourth day, a kid named Jordan sat down without asking and said, "You like baseball?" and that was enough. That was the whole door opening.

Marco's heart beat harder. His palms were sweating against the tray.

He could see an open chair at the end of a bigger table — the kind of chair where you sit on the edge and nobody really talks to you but at least you're near people. Safe. Invisible. Fine.

He looked at the boy by the window.

His sneakers squeaked on the floor as he walked over.

"Can I sit here?"

The boy looked up. His eyebrows lifted just a little, like a window cracking open one inch.

"Yeah," he said. "If you want."

Marco set his tray down. The chair wobbled when he sat, and he grabbed the table edge to steady himself. His knee banged the underside. He winced.

The boy almost smiled.

"I'm Marco."

"Elijah." The boy pulled at his sandwich crust, tearing off a small piece and setting it on his napkin. "Nobody really sits here."

"I know," Marco said.

He said it plain. Like saying the sky was blue. Not sorry. Not trying to be a hero. Not making a point. Just honest.

He opened his milk carton. It took three tries. The flap was stubborn and kept tearing in the wrong spot. Little shreds of waxed cardboard stuck to his fingertip.

Elijah leaned forward. "You have to pinch both sides at the same time."

Marco pinched.

The carton opened clean.

"Thanks."

They ate. The silence between them wasn't heavy. It was just quiet, the way a room goes quiet after someone turns off a fan — you notice it, and then you stop noticing, and then it's just the room.

Elijah asked where Marco had moved from.

"Two states away," Marco said. "We had a yellow front door. And a mailbox that leaned sideways — my dad hit it backing out once and never fixed it."

"I don't have a yellow door," Elijah said. "But my mailbox is blue."

"That's pretty good," Marco said.

"It's regular blue. Not even bright blue."

"Still counts."

Someone at a nearby table threw a balled-up napkin. It bounced off another kid's head. Laughter erupted. A lunch monitor looked over, hands on her hips.

Marco and Elijah ignored all of it.

Marco peeled his orange. The bright citrus smell rose up between them, cutting through the cafeteria air — through the pizza grease and floor cleaner and whatever that mystery smell was near the trash cans.

"You want half?" Marco asked.

Elijah looked at the orange, then at Marco. "You sure?"

"Yeah."

Marco split it down the middle. Juice ran between his fingers. He handed the bigger half across the table and wiped his thumb on his jeans.

"Thanks," Elijah said.

They ate the orange slices one at a time, pulling them apart slowly. Elijah told Marco he liked drawing robots — not regular robots but robots that were also animals. A robot owl. A robot fox.

"That's cool," Marco said. "I draw maps."

"Of what?"

"Places that don't exist. Like, whole countries with rivers and mountains and cities that have names I make up."

Elijah's eyes widened a little. "Do you have one with you?"

"In my backpack. I can show you after lunch."

"Okay."

Elijah pulled the last bite of sandwich apart and ate both pieces. Marco crunched through a baby carrot that was more rubbery than crunchy. Neither of them seemed to mind.

"Hey, Marco?"

"Yeah?"

"Why'd you sit here?"

Marco thought about it. He thought about Devon moving away. He thought about Jordan sitting down. He thought about the wobbling chair and the stubborn milk carton and the orange that was almost too ripe but still sweet enough.

"I just did," he said.

The bell would ring soon. Trays would get stacked on the conveyor belt. Kids would flood through the double doors and scatter down the hallway like marbles on a wood floor.

But right now, two boys sat across from each other at a table near the window. Orange peels lay in a small pile between their trays. Elijah was drawing something on his napkin with a stubby pencil — a robot with wings, its eyes round and bright. Marco leaned forward to watch.

The sun came through the glass and warmed the spot where their elbows almost touched. Outside, a bird landed on the windowsill, tilted its head, and stayed.
