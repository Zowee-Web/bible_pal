The cafeteria was loud. Trays clattered against the metal rails. Chairs scraped across tile. Voices bounced off the walls like rubber balls thrown hard.

Marco carried his tray with both hands. He stepped carefully between backpacks dumped on the floor. He was new. Five days new. His shoes were still clean.

He scanned the room the way you scan a parking lot for your car — hoping something familiar would just appear.

Tables full of kids laughing, elbowing each other, trading chips for cookies. A group near the door comparing shoes. Two girls whispering behind cupped hands.

And then there was a table near the window where one boy sat alone.

The boy had dark hair that fell across his forehead. He was eating a sandwich slowly, looking down at the table like it had something interesting written on it. It didn't. Just a scratch shaped like a check mark and someone's old pen doodle.

Marco knew that look. He'd worn it at his old school the week after his best friend moved away. Three lunches by himself before a kid named Devon finally sat down.

His heart beat a little harder. His sneakers squeaked on the floor.

He stood at the edge of the table.

"Can I sit here?"

The boy looked up. His eyebrows lifted just a little, like a window cracking open.

"Yeah," he said. "If you want."

Marco set down his tray. The chair wobbled when he sat, and he grabbed the table edge to steady himself. Elijah — that was the boy's name, he'd learn in a second — watched this and almost smiled.

"I'm Marco."

"Elijah." The boy pulled at his sandwich crust. "Nobody really sits here."

"I know," Marco said.

He said it plain, like he was saying the sky was blue. Not sorry. Not trying to be a hero. Just honest.

He opened his milk carton. It took three tries because the flap was stubborn and kept tearing in the wrong spot. Elijah leaned forward.

"You have to pinch both sides at the same time."

Marco pinched. The carton opened clean.

"Thanks."

They ate. The silence between them wasn't heavy. It was just quiet, the way a room is quiet after you turn off a fan — you notice it, and then you don't.

Elijah asked where Marco moved from. Marco told him. Two states away. A house with a yellow front door and a mailbox that leaned sideways.

"I don't have a yellow door," Elijah said. "But my mailbox is blue."

"That's pretty good," Marco said.

Someone at a nearby table threw a balled-up napkin and laughed loud. Marco and Elijah ignored it. Marco peeled his orange, and the bright smell of citrus cut through the cafeteria air — through the pizza grease and floor cleaner.

"You want half?" Marco asked.

Elijah nodded.

Marco split the orange down the middle and handed the bigger half across the table. Juice ran down his thumb and he wiped it on his jeans.

They talked about small things. Elijah said he liked drawing robots. Marco said he liked drawing maps of places that didn't exist. Elijah said that was cool. He meant it.

The bell would ring soon. Trays would get stacked. Kids would flood out through the double doors.

But right now, at the table near the window, two boys sat across from each other chewing orange slices. Their elbows rested on the same sticky surface. The sun came through the glass and landed in a warm square between their trays, and neither of them moved out of it.
