The fluorescent tube above the nurses' station had been flickering since midnight. Dana pressed her palms flat on the counter and watched it pulse. Her feet had stopped hurting around hour ten. Now they were just numb, which was worse.

She pulled her water bottle from beside the keyboard and drank. Lukewarm. Everything on this floor was lukewarm — the coffee, the blankets, the air pushing through ceiling vents that hadn't been serviced since April.

Room 714 buzzed again.

She pushed off the counter and walked. The hallway stretched long and pale under its rows of lights. Her shoes made soft sounds against the tile. She counted doors without reading them. She knew the numbers by feel now, by the particular scuff marks and taped-over nameplates.

Mr. Alderman was sitting up when she came in. Eighty-three years old, post-op day two, a man who called every nurse "sweetheart" regardless of age or preference. His IV line had tangled in the bed rail again.

"Didn't mean to bother you," he said.

"You didn't." She untangled the line with practiced fingers, checked the drip rate, adjusted the clamp. His skin was cool and papery under her glove.

"What time is it?" he asked.

"Three fifteen."

He looked toward the window. The blinds were drawn but a thin line of parking-lot light came through at the bottom.

"My wife used to be up at this hour," he said. "She'd go sit in the kitchen and eat toast with butter. Just one piece. Said it was the only quiet she got all day."

Dana smoothed the blanket over his arm. She didn't say anything.

"You remind me of her a little," he said. "She was steady like you."

Dana looked at him. His eyes were wet and clear in the dim room.

"Thank you," she said.

She turned off his call light and walked back to the station. The fluorescent tube was still flickering. The vending machine hummed at the end of the hall. Her shift had four more hours in it.

She sat down and opened her water bottle. She drank again. It was still lukewarm.

But her hands were steady, and the old man was sleeping, and somewhere in the sentence he'd said about his wife there was a small warm thing she could carry for a while.

She set the bottle down and reached for the next chart.
