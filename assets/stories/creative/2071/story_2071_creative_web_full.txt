The fluorescent tube above the nurses' station had been flickering since midnight. Dana pressed her palms flat on the laminate counter and watched it pulse. Three quick blinks, a pause, three more. Her feet had stopped hurting around hour ten. Now they were just numb, which she knew from experience was worse. The pain would come later, in the car, when she pulled off her compression socks in the hospital parking garage.

She checked the monitor bank. Fourteen rooms on her section. Twelve occupied. Most of the green lines moved in the slow rhythm of sleep. Two were awake — she could tell by the heart rate, the restless bumps in the waveform.

She pulled her water bottle from beside the keyboard and drank. Lukewarm. Everything on the seventh floor was lukewarm. The coffee in the break room, the blankets from the warmer that hadn't held proper temperature in weeks, the air pressing down through ceiling vents that maintenance kept promising to look at.

The phone rang. She picked it up, listened, wrote a number on a sticky note, hung up. Lab results for 709. She entered them into the system, her fingers moving without thought.

Room 714 buzzed.

She pushed off the counter and walked. The hallway stretched long and colorless under its rows of lights. Her shoes made soft sounds against the tile, a rhythm she'd stopped hearing hours ago. She passed the supply closet with its smell of plastic and alcohol. She passed the family waiting area where someone had left a paper cup on the floor.

Mr. Alderman was sitting up when she came in. Eighty-three years old, post-op day two, hip replacement, a man who called every nurse "sweetheart" regardless of their age or feelings about it. His IV line had tangled in the bed rail again. He'd been trying to reach his cup of water on the tray table and twisted himself sideways.

"Didn't mean to bother you," he said.

"You didn't." She untangled the line with practiced fingers, checked the drip rate, adjusted the clamp. She moved the cup closer to his hand. His skin was cool and papery under her glove, thin as old cloth.

"What time is it?" he asked.

"Three fifteen."

He looked toward the window. The blinds were drawn tight but a thin bar of parking-lot light came through at the bottom edge, orange and steady.

"My wife used to be up at this hour," he said. His voice was unhurried, the way voices get in hospital rooms at night, when there's no one else listening. "She'd go down to the kitchen and eat one piece of toast with butter. Just one. Said it was the only quiet she got all day. Four kids, you know. She had to steal her peace in small pieces."

Dana smoothed the blanket over his arm. She adjusted his pillow without being asked.

"Been gone eleven years now," he said. "But I still think about that toast."

He smiled. It was a tired smile, creased deep into a face that had earned every line.

"You remind me of her a little," he said. "She was steady like you. Never rushed. Even when everything was loud and the kids were tearing through the house, she moved like she had all the time there was."

Dana looked at him. His eyes were wet and clear in the half-dark room. The monitor beside his bed beeped its slow green rhythm.

"Thank you," she said. She meant it in a way she couldn't have explained.

She turned off his call light, checked his O2 clip, and walked back to the station. The fluorescent tube was still flickering. The vending machine hummed its low single note at the end of the hall. Her shift had four hours and twelve minutes left in it.

She sat down in the rolling chair that listed slightly to the right. She opened her water bottle and drank. Still lukewarm.

But her hands were steady, and the old man was sleeping, and somewhere in the thing he'd said about his wife — the toast, the quiet kitchen, the stolen peace — there was something small and warm she could carry for a while. Not a lesson. Not a reason. Just a thing someone had loved once, handed to her in the dark like a piece of bread.

She set the bottle down and reached for the next chart. The fluorescent tube blinked three times and held.
