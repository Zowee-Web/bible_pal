The fluorescent tube above the nurses' station had been flickering since midnight. Dana pressed her palms flat on the laminate counter and watched it pulse. Three quick blinks, a pause, three more. It had a pattern now, almost a language, though she was too tired to translate it into anything but annoyance.

She shifted her weight from her left foot to her right. Hour fourteen. Her feet had stopped hurting around hour ten, which she knew from years of night shifts was not a good sign. The pain would come later, in the parking garage, when she sat in the driver's seat and pulled off her compression socks and felt the blood remember what it was supposed to do.

The seventh floor was quiet in the way hospitals are quiet at three in the morning — not actually quiet at all. Monitors beeped in staggered rhythms down the hallway. A ventilator hissed behind a closed door. Somewhere below, an elevator chimed and opened and closed again for no one. The building breathed and ticked around her like a living thing pretending to sleep.

She checked the monitor bank. Fourteen rooms on her section. Twelve occupied. Most of the green lines moved in the slow sawtooth rhythm of sleep. Two patients were awake — she could tell by the heart rate, the restless bumps in the waveform, the occasional spike when someone shifted position or reached for a call button and thought better of it.

She pulled her water bottle from beside the keyboard and drank. Lukewarm. Everything on this floor was lukewarm. The coffee in the break room that Josie had made at eleven and no one had touched since. The blankets from the warmer that hadn't held proper temperature in three weeks, despite two work orders and a handwritten note taped to the maintenance office door. The air pressing through ceiling vents that smelled faintly of dust and recycled breath.

Her phone buzzed in her scrub pocket. A text from her daughter: *you up?* Seventeen years old, awake at three a.m. on a school night. Dana typed back: *Go to sleep.* Then she added a heart emoji because she was tired and it was easier than words.

The desk phone rang. She picked it up, listened, wrote a number on a yellow sticky note, hung up. Lab results for 709. She entered them into the system, her fingers tapping keys without conscious thought. White count trending down. Good. She flagged it for Dr. Pham and moved on.

Room 714 buzzed.

She pushed off the counter and stood. Her left knee popped, a small mechanical sound that had started last year and never gone away. She walked. The hallway stretched long and colorless under its rows of fluorescent panels, each one casting a slightly different shade of white. Her shoes made soft sounds against the tile. She passed the supply closet with its sharp smell of plastic packaging and isopropyl alcohol. She passed the family waiting area where someone had left a paper cup on the floor beside a chair, a crescent of cold coffee still pooled in the bottom.

She passed room 710, where Mrs. Okoro slept with the television on, its blue light seeping under the door. She passed 711, empty and stripped, the mattress bare, the side rails down. Someone had been discharged at noon. Or transferred. She couldn't remember which.

Mr. Alderman was sitting up when she came in. Eighty-three years old, post-op day two, right hip replacement. A retired postal carrier from Garfield Heights who called every nurse "sweetheart" regardless of age, gender, or their particular feelings about the word. His IV line had tangled in the bed rail again. He'd been reaching for his cup of water on the rolling tray table and twisted himself sideways in the effort. The cup was still out of reach.

"Didn't mean to bother you," he said.

"You didn't." She untangled the line with practiced fingers, following the tubing back to the pump, checking the drip rate, adjusting the roller clamp. She moved the tray table closer and positioned the cup within easy reach of his right hand. His skin was cool and papery under her glove. Thin as old cloth, the veins visible beneath like blue ink.

She checked his surgical dressing. Clean and dry. She checked his drain output. Within range. She noted the numbers in her head to chart later.

"What time is it?" he asked.

"Three fifteen."

He turned his face toward the window. The blinds were drawn tight, the plastic slats dusty at the edges, but a thin bar of parking-lot light came through at the bottom — orange and steady, the only warm color in the room.

"My wife used to be up at this hour," he said.

His voice had the unhurried quality that hospital voices get at night, when the hallway is still and there's no one waiting and the usual rules about conversation seem suspended.

"She'd go down to the kitchen — didn't turn on the overhead light, just the one above the stove — and she'd make one piece of toast with butter. Just one piece. White bread. Real butter. She'd eat it standing at the counter and then go back to bed."

Dana straightened his blanket. She adjusted the pillow behind his shoulders without being asked.

"Said it was the only quiet she got all day," he continued. "We had four kids. House was never still. Somebody was always yelling or crying or breaking something. But at three in the morning, it was hers. Just the sound of the toaster and the butter knife on bread."

He was looking at the window still, though there was nothing to see through it.

"Been gone eleven years now. Congestive heart failure. She went on a Tuesday. I remember because it was trash day and I kept thinking somebody needed to bring the cans in."

He turned back to Dana. His eyes were wet and perfectly clear.

"Strange what you remember," he said.

"Yes," she said. It was all she had, and it was enough.

He studied her face for a moment. The monitor beside his bed kept its slow green rhythm. The IV pump clicked once and resumed its quiet hum.

"You remind me of her a little," he said. "She was steady like you. Never rushed. Even when the house was falling apart and three of the four were screaming and the dog had gotten into the garbage again, she moved like she had all the time in the world. Like there was enough of her to go around."

He paused.

"There was, too. There was enough."

Dana looked at him. She didn't move for a moment. The room was dim and still. The thin orange line of light held steady at the bottom of the blinds.

"Thank you, Mr. Alderman," she said.

"Arthur," he said.

"Thank you, Arthur."

She turned off his call light. She checked his O2 clip, verified the reading on the monitor, and pulled the blanket straight one more time. He was already closing his eyes when she turned toward the door.

The hallway received her back. She walked past 710, past the blue television glow. Past the empty room. Past the supply closet. Past the waiting area where the paper cup still sat on the floor. She bent down and picked it up, dropped it in the waste bin by the elevator, and kept walking.

The nurses' station was as she'd left it. The flickering tube. The sticky notes. The monitor bank with its rows of green lines. She sat down in the rolling chair that listed to the right and had for as long as anyone could remember.

She opened her water bottle. Drank. Still lukewarm. She set it down.

Four hours and ten minutes left. Her feet would hurt badly in the car. Her daughter was probably still awake, scrolling through something bright on her phone in the dark of her bedroom. The coffee in the break room was beyond saving.

But her hands were steady. Arthur Alderman was sleeping. And somewhere in what he had said — the kitchen light above the stove, the butter knife on bread, the quiet stolen at three in the morning by a woman who had enough of herself to go around — there was something small and warm that Dana could carry for a while. She didn't name it. She didn't need to. It was just there, the way the orange light was there at the bottom of the blinds. Steady. Present. Asking nothing.

She reached for the next chart. The fluorescent tube above her blinked three times, paused, and held.
