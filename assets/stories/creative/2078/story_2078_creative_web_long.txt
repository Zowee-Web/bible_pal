Archer woke up because the world was too quiet.

No garbage truck grinding at the corner. No school bus brakes squealing on Maple Street. No dog barking three houses down. Not even the furnace humming in the basement. He lay under his quilt and listened to the nothing, and the nothing went on and on like a held breath.

Then he looked at the window.

White light pressed against the glass, softer than any morning he could remember. Not yellow. Not gray. White, like the whole sky had come down close to say something.

He kicked off the covers and ran to the sill in his socks. His feet were cold on the wood floor but he didn't care.

Snow.

Everywhere snow.

It covered the mailbox so only the red flag poked out sideways. It buried the fence posts up to their flat tops. It sat on the trampoline like a giant white pillow, sagging in the middle. Every branch of the oak tree out front held a thick roll of white, and the branches hung low under the weight, almost touching the ground.

The street had disappeared completely. Where the road used to be, where Mr. Lindell parked his blue truck and the Huang twins rode their scooters, there was only a long white field that went all the way to the Garcias' front steps without a single mark in it.

No tire tracks. No footprints. Nothing.

His mom appeared in the hallway, still in her robe, phone in one hand. She looked sleepy and amazed at the same time.

"No school today, Arch."

He was already pulling on his boots.

"Coat," she said. "Hat. Both gloves this time, please."

He grabbed everything from the hook by the door, jammed the hat down over his ears, and tumbled out onto the front porch. The screen door banged behind him.

The cold hit his face like a splash of water. He gasped. Then he breathed in deep through his nose. The air tasted like metal and something clean underneath it, something he didn't have a word for. His breath made clouds that hung in front of him for a second and then dissolved into nothing.

He stepped off the porch.

His boot sank down past his ankle. The snow made a soft crunching sound, like biting into a cold apple. He took another step. Another crunch. He was the first person on his whole block to make a footprint, and he looked back at the two dark holes behind him and felt something warm spread through his chest.

The neighborhood woke up slowly. One house at a time.

The Huang twins came out first, already arguing about whether to build a fort or a snowman. Maya wanted the fort. Jackson wanted the snowman. They stood in their driveway in matching green jackets and yelled at each other for thirty seconds. Then they decided to build both.

Across the street, old Mr. Lindell opened his front door and stood on the porch in a bathrobe and brown slippers. He held a mug of coffee in both hands. Steam rose off it in a thin curl. He didn't say anything. He just looked out at the snow like he was reading a letter from someone he hadn't heard from in a long time.

He saw Archer and lifted his mug.

Archer waved back with a gloved hand.

More doors opened. Kids appeared in puffy jackets and snow pants and mismatched boots. Someone's dad—Archer thought it was Chloe Meyers' dad—dragged a red plastic sled out of the back of a garage and set it in the driveway like a boat at a dock. Three kids were sitting on it within a minute.

A woman Archer didn't recognize walked down what used to be the sidewalk carrying a big silver thermos. She had a stack of paper cups in her other hand.

"Hot chocolate," she said. "Anybody want some?"

Everybody wanted some.

She poured and poured. The chocolate was dark and sweet and a little too hot, and Archer held his cup with both gloves and blew on it and watched the steam twist away.

"Thank you," he said.

"Of course, honey." She poured him a second one.

Nobody had called anybody. Nobody had sent a group text or knocked on a door or made a plan the night before. The snow just came in the dark while they were all sleeping, and the day opened up like a gift nobody expected, and everyone walked out into it together without being asked.

Archer and the Huang twins rolled a snowball across the front yard. It started the size of a grapefruit and picked up snow as it went, leaving a dark green trail of grass behind it. By the time they reached the oak tree it was as tall as Archer's waist and too heavy to push anymore.

They made a second ball, smaller, and Jackson boosted it up on top. Maya found two sticks for arms and a pinecone for a nose. Archer pulled off one of his gloves and pressed five finger-dots into the face for a smile.

The snowman looked lopsided. The pinecone pointed a little to the left.

They didn't fix it. It was fine.

Around noon his mom brought out grilled cheese sandwiches wrapped in aluminum foil. She'd cut them into triangles. He sat on the porch steps and ate, and the cheese was warm and stretchy and the bread was golden and crispy, and the snow kept falling in flakes so big he could see the edges of each one before they landed.

His mom sat beside him with her own sandwich. She didn't say anything for a while. She just chewed and watched the neighborhood.

"This is nice," she said finally.

Archer nodded. His mouth was full.

The afternoon went on like that. Slow and easy. No schedule. No clock ticking toward anything. The Meyers kids pulled the sled up the small hill behind the Garcias' house and slid down it again and again, screaming every time like it was the first time. Mr. Lindell eventually came off his porch in actual boots and an actual coat and helped Jackson Huang pack snow into a wall for the fort. He was slow but his hands were steady and he knew how to stack the blocks so they didn't fall.

Someone brought out a radio and set it on a porch railing. Music drifted across the yards, tinny and cheerful.

Later, when the light started turning gray and gold at the same time, that in-between light that happens in winter when the sun is already low, Archer walked to the middle of the front yard by himself. The snow had been trampled near the street but here, closer to the house, a wide patch was still untouched.

He fell backward into it.

His body made a soft whump sound. Snow puffed up around his shoulders. He spread his arms wide and swept them up and down through the powder. Angel wings. He did the same with his legs. Then he lay still.

The cold pressed against his neck and the backs of his ears. Above him the sky was low and white, close enough to touch, still letting go of flakes that drifted down slow as feathers. They landed on his eyelashes. They dissolved on his warm cheeks. They settled on his jacket without a sound.

He could hear the twins laughing somewhere far off. A screen door closed. A dad called a name he couldn't quite make out. The music on the radio changed to something slower.

He stayed like that for a long time.

Just breathing. Just watching the snow come down from somewhere high and hidden, falling steady and gentle, covering everything the same way—the nice yards and the messy yards, the new roofs and the old ones, the carefully mulched garden beds and the cracked parts of the driveway where weeds grew every summer.

All of it. The same soft white.

When he finally stood up, careful not to smudge the wings, he looked down at the angel in the snow. It was simple and clear, arms wide open, pressed into the ground by the shape of a boy who had nothing to do and nowhere to be and a whole neighborhood that had walked outside together into something none of them made.

The porch light came on behind him. His mom opened the door.

The snow kept falling. The yard stretched out white and wide and open, holding every footprint and snow angel and sled track gently, the way a hand holds something it has been waiting to give.
