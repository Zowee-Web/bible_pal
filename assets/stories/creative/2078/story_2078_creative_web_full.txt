Archer woke up because the world was too quiet.

No garbage truck grinding at the corner. No school bus brakes squealing on Maple. No dog barking three houses down. He lay under his quilt and listened to the nothing, and the nothing went on and on.

Then he looked at the window.

White light pressed against the glass, softer than any morning he could remember. He kicked off the covers and ran to the sill in his socks, his feet cold on the wood floor.

Snow. Everywhere snow.

It covered the mailbox and the fence posts and the trampoline. It sat in fat rolls along every branch of the oak tree out front. The street had disappeared completely. Where the road used to be there was only a long white field stretching all the way to the Garcias' house.

His mom appeared in the hallway, still in her robe, phone in hand. "No school today, Arch."

He was already pulling on his boots.

"Coat," she said. "Hat. Both gloves this time."

He grabbed everything from the hook by the door and tumbled out onto the porch. The cold hit his face and he gasped. The air tasted like metal and something clean underneath it, something he didn't have a word for. His breath made clouds that hung in front of him and dissolved.

The neighborhood was waking up slowly, one house at a time.

The Huang twins came out first, already arguing about whether to build a fort or a snowman. They chose both. Across the street, old Mr. Lindell stood on his porch in a bathrobe and slippers, holding a mug of coffee in both hands, just looking at it all. Snow sat on the railing beside him like frosting on a cake.

He saw Archer and lifted his mug.

Archer waved back with a gloved hand.

More doors opened. Kids appeared in puffy jackets and mismatched boots. Someone's dad dragged a red plastic sled from the back of a garage. A woman Archer didn't know carried a thermos of hot chocolate down the sidewalk—or where the sidewalk had been—and poured small cups for anyone who wandered close enough.

"Thank you," Archer said, and she smiled and poured him a second one.

Nobody had called anybody. Nobody had sent a message or made a plan. The snow just came, and the day opened up, and everyone walked out into it together.

Archer and the Huang twins rolled a snowball across the yard until it was as tall as Archer's waist. They stacked a second one on top and gave it stick arms and a pinecone nose. It looked a little lopsided. They didn't fix it.

Around noon his mom brought out grilled cheese sandwiches wrapped in foil. He ate sitting on the porch steps. The cheese was warm and the bread was crispy and the snow kept falling in flakes so big he could see their edges.

Later, when the light started going gray and gold at the same time, Archer lay down flat on his back in the front yard. He spread his arms and swept them up and down through the snow. Angel wings.

The cold pressed against his neck and ears. Above him the sky was low and white and still letting go. Flakes drifted down slow as feathers, landing on his eyelashes, dissolving on his cheeks.

He could hear the twins laughing somewhere far off. A screen door closed. A dad called someone's name.

He stayed like that for a long time. Just breathing. Just watching the snow fall from somewhere high and hidden, coming down steady and soft, covering everything equally—the nice yards and the messy yards, the new roofs and the old ones, the garden beds and the cracked parts of the driveway.

The whole evening stretched ahead of him, unhurried, open and unmarked, like the wide white yard that held him.
