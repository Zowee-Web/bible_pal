Marcus noticed the new girl at recess. She sat on the brick wall near the basketball court, watching everyone play. Her lunch bag sat unopened on her lap. Her sneakers didn't touch the ground.

Nobody had talked to her all day. Marcus had watched from across the classroom as she sat quietly through reading time and math and the long stretch of social studies. She hadn't raised her hand once.

Marcus knew what that felt like. Last year, he had been the new kid. He remembered how the cafeteria sounded too loud and the hallways all looked the same and everyone seemed to have friends already, like friendship was a bus that had left without him.

He dribbled his basketball twice, thinking. His friends were already picking teams. Ricky called his name. "Marcus, come on!"

He could join them. He almost did. His feet pointed toward the court.

Instead, he tucked the ball under his arm and walked over to the wall.

"Hey," he said. "I'm Marcus."

The girl looked up. She had dark braids and serious brown eyes. "I'm Juliet." Her voice was small, like she was testing whether it worked here.

"Did you just move here?"

She nodded. "From far away. Everything is different. Even the trees look different."

Marcus sat beside her on the wall. The bricks were warm from the afternoon sun. "Yeah," he said. "I remember that feeling. Like you're on a different planet."

"Exactly." She looked at him with surprise, like she hadn't expected anyone to understand.

"It gets better, though," he said.

"When?"

He thought about it honestly. "A couple weeks, maybe. But some parts get better faster. By Friday, the hallways won't feel so confusing."

She looked at the basketball court. "I'm not very good at sports."

"That's okay. Neither is Danny, and he plays every day." He pointed at a short boy in a green shirt who tripped over his own feet and laughed about it like falling was the funniest thing in the world.

Juliet almost smiled.

"You want to know a secret?" Marcus said. "The first week is the hardest. After that, you start knowing where things are. You find the good water fountain — it's by the gym. You figure out which lunch lady gives extra crackers."

"Extra crackers?" Now she did smile. A real one.

"Mrs. Benitez. Third one from the left. Just say please, and she'll give you two extra packets."

Juliet laughed quietly. She opened her lunch bag and pulled out an apple. The crunch sounded cheerful.

"What's your teacher like?" she asked.

"Mr. Okafor? He tells terrible jokes on Fridays. And he lets you draw during silent reading if you've finished your book."

"I like drawing," she said.

"Then you'll like Fridays."

The bell rang. They slid off the wall together.

"Marcus?" Juliet said as they walked toward the doors.

"Yeah?"

"Thanks for sitting with me."

He shrugged like it was nothing. But it wasn't nothing. He knew that. A year ago, a kid named Andre had done the same thing for him — walked over when nobody else did. That conversation had changed everything.

He held the door open, and Juliet walked through. Her steps looked a little lighter than before.
