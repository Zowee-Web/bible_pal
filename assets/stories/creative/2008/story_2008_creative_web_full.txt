Every morning at five-thirty, before the taxi horns started their arguments and the subway grates began exhaling warm air onto the sidewalks, Oren climbed the fire escape to the roof of his building on Delancey Street. He carried a watering can in one hand and a thermos of black coffee in the other. His knees complained on every rung, and he answered them by going slower, not by stopping. He'd been making this climb for eleven years, ever since his wife Bea passed and the apartment below became too quiet for a man who wasn't ready for quiet.

The roof was not beautiful by any standard a magazine would recognize. Oren had dragged up mismatched planters — old paint buckets, a cracked bathtub he'd found on the curb, wooden crates lined with plastic sheeting. Soil came from a garden center in Queens, hauled in bags on the B train during off-peak hours, earning him looks from other passengers. Tomatoes grew beside marigolds. A fig tree, impossibly, survived its third winter in a half-barrel wrapped with burlap. Runner beans spiraled up a trellis he'd built from an old bedframe.

The neighborhood kids called him Roof Man. Some of them had never seen a tomato on a vine before. Oren let them come up on Saturday mornings if they carried something — a bag of compost, a jug of water, their own patience. A girl named Yara, maybe ten, asked him once why he bothered growing things where nothing was supposed to grow.

He handed her a cherry tomato, still warm from the morning. "Taste that and tell me it wasn't supposed to grow."

She bit into it and juice ran down her chin, and she didn't ask again.

Below, the city did what it always did. Sirens threaded through traffic noise. A man on the corner sold counterfeit handbags and shouted his prices like a carnival barker. Garbage trucks groaned through their routes at dawn. The building itself shook slightly when the express train passed underground. None of this reached the garden in a way that mattered. The tomatoes didn't care about the noise. The fig tree had no opinion about rent prices.

Oren's neighbor, a young man named Deshi who worked overnight shifts at a hospital, started appearing on the roof in the afternoons. He never said much at first. He'd sit on the overturned bucket near the marigolds and close his eyes. After a week of this, Oren set a pair of pruning shears next to him without a word. Deshi picked them up the next day and started deadheading the spent blooms.

They worked side by side through the rest of that summer. Deshi learned to pinch suckers off the tomato plants. Oren learned that Deshi had come from Dhaka alone at nineteen and hadn't spoken to his father in four years. This came out in fragments, between tasks — while tying up a drooping pepper plant, while mixing bone meal into a pot.

One evening in September, Deshi brought his phone up to the roof and sat near the fig tree for a long time. Oren was across the roof, repotting herbs, and he could hear Deshi's voice speaking in Bengali — halting at first, then faster, then breaking into something that sounded like laughter mixed with crying. The call lasted forty minutes.

Afterward, Deshi walked over and helped Oren finish the repotting. His eyes were red and his hands were steady. He tucked the basil plants into their new containers with the careful pressure Oren had taught him — firm enough to anchor, loose enough to let the roots breathe.

The next morning, Oren found a bag of soil from Queens leaning against the fire escape. No note. He carried it up one handful at a time, filling the cracked bathtub where the spinach would go for fall. The city below was already loud, already rushing. Up on the roof, a fig was ripening on a branch that had no business surviving this far north, and Oren pressed his thumb against it gently, testing. Not quite ready. He'd check again tomorrow.
