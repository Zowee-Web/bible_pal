The building stood on the corner of Halstead and Ninth, twelve stories of brown brick wedged between a parking garage and a cell phone store. Traffic poured through the intersection from early morning until well past midnight, a constant wash of engines and horns and the hiss of tires on wet asphalt. Street vendors set up on the sidewalk below, their carts sending up the smell of roasting nuts and fried dough that drifted upward through the gaps between buildings. Pigeons clustered on the fire escapes, cooing and shuffling, dropping feathers into the alley. On the roof, behind a low concrete parapet stained with decades of weather, an old man named Oren knelt in the dirt and pressed a tomato seedling into a clay pot.

He had arranged the rooftop in rows. Wooden pallets laid flat served as walkways between raised beds he'd built from scrap lumber and lined with landscaping fabric. Along the south-facing wall, he'd mounted a series of old rain gutters, each one filled with soil and trailing herbs — oregano, thyme, a sprawling mint that threatened to take over everything it touched. A blue tarp rigged to PVC pipes provided shade for the lettuce, which wilted in direct afternoon heat. The wind up here was different from the wind on the street; it came in long, steady pulls that smelled faintly of the river three miles east, and it dried the sweat on Oren's neck before he even noticed it forming.

Oren was seventy-four and had lived in apartment 4B for thirty-one years. His wife, Nadia, had died six years ago in the spring, right when the dogwoods along the hospital parking lot were blooming. After that, the apartment felt like a place that belonged to someone else — her reading glasses still on the nightstand, her coat still hanging by the door, the particular silence that filled a room when the person who used to hum in it was gone. He'd stopped cooking for a while, stopped opening the curtains. Neighbors left food outside his door, and he ate it standing at the kitchen counter, not tasting much.

The garden started almost by accident. He'd gone up to the roof one August evening because the apartment was too hot and the window unit had finally quit. Up there, he found a few cracked planters left behind by a previous tenant, bone-dry soil spilling out of them, one dead geranium stalk poking up like a brown finger. He watered the soil from a jug he'd carried up, not expecting anything, just wanting something to do with his hands. Two weeks later, a weed pushed through — a dandelion, bright and defiant. He let it grow. Then he brought up a packet of basil seeds from the dollar store. That was five years ago.

Now the rooftop held more than forty varieties of plants. Cherry tomatoes hung in clusters from cages he'd welded together at the community workshop on Elm Street. Pole beans climbed a lattice of old curtain rods wired to the parapet. Marigolds bordered every bed because Oren had read they kept certain pests away, and their orange heads bobbed in the wind like small, cheerful crowds. He grew peppers — sweet bells, jalapeños, and a Carolina Reaper he kept in its own pot and warned everyone about. Strawberries trailed from hanging baskets near the stairwell door, and on good days in June, he could pick a handful warm from the afternoon and eat them right there, leaning against the wall, juice on his fingers.

The city never stopped making noise below him. Ambulance sirens cut through at odd hours. The parking garage next door had an alarm that tripped at least once a week, a flat, blaring tone that echoed off the surrounding buildings. On Friday nights, the bar two blocks over let out around two in the morning, and voices carried — laughter, arguments, someone singing badly. Oren heard all of it from the roof. But up among his plants, the sounds arrived softer, rounded by distance, mixed with the rustle of leaves and the ticking of water dripping from his irrigation tubes into the soil. He didn't mind the noise. It reminded him the world was still going.

People in the building started to notice. Mrs. Lam from the sixth floor came up one day looking for her cat and stayed for an hour, asking about the cucumbers. Marcus, the teenager in 11A who wore headphones everywhere and rarely spoke, began appearing on Saturday mornings without explanation. He'd just show up, pull weeds for a while, and leave. A young couple on the third floor, both working double shifts at the hospital, asked if they could have some of the basil. Oren gave them basil, rosemary, a bag of cherry tomatoes, and a jar of the jalapeño hot sauce he'd started making in his kitchen. They brought him back a loaf of banana bread wrapped in foil, still warm.

One October afternoon, the building manager, a harried woman named Claudette, climbed the stairs and told Oren the roof wasn't zoned for this. She had a clipboard and a tight expression. She pointed out drainage concerns, liability issues, the weight of the soil on the structure. Oren listened. He didn't argue. He offered her a chair — a folding lawn chair he kept by the parsley — and poured her a cup of the mint tea he brewed in a thermos every morning. Claudette sat down, took a sip, and looked out over the rows of green while traffic honked twelve stories below. She stayed for forty-five minutes, and when she left, she said she'd look into what permits might make it official.

Winter was the hardest part. Oren moved what he could into the stairwell and covered the rest with row fabric and old bedsheets. Some plants died anyway — the cold was merciless, and a January ice storm coated everything in a shell that cracked stems and split pots. He lost his fig tree, a small one he'd been coaxing along for two years in a whiskey barrel. The morning he found it dead, bark split open to pale wood, he sat on an overturned bucket and pressed his palms against his knees. The rooftop was gray and bare, the beds just mounds under frost-stiffened cloth. Wind cut through his jacket.

But he came back the next day. He cleared the broken pots, swept the ice from the walkways, and checked each covered bed with his hands, feeling the soil temperature through his gloves. Under the row fabric, the garlic he'd planted in November was still alive, green shoots curled tight against the cold but intact. The rosemary by the south wall, woody and stubborn, had survived again. Oren trimmed the dead branches from everything else and made a list of what he'd need to replant in March. He wrote it on the back of an electric bill envelope, his handwriting cramped and careful. The list was long, and filling it out took the better part of an hour.

Spring came back the way it always did — slowly, then all at once. A warm week in late March softened the soil enough to work, and Oren was up there every day, turning compost into the beds with a hand fork, his knees aching on the wooden pallets. Marcus showed up again, taller now, still quiet, and started hauling bags of potting mix up the stairs without being asked. Mrs. Lam brought a cutting from her sister's rose bush, wrapped in a damp paper towel. The young couple from the third floor had a baby now, and they carried her up in a sling one Sunday morning so she could feel the breeze and grab at the mint leaves with her small, reaching hands.

Oren stood at the parapet one evening in early May, a watering can in his hand, and looked down at the intersection of Halstead and Ninth. A bus idled at the light, exhaust shimmering. Someone honked. A man on the corner sold pretzels from a cart, steam rising from the warmer. Twelve stories up, the tomato cages were already filling in with new growth, and the first tiny yellow flowers had appeared on the pepper plants. Oren set the watering can down, wiped his hands on his jeans, and bent to tie a loose bean vine back to its support. The vine curled around the rod on its own as he held it, gripping tight, already reaching for the next rung.
