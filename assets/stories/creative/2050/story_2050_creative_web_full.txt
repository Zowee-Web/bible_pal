The highway stretched through low green hills and Elliot had been driving for two hours without turning on the radio.

He hadn't planned the silence. At the gas station outside Medford he'd filled the tank and bought a coffee and gotten back in the rental car and simply hadn't reached for the dial. By the time he noticed, the quiet had settled into the car like a passenger, and he didn't want to disturb it.

He was driving from his mother's funeral to his apartment in Portland. The service had been that morning in the church where she'd played organ for thirty-one years. The pews were full. The minister said good things. Afterward there were sandwiches in the basement — egg salad and ham on white bread, cut into triangles by women who had been doing this for decades and knew exactly how.

Elliot had shaken hands. He had accepted hugs. He had heard the same sentences — she's in a better place, she lived a beautiful life — and he had nodded and said thank you and meant it, even though the words passed through him like light through glass.

He hadn't cried. Not at the service, not at the reception, not when he pulled away from the church parking lot. He kept waiting for it — a pressure behind his eyes, a crack in the wall — but nothing came.

The highway climbed over a ridge and dropped into a wide valley. Fields of grass stretched to the horizon. A creek ran parallel to the road, visible through gaps in old wooden fencing. A few cows stood in a pasture, all facing the same direction, doing absolutely nothing.

The sky was enormous. Not dramatic — just a plain gray-blue dome that stretched from tree line to tree line. The kind of sky that makes you aware of how much space there is above you and how little you fill.

Elliot's foot eased off the gas. Not a decision — just something his body did. The speedometer dropped from seventy to fifty-five. A semi passed him on the left. He didn't care.

A red-tailed hawk sat on a fence post, perfectly still, surveying the valley like it belonged there more than anything else.

The creek caught the late-afternoon light and threw it back in small bright flashes. A barn stood in the distance, red paint faded to the color of rust.

He drove like that for twenty miles. The valley didn't change. The road didn't curve. And gradually, without any dramatic shift, the tight thing behind Elliot's sternum — the knot he'd been carrying all day, maybe longer — began to loosen.

Not like a dam breaking. More like a fist uncurling one finger at a time.

He still hadn't cried. But somewhere on that stretch of empty highway, with the hawk on the fence post and the cows facing nowhere and the creek throwing light, something in Elliot shifted. A door he hadn't known was locked turned quietly on its hinges.

He drove on. The valley ended. The hills closed in again. But the silence in the car felt different now — less like absence and more like company.
