Gideon saw it happen at recess. Three kids from the older class stood in a half circle around Felix, who was new and small and wore glasses that kept sliding down his nose.

"You can't play here," the tallest one said. "This is our spot."

Felix hugged his basketball to his chest. He didn't say anything. He just looked at the ground.

Gideon was sitting on the bench by the water fountain, eating an apple. He wasn't friends with Felix. He didn't really know him — Felix had only been at the school for two weeks, and he was quiet, the kind of kid who ate lunch with a book open.

But something in Gideon's chest felt tight and hot, like a fist squeezing.

He looked around. Mrs. Patterson was across the playground helping a first-grader. She couldn't see what was happening. One of the older kids had grabbed Felix's basketball and was holding it above his head.

Gideon stood up. His legs felt wobbly, the way they did before he had to read aloud in class. The older kids were taller than him. One was in fifth grade.

He walked over anyway.

"Hey, Felix," he said, and his voice came out quieter than he wanted. He cleared his throat. "Want to come play with us? We need one more for our game."

There was no game. Gideon had been sitting alone eating an apple. But he said it like it was true.

The three older kids looked at Gideon. The tallest one still held the basketball high. Gideon looked up at him. His heart was hammering so hard he could feel it in his ears.

Nobody spoke for a long moment.

Then the tallest one lowered the ball. Something shifted in his face — maybe just the recognition that someone was watching. "Whatever," he said. He tossed the ball at Felix, not hard. They walked away toward the swings.

Felix pushed his glasses up. "You don't really have a game, do you?"

"No," Gideon admitted.

Felix almost smiled. "We could start one."

They walked to the empty court together. Gideon's hands were still shaking, so he stuffed them in his pockets. Felix dribbled the ball and passed it to him. Gideon shot and missed the basket entirely.

Felix laughed — a real laugh. "Nice try."

"Terrible try," Gideon said, and then he was laughing too.

Two more kids wandered over and asked to play. Then they were four, enough for two-on-two, and the game became real.

By the time the bell rang, Gideon's heart had stopped hammering. His legs felt solid again. Felix walked beside him toward the door.

"Thanks," Felix said quietly.

Gideon shrugged, but something warm settled in his chest right where the tight fist had been. "See you tomorrow?"

"Yeah. See you tomorrow."
