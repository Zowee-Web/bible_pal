The bench sat at the corner of Elm and Harwick, right where the sidewalk cracked and a maple tree pushed its roots through the concrete. Naldo had built it eleven years ago from leftover cedar planks after finishing a kitchen renovation for the Greenfield family. Nobody asked him to build it. He just noticed that the bus stop had no shade and no seat, and the elderly woman who waited there every Tuesday afternoon kept leaning against the telephone pole with her hand pressed to her lower back.

She moved away that same winter, but the bench stayed.

Every spring, Naldo walked down from his shop on the hill to check the wood. Cedar held up well, but nothing lasted forever against rain and ice and the slow grinding of seasons. He brought sandpaper, wood filler, sometimes a whole new slat if one had split beyond saving. His wife, Delia, stopped asking why years ago. His apprentice, a young man named Corso, asked every single time.

"Nobody sits on it, Naldo. I've driven past there a hundred times."

Naldo would shrug and keep loading his truck with supplies. "Doesn't mean nobody will."

Corso pressed him once over coffee. "You could build something people actually want. A pergola for the church. A bookshelf for the library. Something that gets used."

"I build those things too," Naldo said. He stirred his coffee slowly. "But I keep that bench right."

The truth was, Naldo had his own reasons, ones he didn't talk about. When he was twenty-three, broke and sleeping in his car after his father threw him out, he'd found a bench outside a laundromat in a town he couldn't even remember the name of. He sat there for four hours in the cold. Nobody came. Nothing changed. But the bench held him, and that was enough for one night — to be held by something that wasn't going anywhere.

This past October, Naldo replaced two slats and re-sealed the entire surface. His knees ached the whole time. A woman walking her dog glanced at him and kept moving. A teenager on a skateboard kicked-flipped over the curb nearby without looking twice. Naldo finished, wiped his hands on his jeans, and drove home.

Three weeks later, Corso called him on a Tuesday evening. His voice sounded different — stripped down, quieter than usual.

"Can I come by?"

Naldo said yes. When Corso arrived, his eyes were swollen and his jacket was buttoned wrong. He stood in the doorway of the shop and said that his girlfriend had left, that his brother wasn't speaking to him, that he'd been sitting in his truck for an hour trying to figure out who to call.

Naldo pulled out a chair and set a glass of water on the workbench. They sat together for a long time without talking. Sawdust floated in the light from the single overhead bulb. Corso picked up a scrap of cedar and turned it over in his hands.

"I drove past the bench on the way here," Corso said quietly. "There was a woman sitting on it. Older lady. She had a grocery bag and she was just... sitting there. Resting."

Naldo nodded.

"I almost stopped," Corso said. His voice cracked. "I almost stopped and sat down next to her."

"You can," Naldo said. "Anytime."

Corso pressed his palms against his eyes. Naldo didn't reach over or say anything wise. He just stayed in his chair, steady as a joint he'd cut clean, and let the quiet do its work. After a while, Corso dropped his hands and picked up the cedar scrap again, breathing in the smell of it.

"Will you show me how to seal wood properly?" Corso asked. "For the bench. In the spring."

Naldo got up and pulled a can of sealant off the shelf. He set it between them on the workbench, next to the glass of water, and sat back down.
