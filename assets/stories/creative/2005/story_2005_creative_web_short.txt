The bench sat at the edge of the park where the sidewalk cracked and the grass grew thin. Nobody walked that far. The playground was on the other side, and the food trucks parked near the fountain. But every spring, Odile sanded the bench down and sealed it fresh. Every autumn, she replaced whatever slats the weather had split.

She was seventy-one and had been a finish carpenter for forty-six years. Her shop was three blocks away. She'd built the bench herself when the city wouldn't fund one for that corner, carried the lumber over in her truck, set the posts in concrete on a Saturday morning. That was fourteen years ago.

Her neighbor Terrence asked her once why she bothered. Nobody sits there, he said. She shrugged and kept working the sandpaper along the grain.

Some years the bench took real damage. A truck backed into it once and cracked the backrest clean through. Ice heaved one of the posts out of true. Each time, Odile came with her tools and her coffee thermos and put it right. She never rushed. She liked the feel of wood under her hands, the slow reveal of smooth grain beneath roughness.

On a Tuesday in October, she drove past with a load of cabinet doors and saw a woman sitting on the bench. The woman's coat was too thin for the weather. Her shoes were wet. She was holding a phone but not looking at it — just gripping it, staring at the cracked sidewalk. Her face was blotched and swollen the way faces get after a long time of crying.

Odile parked the truck. She didn't walk over right away. She poured a second cup of coffee from her thermos, then crossed the grass and sat down on the other end of the bench.

She held out the cup.

The woman looked at her. Odile didn't say anything. The bench was solid beneath them, warm where the morning had baked into the wood.

After a while, the woman took the coffee. She wrapped both hands around it and pressed it against her chest.

They sat like that for a long time, saying nothing, while the wind pushed dry leaves along the path.
