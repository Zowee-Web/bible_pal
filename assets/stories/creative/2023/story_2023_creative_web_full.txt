Harriet pulled the car to the shoulder and turned off the engine.

She couldn't drive another mile. Her eyes burned. Her hands on the steering wheel had gone numb twenty minutes ago, and she'd been gripping it anyway — not because it helped, but because letting go felt like giving up.

The highway stretched into the Blue Ridge dusk. Mountains folded into mountains, each ridge a shade darker than the last, like pages in a book left open at twilight. The sky above them was turning the color of a bruise that's begun to heal — purple shading into gold shading into the deep, clean blue of a day that's finished its work.

She'd been driving since dawn. Twelve hours. A funeral in Roanoke, her sister's tight smile across the kitchen table, the paperwork nobody warns you about when a parent dies. Insurance forms. The deed to a house that still smelled like her father's aftershave.

She'd planned to make it home to Charlotte in one push. Gas station coffee and determination.

But the engine in her chest quit before the one under the hood.

She sat in the silence. The cooling car ticked. Through the open window, mountain air came in — cool, pine-scented, carrying the sound of a creek somewhere below the road and the steady chorus of insects tuning up for evening.

She got out. Her legs were stiff, her back a tight fist of pain. She walked to a gap in the guardrail where a gravel pullout offered a view of the valley.

The valley was filling with mist. It pooled in the low places like milk poured into a bowl, slow and deliberate, turning the dark ridgelines into islands. The last light touched the tops of the trees on the highest ridge — just the tips, a thin line of gold against darkening blue, as if someone had drawn a bright edge along the world to remind it where it ended.

Harriet leaned against the guardrail. Her shoulders dropped. Not because she decided to relax, but because her body simply couldn't hold them up anymore. It was surrender, not choice, and there was a strange relief in it.

The mist kept rising. The creek ran below the road, invisible but certain. She stood for ten minutes. Maybe fifteen. The mountains held their ground — enormous, unhurried, patient in a way that had nothing to do with waiting and everything to do with simply being.

The first star appeared, low in the east. Then a second, higher. Then a scatter of them, emerging as the blue deepened, as if they'd been there all along behind the daylight, waiting for the sky to quiet down.

The air grew cooler. A bat flickered past, silent and sure.

She wasn't rested. The paperwork would still be there tomorrow. Her sister would still need a phone call she didn't have the energy to make.

But something had loosened. Not fixed — loosened. The way a knot loosens not because someone unties it but because the rope has finally stopped being pulled tight from both ends.

She got back in the car. Tilted the seat back, pulled her jacket over her chest, and closed her eyes.

She slept. And the mountains kept watch. And the mist filled the valley like a promise that the morning would come, and when it did, the road would still be there, ready to carry her home.
