Harriet pulled the car to the shoulder and turned off the engine.

She couldn't drive another mile. Her eyes burned. Her hands had gone numb on the steering wheel twenty minutes ago, and she'd been gripping it anyway, because letting go felt like giving up.

The highway stretched into the Blue Ridge dusk — mountains folding into mountains, each ridge darker than the last. The sky was turning the color of a bruise that's begun to heal. Purple shading into gold shading into deep blue.

She'd been driving since dawn. Twelve hours. A funeral in Roanoke, her sister's tight smile, the paperwork nobody warns you about when someone dies. She'd planned to make it home in one push.

But the engine in her chest quit before the one under the hood.

She got out. Her legs were stiff. She walked to a gap in the guardrail where a gravel pullout offered a view of the valley.

The valley was filling with mist. It pooled in the low places like milk in a bowl, slow and quiet, turning ridgelines into islands. The last light touched the tops of the tallest trees — just the tips, a line of gold against darkening blue.

Her shoulders dropped. Not because she decided to relax, but because her body couldn't hold them up anymore.

The mist kept rising. The creek ran below the road, invisible but steady. She stood for ten minutes, maybe fifteen. The mountains held their ground, enormous and patient, indifferent to her schedule and her grief.

The first star appeared. Then a second. The air grew cooler.

She wasn't rested. She wasn't healed. But something had loosened — the way a knot loosens not because someone unties it but because the rope has finally stopped being pulled tight.

She got back in the car, tilted the seat back, and closed her eyes.

She slept. And the mountains kept watch.
