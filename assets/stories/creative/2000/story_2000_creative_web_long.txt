Maren Alcott had lived on Fenwick Island for eleven years, tending the lighthouse that stood on its northern point like a white finger raised against the Atlantic. The island was barely two miles across, ringed by black rocks that the sea had carved into jagged teeth over centuries. Wild rosemary grew in the crevices between granite slabs, and the wind carried its scent up to the lighthouse door most mornings. Gulls nested in the cliffs below, and their cries wove through every hour of the day like a second kind of weather. The nearest town, Harrow's Landing, sat four miles across the channel, its rooftops visible on clear days as a smudge of brown and rust above the waterline. Maren could go weeks without seeing another person, and she had grown comfortable with that arithmetic.

The lighthouse itself was a hundred and twelve years old, its walls thick enough to muffle even the worst storms. Inside, a spiral iron staircase wound up through four levels to the lantern room, where the great Fresnel lens turned on its pedestal, splitting a single flame into a thousand directed beams. Maren kept the brass fittings polished until they threw back her reflection in amber. The smell of kerosene and metal polish had become so familiar that she stopped noticing it years ago, the way a baker stops smelling flour. Every evening at dusk, she climbed those seventy-eight steps, lit the wick, and watched the lens begin its slow rotation, painting the dark water with a sweeping arm of white. She took pride in the fact that no ship had wrecked on Fenwick's rocks during her tenure.

Her routine was precise and solitary. She woke before dawn, checked the fuel reserves, inspected the lens for cracks or condensation, and recorded the weather in a leather-bound logbook. Breakfast was black tea and oatmeal with dried cranberries, eaten standing at the kitchen window while she watched the tide pull back from the rocks. Afternoons she spent mending equipment, reading old maritime journals, or walking the island's perimeter trail, counting the nesting pairs of puffins that returned each spring. She spoke aloud sometimes, narrating her tasks to no one, just to keep her voice from going rusty. The isolation suited her, or at least she had convinced herself it did.

What Maren didn't talk about, even to herself, was the slow accumulation of quiet that had settled into her bones. She had come to Fenwick after her sister Cora's death — Cora, who had been loud and generous and who threw dinner parties for fifteen people in a kitchen meant for four. Grief had driven Maren to the edge of the continent, and the lighthouse job had been available, and the distance from everything felt medicinal. Over the years, the grief had faded to a dull ache, but the distance had become structural, load-bearing. She wasn't sure she could remove it without something collapsing. Her logbook entries had grown shorter each year, as if even words were a kind of company she was rationing.

The change began in October, when a fishing boat called the *Pelican Rose* ran aground on the southern shoals during an unexpected fog. Maren heard the distress horn at two in the morning and launched her skiff into water so thick with mist she navigated by sound alone. She found three fishermen clinging to their listing vessel, soaked and shaking. The youngest, a boy of maybe seventeen named Oren, had a gash across his forearm that was bleeding freely into the salt water. Maren pulled them aboard one at a time, wrapped Oren's arm with strips torn from her spare shirt, and ferried them back to the lighthouse. She built a fire in the iron stove and heated soup from a can while they sat on her floor, dripping and dazed.

They stayed three days while the fog held. Maren had not hosted anyone in her home for years, and the logistics of it — the extra blankets, the rationing of fresh water, the noise of other people breathing and coughing and laughing — disoriented her. Oren's uncle, a broad man named Gil, insisted on helping with the lighthouse duties, and Maren found herself explaining the mechanics of the lens to someone for the first time. Gil listened with genuine fascination, his rough hands hovering over the brass gears as if they were sacred objects. The third fisherman, a quiet woman named Dessa, reorganized Maren's pantry without asking and left it twice as efficient. Maren wanted to be irritated, but the pantry genuinely worked better, and Dessa had left a small jar of homemade blackberry jam on the top shelf with a note that said *For mornings that need sweetening*.

When the fog lifted and the fishermen departed on a rescue boat from Harrow's Landing, the lighthouse felt enormous. Maren climbed to the lantern room that evening and lit the wick as always, but she stood watching the beam sweep the water longer than usual. The *Pelican Rose* crew had left behind a warmth that the iron stove couldn't replicate. She found herself thinking about the way Oren had asked her questions about the island's birds, how Gil had laughed at his own terrible jokes, how Dessa had hummed while she worked. The silence that had once felt like protection now had a different texture, like a coat that no longer fit quite right.

Two weeks later, Maren rowed to Harrow's Landing for supplies and stopped at the harbor tavern. Gil was there, and he waved her over with the enthusiasm of an old friend. He introduced her to his wife, his neighbors, the harbormaster. Maren sat at a table crowded with people she didn't know and ate fish stew that someone else had cooked, and the flavor of it — garlic, tomato, fennel — was so much richer than anything she made for herself that she nearly laughed. Oren appeared with his arm in a proper bandage and thanked her again, pressing a hand-carved wooden puffin into her palm. It was clumsy and lopsided and clearly made with care. She closed her fingers around it and felt the grain of the wood against her skin.

Gil asked if he could bring his nephew out to the island sometime to see the puffin colonies. Maren hesitated, her old instinct pulling toward refusal like a tide. But she thought of Cora, who would have already been planning the menu for the visit, and she said yes. The word came out rough, unpracticed, but Gil grinned as if she'd handed him a gift. On the row back to Fenwick, the carved puffin sat on the seat beside her, and the channel water was calm enough that she could hear the distant sound of someone playing an accordion on the Harrow's Landing pier.

Gil brought Oren the following Saturday, and they brought Dessa, who brought her daughter, a girl of eight named Luce who had never seen a lighthouse up close. Maren led them up the spiral staircase, and Luce counted every step aloud — seventy-eight, just as Maren always had. In the lantern room, Maren showed them how the Fresnel lens worked, how it took a single small flame and bent it through hundreds of glass prisms until it could reach twenty miles out to sea. Luce pressed her face close to the glass and whispered, "It gets bigger when it goes through." Dessa put a hand on Maren's shoulder and squeezed once, briefly, and the warmth of that touch lingered through the afternoon.

Over the following months, visitors came to Fenwick more often. Maren started keeping extra tea in the pantry and a second set of blankets in the closet. She taught Gil how to trim the wick properly, and he taught Oren, and Oren taught Luce, who took to the task with solemn concentration. The logbook entries grew longer again. Maren recorded the weather, yes, but also who had visited, what they'd brought, what they'd said that made her laugh. The lighthouse beam still swept the dark water every night, steady and faithful, but now the building it came from smelled like blackberry jam and wood shavings and other people's cooking.

One evening in March, Maren stood in the lantern room with Luce beside her, watching the beam reach out across the channel. Luce held the carved puffin in one hand and a mug of tea in the other, her feet barely reaching the floor of the tall keeper's stool. Down below, Gil and Dessa were arguing cheerfully about the best way to patch the skiff's hull, their voices carrying up through the open stairwell. Maren lit the wick, and the flame caught, and the lens began its slow turning, throwing light in every direction at once. Luce took a sip of her tea and said, "It's warm in here." Maren leaned against the window frame, the glass cool on her shoulder, and listened to the house full of noise below her.
