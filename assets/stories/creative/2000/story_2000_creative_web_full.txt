Maren had kept the Caulfield Point lighthouse for eleven years, and she could tell you the exact weight of a glass lens at two in the morning when your arms ached and the fog wouldn't quit. She knew the sound the gears made when they needed oiling, the particular shade of brass polish that worked best on the reflector plates, and the number of steps — one hundred and twelve — from her kitchen door to the lantern room. The lighthouse was hers in every way that mattered, and she tended it the way some people tend gardens, with a quiet fierceness that left no room for neglect.

She was also, by her own careful design, alone.

Supplies came by boat every two weeks. Harbormaster Gio would stack the crates on the dock, wave once, and motor back to the mainland. Maren preferred it that way. People complicated things. They asked questions about why she'd left the mainland, or they touched the equipment without asking, or they stayed too long and made the silence feel strange when they finally left.

Then the storm of October third tore the roof off the keeper's cottage and flooded her generator room.

Gio brought the repair crew the next morning — four people from town, strangers mostly. A carpenter named Ellis who talked too much. A teenager named Dove who was supposedly learning a trade but spent most of her time sketching seabirds in a pocket notebook. An electrician, Roan, who had enormous hands and a voice so quiet Maren had to lean in to hear him. And Gio's wife, Paloma, who simply appeared with a pot of fish stew and started feeding everyone without being asked.

Maren wanted them gone within a week. She told Gio as much.

But the damage was worse than anyone expected. The cottage needed reframing. The generator required parts shipped from three towns over. And in the meantime, the lighthouse still needed tending every night.

On the fourth evening, Maren climbed the stairs and found Dove already in the lantern room, polishing the brass plates. The girl didn't say anything. She just held up the cloth and kept working, her strokes careful, almost reverent. Maren opened her mouth to tell her to stop, then watched the way Dove's hand moved — slow, deliberate, following the curve of the metal like she knew what the work was for.

Maren picked up a second cloth and worked beside her.

By the sixth night, Ellis had built a new shelf for the oil stores, unprompted. Roan rewired the backup lantern system and added a second circuit Maren hadn't known she needed. Paloma organized the supply room with a logic so clean that Maren found things in half the time. And Dove kept coming to the lantern room each evening, quiet and steady.

The light that went out over the water those nights seemed different to Maren, though she knew the lens and the flame were the same. What changed was the room around it. There were fingerprints on the brass that weren't hers. A sketch of the lantern mechanism tacked to the wall in Dove's careful hand. A second chair Ellis had built from scrap wood, set near the window.

The repairs finished on day twelve. The crew packed their tools. Paloma left the stew pot, claiming she had three others at home. Ellis shook Maren's hand too long. Roan nodded once from the dock.

Dove was last. She stood at the base of the lighthouse, her notebook clutched against her chest.

"Could I come back sometimes?" she asked. "To help with the light?"

Maren looked up at the tower. She thought of eleven years of carrying every night alone, every polish and every climb, and how the weight of it had become so familiar she'd mistaken it for purpose.

"Tuesdays and Fridays," Maren said. "Bring your own cloth."

Dove grinned and ran for the boat. Maren watched it pull away from the dock, then walked back inside and set Paloma's stew pot on the stove. She left the cottage door open behind her, and the salt air moved through every room.
