Maren had kept the Coppervane Light for eleven years, and in all that time she'd never once lent her flame.

The supply boat came every six weeks with oil, wicks, and provisions. She rationed carefully, tracked every ounce in a leather notebook, and kept the great lamp burning through fog and squall alike. The light was hers to tend, and tending meant protecting.

So when the fishing family anchored below the cliff one October evening — their own lantern shattered, their smallest child crying in the cold bow — Maren hesitated at the top of the spiral stairs. She could see them from the gallery, four figures huddled on a boat that had no business being out past the breakwater. The father cupped his hands around his mouth and called up, asking if she could spare a bit of flame for their cabin lamp.

She almost said no. Oil was oil. Every cup she gave away was a cup subtracted from the tower.

But the child's crying carried on the wind, thin and tired, and Maren climbed down with a small tin of oil and a lit taper sheltered in her palm.

The mother took the flame with both hands. The father poured the oil into their cracked reservoir. Within minutes, a second glow appeared on the water — warm and amber against the hull.

Maren stood on the rocks watching. The Coppervane beam still swept its wide arc overhead, steady as ever. And now, below it, this smaller light bobbed on the harbor like a heartbeat. The two of them together made the cove look different — fuller, somehow, the way a room changes when a second voice joins a conversation.

The mother waved up from the deck. The child had gone quiet, wrapped in a blanket near the new warmth.

Maren waved back. She climbed the stairs to her gallery and checked the oil level in the great lamp. It read exactly what it should — no more, no less than she expected for the date.

She looked out at the two lights marking the dark water and laughed once, short and surprised, the sound swallowed happily by the wind.
