Mila had one slice left.

The pizza box sat open on the park bench. The last piece of pepperoni glistened under the afternoon sun, and the cheese had cooled just enough to hold its shape. Mila could still smell the garlic in the crust. Her stomach growled.

She'd been at the park since noon. Her soccer team had won their first game of the season, and her mom had bought two whole pizzas for the team. Everyone had eaten and run off to the playground. Now the team was gone, the second box was empty, and this one slice remained.

Mila reached for it.

Then she noticed the boy.

He stood near the water fountain, maybe ten feet away. He was smaller than Mila — five or six years old, with shoes that were too big for his feet and a T-shirt that hung past his shorts. He kept looking at the pizza box, then looking away, then looking back.

Mila picked up the slice. It was warm in her hand.

She could eat it in four bites. She was still hungry. She deserved it — she'd scored the winning goal.

But the boy's eyes kept drifting to the box.

Mila walked over to him. "You want this?"

He stared at her. "That's your last one."

"I know."

He took it with both hands, careful, like it might break. He bit into it and closed his eyes, and a long string of cheese stretched from his mouth to the crust. He laughed. Mila laughed too.

"I'm Joaquin," he said with his mouth full.

"I'm Mila."

Joaquin sat down on the curb and Mila sat beside him. He told her about his dog named Biscuit who could catch grapes in the air but was afraid of butterflies. She told him about the treehouse her dad was building in the backyard, the one with a trapdoor and a rope ladder. He said he'd never been inside a treehouse.

"You could come see it," Mila said.

"For real?"

"When it's done. My dad says maybe two more weekends."

Joaquin pulled a folded piece of paper from his pocket. "I draw stuff," he said. He unfolded it. It was a crayon picture of a dog — orange and brown, with enormous ears and a grape floating above its nose.

"That's Biscuit?"

"Yeah." He tore the paper carefully in half and handed her the side with the dog. "You can have this part."

Mila held the drawing. The crayon was waxy under her fingers.

They sat on the curb while the shadows of the oak trees stretched long across the grass. Mila's stomach was empty, but something else was full. She couldn't name it exactly. It sat warm behind her ribs like a second lunch, steady and quiet.

Her mom honked from the parking lot.

"Saturday," Joaquin said. "For the treehouse. You promised."

"I promised," Mila said.

She walked across the park with Biscuit's picture folded in her pocket. The empty pizza box flapped once in the breeze on the bench behind her. At the water fountain, Joaquin was already telling his mother about the rope ladder, his hands moving wide in the golden air, cheese still on his chin.
