Mila had one slice left.

The pizza box sat open on the park bench. The last piece of pepperoni glistened under the afternoon sun, its cheese cooled just enough to hold shape, the crust still golden underneath. Mila could smell the garlic from where she sat. Her stomach growled loud enough that she pressed her hand against it.

She'd been at Greenway Park since noon. Her soccer team, the Foxes, had won their first game of the whole season — three to two — and Mila had scored the winning goal in the last four minutes. Her mom had bought two large pepperoni pizzas from Gino's to celebrate. The team had torn through them on the bleachers, laughing and shoving and getting sauce on their jerseys.

Now the team was gone. The second box was already in the recycling bin. And this one slice remained.

Mila reached for it.

Then she noticed the boy.

He stood near the water fountain, maybe ten feet away, holding a stick he'd been dragging through the dirt. He was smaller than Mila — five or six years old — with shoes that were too big for his feet and a green T-shirt that hung past his shorts. He wasn't doing anything. He was just standing there. But his eyes kept drifting to the pizza box.

He'd look. Then look away. Then look again.

Mila picked up the slice. It was warm in her hand, heavier than she expected. The pepperoni had curled at the edges into little cups of grease the way she liked.

She could eat it in four bites. She was still hungry. She'd run hard during the game. She deserved it.

But the boy was looking again.

Mila stood up and walked over to him. Her cleats clicked on the sidewalk.

"You want this?" she said, holding out the slice.

The boy stared at her. His eyes went wide, then narrow, like he was trying to figure out the trick.

"That's your last one," he said.

"I know."

"You sure?"

"Yeah."

He took it with both hands, careful, the way you hold something made of glass. He bit into it. His eyes closed. A long string of cheese stretched from his mouth to the crust, and he caught it with his finger and wound it up.

He laughed.

Mila laughed too.

"I'm Joaquin," he said, mouth full.

"I'm Mila."

He sat down on the curb and she sat beside him. He ate slowly, like he wanted it to last. Between bites he told her things. His dog was named Biscuit. Biscuit was brown and orange and could catch grapes out of the air, but he was terrified of butterflies.

"He hides under the porch," Joaquin said. "Even the little white ones scare him."

"My cat is scared of the vacuum," Mila said.

"Every cat is scared of the vacuum."

"Fair."

She told him about the treehouse her dad was building in the backyard. It was going to have a trapdoor in the floor and a rope ladder and a bucket on a pulley so you could send snacks up from the ground.

Joaquin stopped chewing. "A snack bucket?"

"My dad's idea."

"That's the best idea anyone has ever had."

"I know."

He said he'd never been inside a treehouse. Not once. He'd seen them in books and in a movie about pirates, but never in a real yard with a real tree.

Mila pulled a piece of grass from the edge of the curb. She twisted it between her fingers.

"You could come see it," she said. "When it's done."

Joaquin looked at her. "For real?"

"My dad says maybe two more weekends. He still has to do the railing."

"I could bring Biscuit."

"You should definitely bring Biscuit."

Joaquin finished the last bite of crust. He wiped his hands on his shorts and then reached into his pocket. He pulled out a folded piece of paper, soft at the creases from being carried around.

"I draw stuff," he said. He unfolded it.

It was a crayon drawing of a dog. Orange and brown, with enormous triangle ears and a wide grin. A single purple grape floated in the air above the dog's nose. The background was just green scribbles, but the dog was careful. Someone had taken time on that dog.

"That's Biscuit," Joaquin said.

"He looks fast," Mila said.

"He is fast."

Joaquin studied the drawing. Then he tore the paper carefully down the middle. The left side had the dog. The right side had a tree that Mila hadn't noticed before, with a yellow sun behind it.

He handed her the side with the dog.

"You can have this part," he said.

Mila held it. The crayon was waxy under her fingertips. One of Biscuit's ears crossed onto the torn edge, so it looked like it went on forever, past the border of the paper, into somewhere she couldn't see.

"Thank you," she said.

"Thank you," Joaquin said. "For the pizza."

They sat on the curb and didn't say anything for a while. The oak trees along the path threw long shadows across the grass. A jogger passed. A squirrel ran halfway down a trunk, froze, and ran back up. The breeze smelled like cut grass and warm concrete.

Mila's stomach was empty. But something else was full. She couldn't name it exactly. It sat warm behind her ribs, steady and quiet, like a small lamp someone had switched on in a room she didn't know she had.

Her mom honked twice from the parking lot.

Mila stood up and brushed the grass off her soccer shorts. Joaquin stood too.

"Saturday," he said. "For the treehouse. You promised."

"I promised."

"With the snack bucket."

"With the snack bucket."

He grinned. Cheese was still on his chin.

Mila walked across the park with Biscuit's picture folded in her pocket, her cleats tapping the sidewalk. The empty pizza box sat on the bench behind her. The breeze caught its lid and flapped it once, then let it rest.

At the water fountain, Joaquin was already pulling his mother by the hand toward the parking lot, talking about a rope ladder, his free arm swinging wide in the golden air.
