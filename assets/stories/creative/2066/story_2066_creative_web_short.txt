The rocking chair had worn two pale arcs into the porch boards over the years. Maren settled into it the way water settles into a familiar channel, her weight finding the grooves without effort.

The street was letting go of its noise.

A screen door closed three houses down. A dog barked once, then stopped, as if it had forgotten why it started. The Hendersons' sprinkler ticked back and forth across their lawn, throwing a low fan of water that caught the last copper light.

Maren set her tea on the wide arm of the chair. Steam curled up and disappeared.

She had lived on this street for forty-one years. She had watched children grow tall enough to reach the mailbox, then tall enough to leave. She had carried casseroles to porches and received them on her own. The giving and the receiving had come in seasons, like weather.

The oak tree across the street held its branches still. No wind.

A boy on a bicycle turned the corner and pedaled slowly past. He lifted one hand from the handlebars. Maren raised hers.

That was all.

The sky above the rooflines had turned the color of a peach left on the counter — soft orange fading into something almost gray. Streetlights blinked on, one after the other, down the block. They always started from the south end first. She had noticed that years ago.

Her husband's chair sat empty beside her. The cushion still held the shape of him, though the fabric had faded from blue to a pale, uncertain color. She did not look at it directly. She did not need to.

A mockingbird started up in the hedge, running through its borrowed songs. It sang something that sounded like a cardinal, then a wren, then something she could not name.

The rocker moved. The boards beneath it answered.

Maren wrapped both hands around the warm cup and watched the first star appear — low, steady, fixed in the deep blue above the Hendersons' chimney.
