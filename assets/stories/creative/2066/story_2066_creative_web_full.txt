The rocking chair had worn two pale arcs into the porch boards over the years. Maren settled into it the way water settles into a familiar channel, her weight finding the grooves without effort. The cushion beneath her had gone flat on one side. She did not mind.

The street was letting go of its noise.

A screen door closed three houses down. A dog barked once, then stopped, as if it had forgotten why it started. The Hendersons' sprinkler ticked back and forth across their lawn, throwing a low fan of water that caught the last copper light.

Maren set her tea on the wide arm of the chair. Steam curled up and disappeared.

She had lived on this street for forty-one years. She had watched children grow tall enough to reach the mailbox, then tall enough to leave. She had carried casseroles to porches and received them on her own. The giving and the receiving had come in seasons, like weather.

The oak tree across the street held its branches still. No wind.

A boy on a bicycle turned the corner and pedaled slowly past, his front wheel wobbling just slightly. He lifted one hand from the handlebars. Maren raised hers. He turned at the end of the block and was gone.

Her neighbor Ruth stepped out onto her own porch carrying a watering can. She tilted it over a row of clay pots along the railing. Water darkened the soil.

"Still warm," Ruth called across the two yards.

"Still warm," Maren agreed.

Ruth finished her watering and went back inside. The screen door latched softly behind her.

The sky above the rooflines had turned the color of a peach left on the counter — soft orange fading into something almost gray. Streetlights blinked on, one after the other, down the block. They always started from the south end first. She had noticed that years ago, mentioned it to Harold, and he had walked the whole length of the street one evening to confirm it.

His chair sat empty beside her. The cushion still held the shape of him, though the fabric had faded from blue to a pale, uncertain color. She did not look at it directly. She did not need to.

Forty-one years on this porch. She had nursed babies in this chair. She had shelled peas in it, read letters in it, fallen asleep in it on August afternoons when the heat pressed down like a hand. She had sat in it the night the hospital called, and she had sat in it the morning after, watching the sun come up as if the world did not know what it had done.

But the world had kept on. The sprinklers ran. The children pedaled past.

A mockingbird started up in the hedge, running through its borrowed songs. It sang something that sounded like a cardinal, then a wren, then something she could not name. It paused. It started again from the beginning.

The rocker moved. The boards beneath it answered.

Somewhere a few streets over, someone was grilling. The smell of charcoal drifted through, faint and familiar. A car passed without stopping. Its headlights swept across the yard and moved on.

Maren wrapped both hands around the warm cup. The tea had cooled enough to drink. She lifted it and tasted it — chamomile, a little honey, the way she had always made it for two.

The first star appeared low and steady, fixed in the deep blue above the Hendersons' chimney. Then a second, higher, fainter.

The rocker moved. The porch held.
