The rocking chair had worn two pale arcs into the porch boards over the years. Maren settled into it the way water settles into a familiar channel, her weight finding the grooves without effort. The cushion beneath her had gone flat on one side and thin at the edges, and the armrests had been rubbed smooth to a shine that no varnish could explain.

She pressed her feet against the boards. The chair moved.

The street was letting go of its noise.

A screen door closed three houses down. A dog barked once, then stopped, as if it had forgotten why it started. The Hendersons' sprinkler ticked back and forth across their lawn, throwing a low fan of water that caught the last copper light and broke it into pieces.

Maren set her tea on the wide arm of the chair. Steam curled up and disappeared into the cooling air. The mug was clay, brown-glazed, with a chip on the handle she could feel with her thumb.

She had lived on this street for forty-one years. She had watched children grow tall enough to reach the mailbox, then tall enough to mow the lawn, then tall enough to leave. She had carried casseroles to porches and received them on her own. She had stood in driveways and held other women's hands in the dark. She had been held.

The giving and the receiving had come in seasons, like weather.

The oak tree across the street held its branches still. No wind. Its leaves had thickened through June into a canopy so dense the streetlight beneath it barely reached the sidewalk.

A boy on a bicycle turned the corner and pedaled slowly past, his front wheel wobbling just slightly, his sneakers loose on the pedals. He lifted one hand from the handlebars. Maren raised hers. The boy turned at the end of the block and was gone, and the sound of his tires on the pavement faded into the larger quiet.

Her neighbor Ruth stepped out onto her own porch carrying a watering can. She tilted it over a row of clay pots along the railing. Water darkened the soil and dripped through the drainage holes onto the painted boards below.

"Still warm," Ruth called across the two yards.

"Still warm," Maren agreed.

"You need anything?"

Maren shook her head. "I'm fine right here."

Ruth finished her watering, tapped the last drops from the can, and went back inside. The screen door latched softly behind her. Through the lit window, Maren could see Ruth moving in her kitchen, opening a cabinet, reaching for something on a shelf.

Ordinary things.

The sky above the rooflines had turned the color of a peach left on the counter — soft orange fading into something almost gray, with a thin band of gold still burning along the western edge where the sun had gone. Streetlights blinked on, one after the other, down the block. They always started from the south end first. She had noticed that years ago, mentioned it to Harold, and he had walked the whole length of the street one evening just to confirm it. He came back nodding.

"South to north," he said. "Every time."

She could still hear the way he said it — quiet, satisfied, as though the order of streetlights was a thing worth confirming.

His chair sat beside her. The cushion still held the shape of him, though the fabric had faded from blue to a pale, uncertain color. A crease ran along the back where he used to lean his head. She did not look at it directly. She did not need to. It was there the way the porch was there, the way the street was there.

Forty-one years on this porch. She had nursed babies in this chair. She had shelled peas in it, pressing her thumbnail along the seam until the pod split and the peas rolled into the bowl in her lap. She had read letters in it — from her sister in Roanoke, from her son at boot camp, from the church when they asked her to teach the children's class. She had fallen asleep in it on August afternoons when the heat pressed down like a hand and the cicadas sawed through the stillness.

She had sat in it the night the hospital called. And she had sat in it the morning after, watching the sun come up as if the world did not know what it had done.

But the world had kept on. The sprinklers ran. The children pedaled past. The mail arrived at eleven-fifteen.

Maren lifted the mug and turned it slowly in her hands. The warmth seeped through the clay into her palms and her fingers. She thought of the garden out back — the tomatoes she had staked that morning, the basil that had come up thicker than she expected. She would pick some tomorrow. She would take a bundle to Ruth.

A mockingbird started up in the hedge along the fence, running through its borrowed songs one after another without pause. It sang something that sounded like a cardinal, then a wren, then a sharp two-note call she could not name. It paused. It started again from the beginning, as though practicing for an audience that had not yet arrived.

The rocker moved. The boards beneath it answered.

Somewhere a few streets over, someone was grilling. The smell of charcoal and something sweet — maybe a sauce, maybe just the wood — drifted through the evening air. A car passed without stopping. Its headlights swept across the yard and the trunk of the oak and moved on, leaving the street darker for a moment before the eyes adjusted.

Two girls walked past on the sidewalk, one carrying a basketball under her arm. They were talking low, their voices blending into a single murmur. One of them laughed. The sound carried.

Maren wrapped both hands around the mug. The tea had cooled enough to drink. She lifted it and tasted it — chamomile, a little honey, the way she had always made it. For years she had made two cups and carried them both out here. The second cup sat on the second armrest. They drank without talking, sometimes, for an hour.

One cup now. She held it with both hands.

The first star appeared low and steady, fixed in the deep blue above the Hendersons' chimney. Then a second, higher, fainter. Then a third, near the place where the gold band had been.

The mockingbird sang on.

The rocker moved forward. The rocker moved back. The porch held its ground beneath her, solid and sure, the old boards still carrying what they had always carried.

Maren set the empty mug on the armrest and folded her hands in her lap. The street had gone quiet. The sprinkler had stopped. The air smelled like cut grass and charcoal and the faint sweetness of the honeysuckle climbing the trellis at the far end of the porch.

She closed her eyes. She opened them.

The stars had multiplied.
