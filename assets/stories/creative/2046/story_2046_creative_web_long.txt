Have you ever held a piece of wood in your hands and felt like it wanted to become something? Juliana had. She felt it every single time she walked through the side door of her aunt's workshop.

The workshop was behind the house, in an old two-car garage that hadn't held a car in years. It smelled like cedar shavings and linseed oil and the faint sweetness of wood glue. Tools hung on the pegboard wall in neat rows — chisels and hand planes and rasps and files, each one resting in its own outline drawn with black marker so you always knew where it belonged. A big workbench sat in the middle, scarred and stained from a thousand projects. And everywhere, on every surface, sawdust lay in pale gold drifts like the lightest, finest snow.

Juliana came here every Saturday. It was her favorite place in the world.

Today, Tia Carmen was building a rocking horse for Juliana's baby cousin, who was turning one next month. The body was already done — smooth and curved, sanded until the grain shone in the overhead light. Two rockers sat underneath, their curves precise and even. But the horse had no head yet. Just a flat neck stump sticking up at the front, waiting.

Juliana ran her fingers along the horse's back. The wood was silky. "Can I make the head?"

Tia Carmen looked at her over the tops of her safety glasses. She had a pencil behind one ear and sawdust in her eyebrows. "You think you're ready for that? The head is the most important part. It's what gives the horse its personality."

"I've been practicing on the scrap pieces all month. I carved a duck last week."

"How did it turn out?"

"It looked almost like a duck. Mostly like a duck. My brother said it looked like a potato, but he's wrong."

Tia Carmen's mouth twitched. "Almost counts in this workshop. Every good carver started with a potato." She reached under the bench and set a block of maple on the surface. It was dense and cream-colored, with a tight, beautiful grain. "The head is yours."

Juliana's heart jumped. She pulled on her own safety glasses — the ones Tia Carmen had bought her with her name written on the strap in silver marker — and picked up a pencil.

She drew the shape right on the wood, turning the block to sketch all four sides. A horse's head with its ears pointing forward, alert and friendly. Wide eyes. A gentle slope to the nose. She took her time with the drawing. Tia Carmen always said, "Measure twice, cut once, and draw three times before you even think about the saw."

Then she clamped the block in the bench vise and picked up the coping saw.

The saw buzzed through the maple in thin, careful strokes. Sawdust fell in a soft stream onto the bench and Juliana's shoes. She followed her pencil line, turning the block a little at a time, cutting the rough shape free from the square block. The horse was in there, inside the wood. She just had to find it.

Then came the chisel work — the slow, patient part. She held the chisel at an angle and tapped it with the wooden mallet, shaving away thin curls of maple that spiraled down and landed in her lap. The horse's face came forward bit by bit, like someone waking up. The curve of the cheek appeared. Then the slope of the nose. Then the small, delicate notch between the ears.

It took most of the afternoon. The light through the garage windows shifted from bright white to warm gold. Juliana's arms ached from holding the chisel steady. Her fingers were dusty and sore. Twice she had to stop and shake out her hands, opening and closing them until the stiffness faded.

But she kept going. She smoothed the cheeks with the rasp. She defined the eyes with a small V-gouge, giving them depth and gentleness. She sanded and sanded, working through finer grits until the wood felt like river stone under her fingertips.

When she held the finished head up to the workshop light, she grinned so wide her cheeks ached.

It looked like a horse. Not almost. Not mostly. Really and truly. A friendly, bright-eyed little horse with its ears up, ready to listen.

Tia Carmen put down her own work and came over. She took the head in both hands and turned it slowly, studying it from every angle. She ran her thumb along the jawline. She looked at the ears, the eyes, the careful curve of the muzzle.

"Juliana," she said. "This is beautiful work."

"Really?"

"Really. Look at this grain here, how you followed it around the cheek. That takes instinct. You can't teach that."

Juliana felt something bloom in her chest — warm and bright, like swallowing sunlight.

They mixed the wood glue together and attached the head to the body. Juliana held it steady with both hands wrapped around the neck while the glue set, feeling the smooth wood warm under her palms. She could see the whole horse now. Body and head and rockers, all one piece. Her cousin was going to ride this. A baby was going to sit on something Juliana helped make with her own two hands.

"It's a real rocking horse," she said.

"It sure is."

Juliana sat down on the workshop floor with sawdust in her hair and wood shavings on her jeans and glue on her fingers, and she laughed. Not because anything funny had happened. Just because something inside her was so full and bright it had to come out somehow, and laughter was the closest thing to what she felt.
