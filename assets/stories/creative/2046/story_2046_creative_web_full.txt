Have you ever held a piece of wood and felt that it wanted to become something? Juliana had. She felt it every time she walked into her aunt's workshop.

The workshop was in an old garage that smelled like cedar shavings and linseed oil. Tools hung on the pegboard wall, each in its own outline drawn with black marker. Sawdust covered the floor like pale gold snow.

Juliana came here every Saturday. It was her favorite place in the world.

Today, Tia Carmen was building a rocking horse for Juliana's baby cousin. The body was smooth and curved, sanded until it shone. But it had no head yet — just a neck stump sticking up at the front, waiting.

"Can I make the head?" Juliana asked.

Tia Carmen looked at her over her safety glasses. "You think you're ready? The head is the most important part."

"I've been practicing all month. I carved a duck last week."

"How did it turn out?"

"It looked almost like a duck. My brother said it looked like a potato, but he's wrong."

Tia Carmen's mouth twitched. "Almost counts. Every good carver started with a potato." She set a block of maple on the workbench. "The head is yours."

Juliana pulled on her safety glasses and picked up a pencil. She drew the shape right on the wood — a horse's head with ears forward, alert and friendly. Wide eyes. A gentle slope to the nose.

She clamped the block in the vise and picked up the coping saw. It buzzed through the maple in careful strokes. Sawdust fell in a soft stream. She followed her pencil line, turning the block, cutting the rough shape free. The horse was in there, inside the wood. She just had to find it.

Then came the chisel work. She tapped the mallet gently, shaving away thin curls of maple. The horse's face emerged bit by bit. The curve of the cheek. The slope of the nose. The small notch between the ears.

It took most of the afternoon. Her arms ached and her fingers were sore. But she kept going — smoothing with the rasp, defining the eyes with a V-gouge, sanding through finer grits until the wood felt like river stone.

When she held the finished head up to the light, she grinned so wide her cheeks ached.

It looked like a horse. Not almost. Really and truly.

Tia Carmen took it and turned it slowly, studying every angle. "Juliana. This is beautiful work. Look at this grain, how you followed it around the cheek. You can't teach that."

Something bloomed in Juliana's chest — warm and bright, like swallowing sunlight.

They glued the head onto the body together. Juliana held it steady with both hands while the glue set. She could see the whole horse now. Her cousin was going to ride something Juliana helped make with her own two hands.

She sat on the workshop floor with sawdust in her hair and laughed — not because anything was funny, but because something inside her was so full it had to come out somehow.
