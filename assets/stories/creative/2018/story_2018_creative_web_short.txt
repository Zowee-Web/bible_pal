The stone bench had been there longer than anyone in the village could remember.

It sat at the edge of the monastery garden, half-shaded by a fig tree whose branches had grown crooked with age. The bench was smooth from years of sitting — its surface worn to a soft hollow in the center where countless people had rested.

From the kitchen window, Sister Adah watched the young woman find it.

The visitor had arrived that morning, checking in at the guesthouse with a bag that looked hastily packed and eyes that looked like they hadn't closed in days. She'd eaten nothing at breakfast. She'd paced the cloister walk twice, her phone pressed to her ear, her free hand clenching and unclenching at her side.

Now the phone was in her pocket. She stood at the edge of the garden, looking at the bench as if deciding whether to trust it.

She sat down.

Nothing happened. That was the point.

The fig tree shifted its leaves in a slow wind. A wren landed on the armrest, considered the woman, and flew off toward the bell tower. The sun moved a thumb's width across the flagstones.

The young woman's shoulders dropped. Not all at once, but in stages — the way ice melts, so gradually you can't identify the exact moment solid becomes liquid. Her hands uncurled in her lap. Her breathing, which Adah could practically see from the window, slowed and deepened.

She sat for an hour. She sat for two.

Adah washed dishes, dried them, put them away. She kneaded bread and set it to rise. Every few minutes she glanced out the window. The woman hadn't moved, but she was different. Something in her posture had softened, the way a sail softens when the storm passes and the wind goes gentle.

Late in the afternoon, the woman stood. She touched the fig tree's trunk with one hand, lightly, the way you'd touch a friend's shoulder. Then she walked back toward the guesthouse.

The bench remained. The fig tree rustled. The wren returned to its spot on the armrest.

Sister Adah smiled at the bread dough rising under its cloth, warm and patient and unhurried, and thought about how peace sometimes needs nothing more than a place to sit down.
