The stone bench had been there longer than anyone in the village could remember.

It sat at the edge of the monastery garden, half-shaded by a fig tree whose branches had grown crooked with age. The bench was smooth from years of sitting — its surface worn to a soft hollow in the center where countless people had rested. Lichens patterned the legs in pale green, and one corner had chipped decades ago, leaving a rough edge that the rain had since softened.

From the kitchen window, Sister Adah watched the young woman find it.

The visitor had arrived that morning, checking in at the guesthouse with a bag that looked hastily packed and eyes that looked like they hadn't closed in days. She'd eaten nothing at breakfast. She'd paced the cloister walk twice, phone pressed to her ear, free hand clenching and unclenching at her side. Her shoes were wrong for the gravel paths — city shoes, thin-soled, meant for pavement and hurrying.

Now the phone was in her pocket. She stood at the edge of the garden, looking at the bench as if deciding whether to trust it. The morning light came through the fig leaves and laid a lattice of shadow across the stone.

She sat down.

Nothing happened. That was the point.

The fig tree shifted its leaves in a slow wind that smelled of rosemary and turned earth. A wren landed on the armrest, considered the woman with its head tilted, and flew off toward the bell tower. Somewhere in the orchard, a bee worked the late blossoms. The sun moved a thumb's width across the flagstones.

The young woman's shoulders dropped. Not all at once, but in stages — the way ice melts, so gradually you can't identify the exact moment solid becomes liquid. Her hands uncurled in her lap. Her breathing, which Sister Adah could practically see from the window, slowed and deepened.

She sat for an hour. She sat for two. The garden did what the garden always did — it grew, it rustled, it held its ground. It asked nothing of her.

Adah washed dishes, dried them, put them away. She kneaded bread and set it to rise beneath a cotton cloth. She swept the kitchen floor and wiped down the long oak table where the community would eat their evening meal. Every few minutes she glanced out the window. The woman hadn't moved, but she was different.

Something in her posture had softened, the way a sail softens when the storm passes and the wind goes gentle. Her face, which had been tight as a fist, had opened slightly. She wasn't smiling. It was quieter than a smile — more like the absence of the thing that had been clenched.

A cat appeared from under the hedge and curled at the woman's feet. She reached down and touched its back with two fingers. The cat purred. The wren returned.

Late in the afternoon, the woman stood. She stretched — arms overhead, slow, like someone remembering they had a body. She touched the fig tree's trunk with one hand, lightly, the way you'd touch a friend's shoulder in passing. Then she walked back toward the guesthouse. Her steps were slower now. Unhurried.

The bench remained. The fig tree rustled. The cat yawned and stayed where it was.

Sister Adah smiled at the bread dough rising under its cloth — warm, patient, unhurried — and thought about how peace sometimes needs nothing more than a place to sit down and the faith to stay there long enough for the stillness to do its work.
