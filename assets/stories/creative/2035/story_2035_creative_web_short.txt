"You're going to think this is stupid," Wren said, sliding into the booth across from her sister.

Margot looked up from her coffee. "Try me."

"I applied for the teaching fellowship. The one in Asheville."

"That's not stupid."

"I haven't taught in three years. I dropped out of the credential program. My resume has a gap the size of a canyon."

Margot set down her mug. "Did you finish the application?"

"All of it. The essay and the references. I stayed up until two. Rewrote the essay four times."

"Four times." Margot leaned back. "People who don't care rewrite things zero times."

Wren tore the corner of a sugar packet. White crystals fell on the table. "What if they say no?"

"Then they say no. And you'll still be the person who stayed up until two because she cared enough to try four times."

The diner was warm and loud. A cook called an order through the window. Someone laughed in the booth behind them.

"I keep thinking about Mrs. Calloway," Wren said. "Fourth grade. She told me I asked the best questions of anyone in her class. Nobody had ever said anything like that to me."

"I remember. You came home practically vibrating."

"One sentence. She probably forgot it by the next week. But I never forgot. I want to be that for someone."

Margot brushed the spilled sugar off the table's edge. "You already are. You just haven't met them yet."

Wren looked at her sister — steady and certain, the way she'd been their whole lives. Through the move, the divorce, every late-night phone call when Wren needed someone to say the thing she couldn't say to herself.

"Okay," Wren said. "I'll wait for their answer. And I won't preemptively reject myself before they get the chance."

Margot smiled. "That's the bravest sentence you've said all morning."

Wren laughed — small, a little shaky, but real. She picked up a menu she didn't need. "I'm getting pancakes. Big ones."

"Get the biggest ones they have," Margot said. "You earned them."
