"You're going to think this is stupid," Wren said, sliding into the booth across from her older sister at the Clearwater Diner.

Margot looked up from her coffee. She had arrived fifteen minutes early, as always, and was halfway through the crossword on the back of the placemat. "Try me."

"I applied for the teaching fellowship. The one at the Montessori school in Asheville."

"That's not stupid."

"I haven't taught anything in three years, Margot. I dropped out of the credential program. I was working at a call center six months ago. My resume has a gap the size of a canyon, and the essay question asked why I wanted to teach, and I nearly wrote 'because I can't think of anything else that matters' and then deleted it because it sounded desperate."

She pulled a sugar packet from the chrome dispenser and turned it over in her fingers, creasing the paper with her thumbnail.

Margot set down her mug. The coffee inside had gone lukewarm. She didn't care. "Did you finish the application?"

"Yes."

"All of it? The essay, the references, the portfolio piece?"

"All of it. I stayed up until two in the morning. I rewrote the essay four times. I asked Professor Hendricks for a reference and she said yes within an hour, which honestly made me cry a little."

"Four times." Margot leaned back against the vinyl seat. "Wren, people who don't care rewrite things zero times. People who care a little rewrite once. You rewrote it four times at two in the morning. That's not desperation. That's precision."

Wren tore the corner of the sugar packet. A thin line of white crystals fell on the table between them. "What if they say no?"

"Then they say no. And you'll still be the person who stayed up rewriting because she cared enough to get it right. That person exists whether Asheville sees her or not."

The diner was warm and loud around them. A cook called an order through the kitchen window — two over easy, wheat toast. Plates clattered on the pass. A child in the corner booth drew circles on the fogged glass with his finger. Someone laughed behind them, a bright sudden sound that cut through the noise and disappeared.

"I keep thinking about Mrs. Calloway," Wren said. She set the torn sugar packet down. "Remember her? Fourth grade. The one who—"

"The green cardigan," Margot said. "She wore it every single day."

"Every day. Even in May." Wren smiled at the table. "She told me once — it was after school, I was the last one in the classroom because I'd been reorganizing the book corner — she told me I asked the best questions of anyone in her class. She said most kids wanted answers, but I wanted to understand things."

She paused. The memory was twenty years old and still sharp-edged.

"Nobody had ever said anything like that to me before. I was nine. I didn't even know what she meant, exactly. But I felt it. Something opened up."

"I remember," Margot said. "You came home and told Mom, and you were practically vibrating. You said, 'My teacher says I'm good at wondering.' Mom put it on the fridge."

"One sentence." Wren shook her head. "She probably forgot she said it by the next week. But I carried it for twenty years. I'm still carrying it. That's what I want to do, Margot. I want to be the person who says the one sentence that someone carries."

The diner noise settled into its background rhythm. The cook slid two plates onto the counter. The child at the window had drawn a lopsided sun.

Margot reached across the table and brushed the spilled sugar off the edge with the side of her hand. "You already are that person. You just haven't met them yet."

Wren looked at her sister. Margot's face was steady and open, the same face that had been across from her at a hundred tables — the kitchen table during their parents' divorce, the dorm room desk during college, the sticky booth at the pizza place after Wren quit the credential program and couldn't stop apologizing for it. Margot never fixed things. She just stayed at the table.

"Okay," Wren said.

"Okay what?"

"Okay, I'll wait for their answer. And I won't preemptively reject myself before they get the chance to reject me or, possibly, not reject me."

Margot smiled. "That's the bravest sentence you've said all morning."

Wren laughed. It was small and slightly shaky and entirely real. She picked up a menu she didn't need — she'd been ordering the same thing here for six years — and opened it wide in front of her face. "I'm getting pancakes. Big ones. The kind with the powdered sugar."

"Get the biggest ones they have," Margot said. "You earned them at two in the morning."

Wren set the menu down. Through the window, the parking lot was bright with mid-morning sun, and a woman was walking in with a little girl who held the door open with both hands, straining against the weight of it, grinning like the effort was the best part.

Wren watched them. Something in her chest felt lighter than it had in months — not because the fear was gone, but because it had been named aloud at a table with someone who didn't flinch at it.

She flagged down the waitress. The pancakes would be enormous. She was going to eat every bite.
