"You're going to think this is stupid," Wren said, sliding into the booth across from her older sister.

Margot looked up from her coffee. "Try me."

"I applied for the teaching fellowship. The one in Asheville."

"That's not stupid."

"I haven't taught anything in three years. I dropped out of the credential program. My resume has a gap the size of a canyon." Wren pulled a sugar packet from the dispenser and turned it over in her fingers. "What if they say no?"

Margot set down her mug. "Did you finish the application?"

"All of it. I stayed up until two in the morning. I rewrote the essay four times."

"Four times." Margot leaned back. "People who don't care rewrite things zero times. You rewrote it four times at two in the morning. That's not desperation. That's precision."

Wren tore the sugar packet's corner. White crystals fell on the table. "But what if they say no?"

"Then they say no. And you'll still be the person who stayed up because she cared enough to get it right. That person exists whether Asheville sees her or not."

The diner was warm and loud around them. A cook called an order through the kitchen window. Plates clattered. A child in the corner booth drew circles on the fogged glass.

"I keep thinking about Mrs. Calloway," Wren said. "Remember her? Fourth grade."

"The green cardigan. She wore it every single day."

"She told me once — I was the last one in the classroom, reorganizing the book corner — she said I asked the best questions of anyone in her class. That most kids wanted answers, but I wanted to understand things."

She paused. The memory was twenty years old and still sharp.

"Nobody had ever said anything like that to me. I was nine. I didn't even know what she meant. But I felt it. Something opened up."

"I remember," Margot said. "You came home vibrating. You told Mom, 'My teacher says I'm good at wondering.' She put it on the fridge."

"One sentence. She probably forgot she said it by the next week. But I carried it for twenty years." Wren looked at the sugar on the table. "That's what I want to do, Margot. I want to be the person who says the one sentence someone carries."

Margot reached across and brushed the spilled sugar off the table's edge. "You already are that person. You just haven't met them yet."

Wren looked at her sister. Margot's face was steady and certain, the way it had been their whole lives — through the divorce, through college, through the pizza booth after Wren quit the credential program. Margot never fixed things. She just stayed at the table.

"Okay," Wren said.

"Okay what?"

"Okay, I'll wait for their answer. And I won't preemptively reject myself before they get the chance."

Margot smiled. "That's the bravest sentence you've said all morning."

Wren laughed — small, shaky, real. She picked up a menu she didn't need and opened it wide. "I'm getting pancakes. Big ones."

"Get the biggest ones they have. You earned them at two in the morning."

Through the window, a woman walked in with a little girl who held the door open with both hands, straining, grinning like the effort was the best part.

Wren watched them. Something in her chest felt lighter — not because the fear was gone, but because it had been named aloud at a table with someone who didn't flinch.

She flagged down the waitress. The pancakes would be enormous. She was going to eat every bite.
