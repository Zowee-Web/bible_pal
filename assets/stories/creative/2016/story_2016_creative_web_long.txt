"You've checked the weather four times," Lila said from the doorway.

Gil looked up from his phone, caught. He had also checked the road conditions, the bridge report, and the tide table, though the ocean was sixty miles from their house and had nothing to do with tomorrow's drive. He'd checked the traffic cameras on Route 9, even though the interview wasn't until two in the afternoon and the morning rush would be long finished.

"Just being prepared," he said.

Lila leaned against the doorframe. She wore her old college sweatshirt, the one with the fraying cuffs, and her eyes were soft in the hallway light.

"You're going to be fine," she said.

"You don't know that."

"No," she agreed. "But I know you."

She kissed the top of his head and went to bed, leaving him alone with the glow of the screen and the quiet hum of the refrigerator. The house ticked around him — pipes cooling, the settling creak of old joists. Every small sound was a question his mind tried to answer before it was asked.

Tomorrow was the interview. The one at the firm downtown, the one he'd spent three weeks rehearsing for. He could see himself sitting in the lobby in his good shoes, could already feel the tightness in his collar. The whole thing could change everything. Or it could change nothing. Both possibilities sat in his chest like stones, and he kept turning them over, as if enough examination might tell him which was heavier.

He closed the weather app. Opened it again. Closed it.

Then he turned off the phone entirely and stepped outside.

The porch was cold. The boards were rough under his socks, and a moth tapped against the yellow bulb by the door. Above the neighbors' roofline, the sky was cluttered with stars he rarely noticed — more than seemed reasonable for a Tuesday in October. He sat on the top step and pressed his palms flat against the wood, feeling the grain, the slight dampness of evening dew.

A sound reached him. Faint. Rhythmic. He held still.

Across the street, in the Okafors' yard, a woman he'd never seen before was pushing a child on a tire swing. Back and forth, slow and steady, the rope creaking against the branch. The child wasn't laughing or shouting. She was just swinging, head tilted back, watching the same sky Gil had only just looked up at.

The woman hummed something low. Gil couldn't name the tune, but it settled into the air the way wood smoke settles — unhurried, finding its own level. There was no performance in it, no audience. She hummed the way someone breathes — because the body knows to, because the rhythm is older than the reason.

He realized his hands had unclenched. When had they been clenched? His shoulders had dropped an inch. The list of things that could go wrong tomorrow still existed, somewhere behind his sternum, but it had moved slightly to the side, the way furniture shifts when someone opens a window and a breeze passes through.

The child on the swing pointed up at something — a star, a plane, a satellite threading its tiny light across the dark. The woman looked where she pointed and nodded, slow and serious, as if the child had said something worth agreeing with.

Gil looked up too.

Whatever the child had found, he couldn't identify it. But the looking itself felt like enough — three people tilted toward the same dark sky, each one finding something worth noticing in all that wide, uncharted space. Nobody was checking anything. Nobody was preparing. They were just there, present to a moment that didn't require anything of them.

The humming continued. The swing creaked its patient rhythm. Somewhere down the block a dog barked once and stopped, satisfied with its own announcement. A car passed on the main road, its headlights sweeping across the trees and disappearing.

Gil sat on the porch for another ten minutes. He didn't check the time. The air smelled like cut grass and the faint iron tang of the garden hose left coiled by the steps. A cricket started up near the foundation, tentative at first, then committed.

He thought about the interview. It was still there — the questions he'd rehearsed, the route he'd mapped, the shoes he'd polished. But the thinking felt different now. Quieter. Like the difference between gripping a rope and simply holding it.

The woman across the street gathered the child from the swing. The girl wrapped her arms around the woman's neck and rested her head on her shoulder, already half-asleep. They went inside, and the tire swing swayed empty in the dark, still carrying the ghost of its arc.

Gil went inside too. He left his phone on the kitchen counter, screen down. He brushed his teeth listening to the silence of the house, and for the first time in weeks, the silence didn't feel like it was waiting for something to go wrong.

It just felt like silence. The kind that holds you up instead of pressing in.

He pulled the covers back and lay down next to Lila. She shifted toward him in her sleep, one hand finding his arm.

Tomorrow would come with whatever it carried. But tonight the stars were out, a child had found something worth pointing at, and a stranger's quiet humming had drifted across a street and landed exactly where it was needed.

That was enough. For now, that was plenty.
