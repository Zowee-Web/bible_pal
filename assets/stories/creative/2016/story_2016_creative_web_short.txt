"You've checked the weather four times," Lila said from the doorway.

Gil looked up from his phone, caught. He had also checked the road conditions, the bridge report, and the tide table, though the ocean was sixty miles from their house and had nothing to do with tomorrow's drive.

"Just being prepared," he said.

Lila didn't argue. She kissed the top of his head and went to bed, leaving him alone with the glow of the screen and the quiet hum of the refrigerator.

He stepped outside. The porch was cold, the boards rough under his socks. Above the neighbors' roofline, the sky was cluttered with stars he rarely noticed. He sat on the top step and pressed his palms flat against the wood, feeling the grain, the slight dampness of evening dew.

A sound reached him. Faint, rhythmic. He held still.

Across the street, in the Okafors' yard, a woman he'd never seen before was pushing a child on a tire swing. Back and forth, slow and steady, the rope creaking against the branch. The child wasn't laughing or shouting. She was just swinging, head tilted back, watching the same sky Gil had only just looked up at.

The woman hummed something low. Gil couldn't name the tune, but it settled into the air the way wood smoke settles — unhurried, finding its own level.

He realized his hands had unclenched. His shoulders had dropped an inch. The list of things that could go wrong tomorrow still existed, but it had moved slightly to the side, the way furniture shifts when someone opens a window and a breeze passes through.

The child on the swing pointed up at something — a star, a plane, a satellite. The woman looked where she pointed and nodded.

Gil looked up too.

Whatever the child had found, he couldn't identify it. But the looking itself felt like enough — three people tilted toward the same dark sky, each one finding something worth noticing in all that wide, uncharted space.
