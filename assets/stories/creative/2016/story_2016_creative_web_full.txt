"You've checked the weather four times," Lila said from the doorway.

Gil looked up from his phone, caught. He had also checked the road conditions, the bridge report, and the tide table, though the ocean was sixty miles from their house and had nothing to do with tomorrow's drive.

"Just being prepared," he said.

Lila didn't argue. She kissed the top of his head and went to bed, leaving him alone with the glow of the screen and the quiet hum of the refrigerator. The house ticked around him — pipes cooling, the settling creak of old joists. Every small sound was a question his mind tried to answer before it was asked.

Tomorrow was the interview. The one he'd spent three weeks rehearsing for, the one that could change everything or change nothing. Both possibilities sat in his chest like stones.

He turned off the phone and stepped outside. The porch was cold, the boards rough under his socks. Above the neighbors' roofline, the sky was cluttered with stars he rarely noticed. He sat on the top step and pressed his palms flat against the wood, feeling the grain, the slight dampness of evening dew.

A sound reached him. Faint, rhythmic. He held still.

Across the street, in the Okafors' yard, a woman he'd never seen before was pushing a child on a tire swing. Back and forth, slow and steady, the rope creaking against the branch. The child wasn't laughing or shouting. She was just swinging, head tilted back, watching the same sky Gil had only just looked up at.

The woman hummed something low. Gil couldn't name the tune, but it settled into the air the way wood smoke settles — unhurried, finding its own level. There was no performance in it. She hummed the way someone breathes — because the body knows to.

He realized his hands had unclenched. His shoulders had dropped an inch. The list of things that could go wrong tomorrow still existed, somewhere behind his sternum, but it had moved slightly to the side, the way furniture shifts when someone opens a window and a breeze passes through.

The child on the swing pointed up at something — a star, a plane, a satellite. The woman looked where she pointed and nodded, as if the child had said something worth agreeing with.

Gil looked up too.

Whatever the child had found, he couldn't identify it. But the looking itself felt like enough — three people tilted toward the same dark sky, each one finding something worth noticing in all that wide, uncharted space.

The humming continued. The swing creaked. Somewhere down the block a dog barked once and stopped, satisfied with its own announcement.

Gil sat on the porch for another ten minutes, not checking anything. The air smelled like cut grass and the faint iron tang of the garden hose left coiled by the steps. When he finally went inside, he left his phone on the kitchen counter, screen down. He brushed his teeth listening to the silence of the house, and for the first time in weeks, the silence didn't feel like it was waiting for something to go wrong.

It just felt like silence. And that was enough.
