The gloves were in the bottom drawer of the dresser, underneath a stack of dish towels Miriam never used. She had put them there in May, the week after the funeral, and she hadn't moved them since.

Green canvas garden gloves. Dirt still ground into the fingertips. The left thumb had a small tear where a thorn had caught it years ago. The insides smelled like soil and something else — lotion, the almond kind her mother rubbed into her hands every evening.

Her mother's hands had been in those gloves last April. She had been on her knees in the garden, pulling weeds along the back fence, her straw hat sliding off her head, complaining that the mint was taking over again. That was the last time she was well enough to kneel. By June she was in the hospital. By August she was gone.

The garden had gone wild. The mint had overrun the basil. The tomato cage stood empty and rusted. Crabgrass pushed through the stepping stones.

Miriam watered occasionally — a guilty, halfhearted spray from the hose. But she did not weed. She did not plant. The garden was her mother's territory, and entering it felt like trespassing.

One Saturday in October, the light came through the kitchen window in a way that reminded Miriam of mornings when her mother would stand at the sink, filling her watering can, humming something from church. The memory came and went in two seconds, but it left a residue — not sadness exactly, more like weight.

Miriam opened the drawer. She picked up the gloves.

They were stiff. The canvas had dried over the summer, losing its flexibility. She could see the impression of her mother's fingers in the creases — the way the thumb bent, the curl of the palm.

She carried them outside. The October air was cool. The garden looked terrible. Weeds everywhere. Dead stems nobody had trimmed.

Miriam put the gloves on. They were too big. Her fingers didn't reach the ends. Her mother had bigger hands — hands that could turn soil for hours, that could pull a root out of packed clay with one steady yank.

She knelt by the back fence and pulled the first weed. It came out with a small sound, roots trailing dirt. She set it aside and pulled another.

An hour passed. The pile of weeds grew beside her on the path. Her knees ached on the cold ground. Dirt worked into the creases of the gloves — new dirt, on top of old dirt, filling the same patterns her mother's work had made.

She didn't restore the garden. She cleared the fence line and weeded the stepping stones and pulled the dead tomato stems from the cage. It was a start. Maybe that was all it needed to be.

When she finished, Miriam sat on the largest stepping stone and looked at her hands. Her mother's gloves, on her smaller hands, covered in new soil. Her mother was not in them. But something of her mother was — the shape of her grip, the stain of seasons past, the quiet stubbornness of hands that believed things could grow.

She set the gloves on the porch railing to dry in the afternoon sun. The canvas was softer now. More flexible. Used.

She would put them on again tomorrow.
