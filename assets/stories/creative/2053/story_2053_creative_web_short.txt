The gloves were in the bottom drawer of the dresser where Miriam kept things she couldn't wear and couldn't give away.

Garden gloves. Green canvas, dirt still ground into the fingertips. Her mother's hands had been in them last April, pulling weeds along the back fence, the last time she was well enough to kneel.

Miriam hadn't touched them since. She opened the drawer sometimes and looked at them, the way you look at a photograph — not to hold, just to see.

The garden had gone wild. The mint had overtaken the basil. The tomato cage stood empty, rusted at the joints. Weeds grew through the stepping stones.

One Saturday in October, Miriam opened the drawer and picked up the gloves. She held them for a while. They were stiff. The canvas had dried out over the summer. She could see the shape of her mother's fingers in the creases.

She carried them outside and put them on. They were too big. Her mother had larger hands — broad palms, long fingers, the kind of hands that could work soil for hours without tiring.

Miriam knelt by the back fence. She pulled the first weed. Then another. The soil was cold and damp. A worm curled away from the light.

She worked for an hour. The pile of weeds grew beside her. Her knees ached. Her mother's gloves held dirt in the same creases they always had.

When she finished, the garden wasn't restored. But the fence line was clear. Miriam sat on the stepping stone and looked at the green gloves on her hands. Her mother wasn't in them. But something of her mother was — the shape, the soil, the work.

She left the gloves on the porch railing to dry. She would use them again.
