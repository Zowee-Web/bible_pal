Ezra found the puddle on the way home from school, and everything about his afternoon changed.

He almost walked past it. He was looking at his phone — his mom's text said "come straight home, dentist at 4" — and he nearly stepped right through without seeing. But his left foot hit the edge of the water and the cold shot through his shoe and he stopped.

The puddle was enormous. The rain had come down hard during last period, the kind of rain that hammered the classroom windows and made the teacher raise her voice. Now the rain had stopped, the clouds were thinning, and the low spot in the sidewalk near the vacant lot had collected every drop. The puddle stretched from the sidewalk to the curb, maybe six feet across. The water was brown and perfectly still, a mirror reflecting gray sky and the bare branches of the oak tree overhead.

Ezra looked at it. His phone buzzed again in his pocket. Dentist at 4.

He was wearing his good shoes. The new ones. His mom had bought them two weeks ago and had specifically said — in the voice she used when she really meant it — "Do not get these wet."

He looked at his shoes. Clean, white, dry.

He looked at the puddle.

He put his phone in his backpack, zipped it shut, and set the backpack on the dry part of the sidewalk.

Then he stepped in.

The water came over the top of his right shoe and filled it immediately. His sock went cold and heavy. He put his left foot in. Both shoes submerged. The water was maybe two inches deep in the middle, brown and murky, the bottom invisible.

He stood there for a second. The damage was done. His shoes were already soaked.

He stomped.

Water sprayed up his jeans in a satisfying arc. He stomped again, harder. The splash went two feet. He stomped with his other foot — left, right, left — like he was marching in a parade for nobody.

A woman walking a small white dog stopped on the other side of the street and watched. Ezra didn't see her.

He jumped. Both feet together. Knees bent. Full commitment. He launched himself into the center of the puddle and the impact sent up a crown of brown water that rose a foot in the air and came back down on his jeans, his jacket, his backpack strap — everything.

He laughed. Out loud, by himself, standing in a puddle on a Tuesday afternoon. The laugh surprised him. It had been a long week. The math test. The argument with his friend Davis. The dentist appointment looming.

He jumped again. Higher this time. The splash was bigger.

Again. The water was everywhere now.

When his legs got tired, he stood in the middle of the puddle breathing hard. His shoes were destroyed. His socks were cold mush. His jeans were soaked to the knee and spotted up to the thigh. A bead of muddy water ran down his chin.

He looked up. The clouds had broken. A wide stripe of blue sky opened above the oak tree, and late-afternoon sunlight came through and hit the puddle. The water turned gold. The reflection of the bare branches surrounded his feet like roots growing into another world beneath the sidewalk.

He stood in the gold water and breathed.

His phone buzzed in his backpack. He ignored it.

After a minute, he stepped out of the puddle. He picked up his backpack. His shoes squelched with every step — a sound so good he walked slower just to hear it.

He got home twelve minutes before the dentist appointment. His mom met him at the door, looked at his shoes, and closed her eyes.

"Worth it?" she asked.

Ezra grinned. Water pooled on the kitchen floor around his feet.
