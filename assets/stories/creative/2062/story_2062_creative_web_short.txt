Ezra found the puddle on the way home from school.

It was enormous — the biggest puddle he had ever seen. The rain had stopped an hour ago, but the low spot in the sidewalk near the vacant lot had filled up like a lake. The water was brown and still and stretched from the sidewalk all the way to the curb.

Ezra stopped. He looked at the puddle. The puddle looked back — a perfect mirror of gray sky and bare tree branches.

He was wearing his good shoes. His mom had specifically said, "Don't get your good shoes wet."

He looked down at his shoes. He looked at the puddle.

He stepped in.

The water came over the top of his shoe and soaked his sock instantly. Cold. Surprisingly cold. He took another step. Both feet in now. The water was two inches deep in the middle. His shoes made a squelching sound that was deeply satisfying.

He stomped. Water sprayed up his jeans. He stomped again. Harder. The splash went three feet.

A woman walking her dog on the other side of the street watched him. Ezra didn't notice.

He jumped. Both feet together, knees bent, a full commitment to the puddle. The splash was spectacular — a crown of brown water that rose and fell and soaked everything below his knees.

He did it again. And again. Each jump sent water higher.

When he ran out of jumping energy, he stood in the middle of the puddle, breathing hard, soaked from the shins down. His shoes were ruined. His socks were a lost cause.

He didn't care. He looked up at the sky. A patch of blue was breaking through the gray, and the bare branches of the tree reflected in the water around his feet looked like they were growing downward into another world.

He sloshed home. His mom saw his shoes and sighed.

"Worth it?" she asked.

Ezra grinned. Water dripped on the kitchen floor.
