The numbers didn't add up, and Gil Otieno knew exactly why.

He sat at his desk on the fourteenth floor of Harmon & Dwight, staring at a spreadsheet that showed $340,000 in billable hours for a project that had taken roughly half that time. The senior partner, Ed Vicarro, had padded the invoice. Gil had watched him do it from three desks away — copy a column of hours, change the date headers, and send the revised invoice with a confidence that bordered on boredom.

Gil was thirty-one. Eighteen months at the firm. Student loans that arrived like clockwork, a one-bedroom in Bridgeport, and a letter from HR confirming his promotion review in six weeks.

He closed the spreadsheet and opened it again. The numbers hadn't rearranged themselves.

The easy thing was silence. Ed had been at the firm twenty-two years. He brought in clients the way rivers bring water — steadily, without apparent effort. The client was a hospital system that wouldn't audit a legal bill under half a million. Nobody would ever notice.

Gil noticed. He didn't want to, but there it was, stubborn as a stone in his shoe.

He spent two days thinking about it. Four miles on the treadmill. A frozen dinner, slightly burnt. The ceiling fan turning its slow, unhelpful circles. The right thing and the safe thing stood on opposite sides of a very wide room, and Gil was in the middle with his career in his hands.

He called his mother on Wednesday night. She lived in Nairobi and picked up on the second ring.

"Mama, if doing the right thing might cost you everything, do you still do it?"

She was quiet for a moment. "Gilbert. What happened?"

"I can't say yet."

"You already know," she said. "That's why you called."

On Thursday morning, Gil printed the original time logs and the modified invoice. He put them in a manila folder and walked to the office of the managing partner, Diane Chao. Her door was open. She was drinking tea from a ceramic mug with a chipped handle.

"Do you have a minute?" Gil said.

Diane looked at the folder in his hand. She set down the mug. "Close the door."

He placed the folder on her desk. She opened it, scanned the first page, flipped to the second, and stopped. Her hand went very still on the paper.

"You understand what happens next."

"Yes."

"Ed has been here twenty-two years. He's on the management committee."

"I know."

"And your review is in November."

"I know that too."

She closed the folder and placed both hands flat on top of it. "I'll need a few days. Don't discuss this with anyone."

Gil walked back to his desk. His hands were steady. His stomach was not. A colleague waved from across the floor and he waved back — a normal gesture on a morning that didn't feel normal.

He sat down and opened the next file. A contract review. A research memo. Nothing heroic. He did the work carefully, the way he always did, because the only thing he could control now was the task directly in front of him.

Outside, the October sun hit the glass and turned the whole floor amber. His phone buzzed with a text from his mother. Just three words: I am proud.

He set the phone face-down on his desk. He ate lunch — turkey on wheat, nothing remarkable. Then he kept working. The afternoon was long and ordinary, and he was grateful for every unremarkable minute of it.
