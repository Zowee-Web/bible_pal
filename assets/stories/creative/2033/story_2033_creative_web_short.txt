The numbers didn't add up, and Gil knew exactly why.

He sat at his desk on the fourteenth floor of Harmon & Dwight, staring at a spreadsheet that showed $340,000 in billable hours for a project that had taken half that time. The senior partner, Ed Vicarro, had padded the invoice. Gil had watched him do it — copy a column, change the dates, send it to the client with a confidence that bordered on boredom.

Gil was thirty-one. Eighteen months at the firm. Student loans, a one-bedroom lease, and a promotion review in six weeks.

The easy thing was silence. Ed was untouchable. The client was a hospital system that wouldn't audit a legal bill under half a million. Nobody would ever notice.

Gil noticed.

He spent two days thinking about it. Treadmill. Frozen dinner. Ceiling fan turning its slow, unhelpful circles. The right thing and the safe thing stood on opposite sides of a very wide room, and Gil was in the middle with his career in his hands.

On Thursday morning, he printed the original time logs and the modified invoice. He put them in a manila folder and walked to the office of the managing partner, Diane Chao.

Diane looked at the folder. She looked at Gil.

"You understand what happens next."

"Yes."

"Ed has been here twenty-two years."

"I know."

She closed the folder. "I'll need a few days."

Gil walked back to his desk. His hands were steady. His stomach was not. He opened the next file in his queue — a contract review, a research memo, nothing heroic. He did it carefully, because the only thing he could control now was the work in front of him.

Outside, the October sun hit the windows and turned the whole floor amber. His phone buzzed with a text from his mother. Just three words: I am proud.

He finished the memo. He ate lunch. Then he kept working.
