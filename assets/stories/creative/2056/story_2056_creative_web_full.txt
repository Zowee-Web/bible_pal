Mabel stood outside the door of Room 14 and counted the tiles on the hallway floor. Twenty-two from the water fountain to the classroom door. She counted them once. Then again. Then a third time, just to be sure.

Inside the classroom, chairs scraped against the floor. Kids talked and laughed. Someone dropped a pencil case and everything scattered. It sounded like a room full of people who already knew each other.

It was Mabel's first day. Her family had moved two states away over the summer. New house. New town. New school. Her old friends were back in Ohio, in classrooms she could still picture if she closed her eyes — the blue lockers, the gym with the squeaky floor, Mrs. Donnelly's room with the poster of the solar system.

Her mother had hugged her that morning at the front entrance. "You'll make friends fast. You always do."

Mabel had nodded, but her stomach didn't agree.

She put her hand on the door handle. It was cold. She pushed.

The room went quiet for exactly one second — the way a room does when the door opens and everyone looks to see who it is — and then the noise rushed back in like water filling a hole.

A teacher with curly brown hair and round glasses stood at the whiteboard. She turned and smiled. "You must be Mabel. Welcome. There's a seat for you by the window, third row."

Mabel walked between the desks. She could feel people looking. Not mean looks — just curious, the way you look at something new. She sat down at the desk by the window and put her backpack on the floor. She folded her hands on the desk and stared at the whiteboard.

The girl sitting next to her had red sneakers with white laces and a brown ponytail that swung when she moved. She was bent over her notebook, drawing something in the bottom corner with a green marker. Mabel glanced over. It was a cat. A fat cat with enormous round eyes and a tiny triangle nose.

"That's a good cat," Mabel said. She hadn't meant to speak. The sentence just walked out of her mouth on its own.

The girl looked up. She had freckles and a gap between her front teeth. "Thanks. His name is Beans." She turned the notebook so Mabel could see the full drawing. "I draw him on literally everything. My mom says it's becoming a situation."

"A situation?"

"Like, she found Beans on the electric bill. And the grocery list. And the back of a birthday card for my grandma." The girl shrugged. "Grandma thought it was great."

Mabel felt something loosen in her chest. Just a little. "I had a cat named Soup," she said.

The girl's eyebrows shot up. "Soup?"

"He was orange. Like tomato soup."

"That is the best name I've ever heard for a cat." The girl stuck out her hand. "I'm Vera."

"Mabel."

They shook hands. Vera's palm was smudged with green marker.

The teacher started talking about fractions. Mabel opened her notebook to the first page. She picked up her pencil and started writing the date.

Something caught her eye. On the bottom corner of her notebook — while Mabel had been looking at the whiteboard — Vera had drawn a tiny orange cat with round eyes and a triangle nose. Underneath it, in small green letters, she had written: SOUP.

Mabel looked at Vera. Vera was staring straight ahead at the whiteboard, but the corner of her mouth was turned up.

Mabel put her pencil down. She looked at the little orange cat on her notebook. Her stomach had stopped hurting.
