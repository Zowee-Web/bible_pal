Marta painted a tree.

It wasn't a good tree. She knew that before she finished it. The trunk leaned sideways like it was tired of standing, and the leaves were too big — fat green circles that looked more like balloons than leaves. She'd accidentally mixed the green into the brown, so the whole middle part turned the color of mud.

She painted it anyway.

She used the fat brush for the sky and the thin brush for the grass. She used her finger for the sun because the yellow paint was almost gone and she had to squeeze the tube flat to get anything out.

When it was finished she held it up with both hands. Water dripped down the bottom edge onto her shoe.

"Time to clean up," said Ms. Rashid.

The paintings went on the drying rack. Then into cubbies. Then into backpacks. Marta folded hers once and pushed it down next to her lunch box.

On the bus ride home she didn't look at it.

At home Grandpa was sitting at the kitchen table with his coffee. He had his reading glasses on and was doing the crossword puzzle with a short pencil. The kitchen smelled like toast.

"How was today?" he asked without looking up.

"Fine."

Marta set her backpack on the chair. The painting was sticking out the top, one corner bent.

Grandpa noticed. He always noticed small things — a new scratch on her knee, a missing button, a bent corner.

"What's that?"

"Nothing. Just a painting."

He pulled it free with two fingers. He unfolded it slowly and held it away from his face the way he held the newspaper when the print was small.

"That's a tree," he said.

"It's not very good," Marta said. "The middle's all muddy."

Grandpa didn't answer right away. He tilted the painting toward the window. He looked at the leaning trunk and the balloon leaves and the finger-smeared sun with its smudgy edges.

"The sun is warm," he said quietly. "I can feel it on this tree."

Marta looked at him. He wasn't smiling the way adults smile when they're being nice about something bad. He was just looking.

He stood up from the table. His chair scraped the floor. He opened the junk drawer — the one with the tape and the scissors and the batteries that might be dead — and found the magnet shaped like a strawberry.

He pressed the painting flat against the refrigerator door with both hands. He smoothed the fold crease with his thumb, running it back and forth until the paper lay even. He stuck the strawberry magnet right at the top center.

Then he stepped back.

The painting hung on the white door, still a little damp at the edges. Light from the window above the sink fell across it, and the yellow sun Marta had pressed there with her finger seemed to glow.

Grandpa sat back down. He picked up his pencil and went back to his crossword.

Marta got a glass from the cabinet. She opened the fridge to pour some milk. The painting fluttered against her hand in the cool air that spilled out. She poured the milk. She closed the door carefully.

The painting settled flat again, held fast by the small red strawberry.

The mud-colored middle caught the afternoon light. The leaning tree stood exactly as she'd made it, and it stayed.
