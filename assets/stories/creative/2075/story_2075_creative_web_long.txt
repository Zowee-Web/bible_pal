Marta painted a tree.

It wasn't a good tree. She knew that before she even finished it. The trunk leaned sideways like it was tired of standing up, and the leaves were too big — fat green circles that looked more like balloons than anything that grew on a branch. Somewhere in the middle she'd mixed the green brush into the brown paint by accident, and the whole center of the painting turned the color of mud.

She almost threw it away.

But the paper was already wet, and the sky was already blue, and she'd already spent the whole art period on it. So she kept going.

She used the fat brush for the sky and the thin brush for the grass. She used her finger for the sun because the yellow paint was almost gone, the tube squeezed flat, and she had to press hard just to get a smear of color on her fingertip.

The sun came out round and messy with a fingerprint swirl in the middle.

"Time to clean up," said Ms. Rashid.

Marta held the painting up with both hands. Water dripped from the bottom edge onto her shoe. Around her, other kids carried their paintings to the drying rack. She saw Elena's dolphin — sleek and silver-blue. She saw Jordan's painting of his house with every window perfectly square.

Marta put her tree on the rack at the far end.

After it dried, she folded it once and pushed it into her backpack next to her lunch box. On the bus ride home she didn't look at it. She looked out the window at real trees, the kind with trunks that stood straight and leaves that were the right size.

At home Grandpa was sitting at the kitchen table.

He had his coffee in the blue mug and his reading glasses low on his nose. He was doing the crossword puzzle with a short pencil, the eraser worn down to a nub. The kitchen smelled like toast and the little bit of cinnamon he always shook on top.

"How was today?" he asked without looking up.

"Fine."

Marta set her backpack on the chair. The painting was sticking out the top, one corner bent from where she'd crammed it in.

Grandpa noticed. He always noticed small things. A new scratch on her knee. A missing button. The way she said "fine" when she meant something else.

"What's that poking out?"

"Nothing. Just a painting from art."

He set his pencil down. He pulled the painting free with two fingers, carefully, the way you'd pull a letter from an envelope. He unfolded it. The crease ran right through the middle of the trunk.

He held it away from his face — the way he held the newspaper when the print was too small.

"That's a tree," he said.

"It's not very good," Marta said. She sat down across from him. "The middle got all muddy. And the leaves are wrong."

Grandpa didn't answer right away. He tilted the painting toward the window where the late afternoon light came in above the sink. He looked at the leaning trunk. He looked at the balloon leaves. He looked at the smeared yellow sun with its fingerprint center.

"The sun," he said. "It's warm. I can feel it coming right off the paper."

Marta looked at him. She looked at his eyes behind his glasses.

He wasn't smiling the way grown-ups smile when they're trying to be nice about something that isn't very good. That smile she knew — the tight one, the quick one, the one that came with "Great job, sweetie" and then they moved on.

Grandpa wasn't doing that. He was just looking at her painting. Really looking, the way he looked at his crossword when he was thinking about a word.

"And this part here," he said, tapping the muddy middle. "That's the shadow under the branches. Where it's cool."

Marta hadn't meant it to be a shadow. But she looked where his finger was, and she could see it — a dark cool spot under the big balloon leaves where someone might sit on a hot day.

Grandpa stood up. His chair scraped the kitchen floor. He walked to the counter and opened the junk drawer — the one with the tape and the scissors and the batteries that might be dead and the rubber bands and the take-out menus from places that might have closed.

He found the magnet. The one shaped like a strawberry, red and shiny, that had been in that drawer for as long as Marta could remember.

He walked to the refrigerator. He pressed the painting flat against the white door with both hands. He smoothed the fold crease with his thumb, running it back and forth, back and forth, until the paper lay as flat as it could. He placed the strawberry magnet at the top center, right above the blue sky.

Then he stepped back.

The painting hung on the refrigerator door. It was still a little damp at the edges. The late sun from the window fell across it, and the yellow smear Marta had put there with her finger seemed to catch the real light and hold it.

The leaning tree. The too-big leaves. The mud-shadow underneath.

Grandpa sat back down. He picked up his short pencil. He filled in a crossword square — just one letter — and took a sip of coffee.

Marta sat at the table for a minute. She looked at the painting on the fridge from across the room. It looked different there. Held up. Flat and bright against the white door with the strawberry at the top like a little red crown.

She got up to get some milk. She took a glass from the cabinet, the short one she always used. She opened the refrigerator door and cool air spilled out. The painting fluttered against her hand.

She poured the milk. She put the carton back on the shelf. She closed the door carefully, with both hands, so it wouldn't slam.

The painting settled flat against the door again. The strawberry magnet held it firm.

Outside the window, the real sun was going down behind the real trees in the backyard. Inside, the painted sun stayed right where Marta had pressed it — bright and messy and warm, held up for anyone who walked into the kitchen to see.
