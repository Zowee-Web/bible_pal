Rafe pulled his boots on by the back door and walked across the yard to the chicken coop. Morning chores were his job every day during summer — water for the six chickens, feed and fresh hay for the goat named Pepper, and eggs collected in the wire basket his grandfather had bent by hand years ago.

The morning was already warm. Dew sat heavy on the grass, and the air smelled like cut hay from the field across the road. Rafe liked mornings best. Everything felt possible when the day was new and the sun was still low.

He was filling Pepper's water trough when he noticed his neighbor, Mr. Hadley, standing in his own yard on the other side of the fence. Mr. Hadley was old — older than Rafe's grandfather had been — and thin, with white hair that stuck up in the back like he'd slept on it wrong. He had a garden that used to be the nicest one on the whole road. People would slow their cars to look at it. Rafe's mother said Mr. Hadley's tomatoes won the county fair three years running.

But this summer, the garden looked tired and sad. Weeds pushed up through the tomato rows. The bean poles leaned sideways, their strings loose and tangled. The zucchini plants had gone yellow at the edges.

"Morning, Mr. Hadley," Rafe called.

The old man turned slowly. "Morning, Rafe." His voice was scratchy and low, like a gate that needed oiling.

Rafe set his basket on the fence post and leaned his arms on the top rail. "Your sunflowers are getting real tall."

Mr. Hadley looked over his shoulder at the two sunflowers growing by his back porch. They were tall, almost to the rain gutter. But their big yellow heads drooped down toward the ground, and their broad leaves had brown, curling edges.

"They need water," Mr. Hadley said. He sighed, and it was the kind of sigh that sounded like it came from somewhere deep and heavy. "Everything needs water. The whole garden does. I just can't keep up the way I used to. My knees don't bend right anymore, and the hose is heavier than it used to be."

He said it matter-of-factly, not looking for pity. But Rafe could see it in the way Mr. Hadley looked at his garden — the way you look at something you love that's slipping away from you.

Rafe looked at the weedy rows. He looked at Mr. Hadley's hands on the fence, rough and spotted with age, the knuckles swollen. Then he looked at his own hands, brown from the sun and still dirty from chicken feed.

"I could help," Rafe said. "After my chores are done. I'm real good at weeding — my mom says I'm the fastest weeder in the county."

Mr. Hadley's eyebrows went up. "That so?"

"Well." Rafe grinned. "Second fastest. She's first. But she's had more practice."

A small sound came from Mr. Hadley's throat. It might have been a laugh. It had been a while since Rafe had heard him laugh.

"I wouldn't want to trouble you," Mr. Hadley said.

"It's not trouble. Pepper can wait an extra hour for her second breakfast."

After the chickens were fed, the eggs were collected — four brown ones and one speckled — and Pepper had fresh water and a scratch behind her ears, Rafe climbed through the gap in the fence where a rail had fallen down.

He started with the tomato rows. The weeds came up easy because the soil was soft from last week's rain. He worked on his hands and knees, pulling chickweed and clover and the long, stubborn roots of dandelions. After the weeding, he straightened the bean poles and retied the strings. Then he uncoiled Mr. Hadley's hose and carried water to every row, soaking the soil until it turned dark and rich.

Mr. Hadley sat on his back porch step and watched. Sometimes he'd call out, "Those peppers in the corner could use a drink too," or "Mind that basil — it's delicate." His voice got stronger as the morning went on. More certain.

When Rafe finished, he stood up and stretched his back. His knees were muddy and his shirt was soaked through with sweat. The garden looked different now — the rows neat and clean, the soil dark with water, the plants standing up a little taller like they were relieved.

Mr. Hadley came down the steps slowly, holding the railing. He stood at the edge of his garden and looked at it for a long time without speaking. When he turned to Rafe, his eyes were bright and wet behind his glasses.

"Looks like a proper garden again," he said quietly.

"It was always a proper garden," Rafe said. "It just needed a little help. Everybody does sometimes."

Mr. Hadley nodded. He reached into a paper bag sitting on the porch and held out a peach, perfectly ripe, its skin blushed red and gold.

"From my tree out front," he said. "Best one on the branch. I was saving it."

Rafe took it. "Thank you, sir."

He bit into it on the walk home, climbing back through the fence gap with juice running down his chin and dripping onto his muddy boots. It was the sweetest, most perfect thing he had tasted all summer — warm from the sun and so ripe the flavor filled his whole mouth.

He looked back through the fence. Mr. Hadley was standing in his garden now, bending slowly to touch the top of a tomato plant. Checking on it. Talking to it, maybe.

The garden was still old and a little ragged. But it was cared for again, and that made all the difference.
