Rafe pulled his boots on by the back door and walked across the yard to the chicken coop. Morning chores were his job — water for the chickens, feed for the goat, eggs collected in the wire basket his grandfather had bent by hand.

He was filling the water trough when he noticed his neighbor, Mr. Hadley, standing on the other side of the fence. Mr. Hadley was old and thin and had a garden that used to be the nicest one on the road. But this summer it looked tired. Weeds pushed through the tomato rows. The bean poles leaned sideways.

"Morning, Mr. Hadley," Rafe called.

"Morning, Rafe." His voice was scratchy and low.

Rafe leaned on the fence. "Your sunflowers are getting tall."

Mr. Hadley looked at the two sunflowers by his porch. They were tall — almost to the gutter. But their heads drooped and their leaves had brown edges.

"They need water," Mr. Hadley said. He sighed. "Everything needs water. I just can't keep up like I used to. My knees don't bend right anymore."

He said it matter-of-factly. But Rafe could see it in the way Mr. Hadley looked at his garden — the way you look at something you love that's slipping away.

Rafe looked at the weedy rows. He looked at Mr. Hadley's hands on the fence, rough and spotted with age. Then he looked at his own hands, brown from the sun.

"I could help," Rafe said. "After my chores. I'm good at weeding. My mom says I'm the fastest weeder in the county."

Mr. Hadley's eyebrows went up. "That so?"

"Well. Second fastest. She's first."

A small sound came from Mr. Hadley's throat. It might have been a laugh.

After the chickens were fed and the goat had fresh water, Rafe climbed through the fence gap. He pulled weeds from the tomato rows on his hands and knees. He straightened the bean poles and retied the strings. He carried bucket after bucket of water from the spigot, soaking every row until the soil turned dark.

Mr. Hadley sat on his porch step and watched. Sometimes he called out directions — "Those peppers need a drink too" or "Mind the basil, it's delicate." His voice got stronger as the morning went on.

When Rafe finished, the garden looked different. The rows were neat, the soil dark with water, the plants standing taller. Mr. Hadley came down the steps slowly and stood at the edge. His eyes were bright and wet.

"Looks like a proper garden again," he said quietly.

"It was always a proper garden," Rafe said. "It just needed a little help."

Mr. Hadley reached into a paper bag and held out a peach, perfectly ripe.

Rafe bit into it on the walk home. Juice ran down his chin and dripped onto his muddy boots. It was the sweetest thing he'd tasted all summer.
