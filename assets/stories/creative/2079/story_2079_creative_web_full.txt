Mara's legs hurt before they even reached the trailhead.

They had driven two hours to get here. She had slept in the car with her cheek pressed against the window, and when her dad woke her up, the mountain filled the whole windshield. It looked like it went up forever.

"Ready?" her dad asked.

"I guess," she said.

The first part wasn't bad. The trail wound through pine trees and the air smelled green and sharp. Mara kicked pinecones as she walked. A squirrel chattered at her from a branch and she chattered back.

But then the trail tilted upward.

Mara's calves started to burn. Her backpack straps dug into her shoulders. She could feel every ounce of the water bottle and the sandwiches and the extra jacket her dad had made her carry.

"Dad," she said. "This is really hard."

"Yeah," he said. "It is."

They kept walking. The pines gave way to smaller, scrubby trees. The dirt turned to gravel. Mara's boots slid on loose rock and she caught herself with her hands, scraping her left palm.

She looked at the pink scratch and her eyes burned.

"I can't do this," she said. She sat down right in the middle of the trail. A fly buzzed near her ear. "My legs are done."

Her dad sat down next to her. He pulled out the water and they both drank. He didn't tell her to get up. He didn't say she could do it. He just sat there, stretching out his own legs like they hurt too.

They watched a cloud move across the sun. The shadow rolled over the mountainside like a blanket being pulled across a bed.

"I don't think I can make it," Mara said quietly.

Her dad looked at her. "Maybe not," he said. "But we could try the next bend."

Just the next bend. Mara could picture that. Not the top. Not the whole rest of the trail. Just around the next corner.

She got up.

The next bend showed her a view of the valley — green and brown squares of farmland far below, a ribbon of road with tiny cars. She stood there and looked.

"One more bend?" her dad asked.

She nodded.

They went bend by bend. Each one showed her something new. A cluster of purple wildflowers growing out of bare rock. A creek so small it was just a dark line of wet across the path. An old tree, twisted and silver, still standing with a few green needles at its crown.

Mara's legs shook. Her breath came in short, sharp pulls. She stopped counting bends.

The trail flattened. The wind hit her face, cool and open. She looked up.

There was sky in every direction.

She was at the top.

The world spread out below her — wide and gold and quiet. She could see the parking lot where they'd started, impossibly far down, impossibly small. Hawks rode the air below the ridge.

Her dad put his hand on her shoulder. They stood there, the two of them, with the wind pressing against their jackets and the sun warm on their faces.

Mara sat down on a flat rock at the summit. She unlaced her boots and let her tired feet rest against the cool stone. A ladybug landed on her knee and she watched it walk to the edge and fly.
