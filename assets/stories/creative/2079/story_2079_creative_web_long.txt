Mara did not want to go on the hike.

She said so at breakfast. She said so in the car. She said so in the parking lot while her dad tightened her bootlaces and adjusted her backpack straps.

"Noted," her dad said. He handed her a water bottle. "Drink before we start."

The mountain stood above the parking lot, enormous and brown and green, its peak hidden by a band of white cloud. A wooden sign at the trailhead said the summit was four and a half miles away. Mara didn't know exactly how far four and a half miles was, but she knew it was far.

They started walking.

The first mile was shaded. Tall ponderosa pines lined both sides of the trail, and the ground was soft with orange needles. Mara liked this part. The air smelled like vanilla and sap. A woodpecker knocked somewhere high above them, and she tried to spot it but couldn't.

"Not so bad, right?" her dad said.

Mara shrugged. She wasn't going to admit anything yet.

Then the trail went up.

Not gently. Not gradually. It went up the way stairs go up, except the stairs were made of rocks and roots and red clay that crumbled under her boots. Mara grabbed a tree trunk to pull herself over a steep section. Bark pressed rough lines into her palms.

Her breathing changed. It got loud.

"Dad," she said after ten minutes. "My legs hurt."

"Mine too," he said. He was breathing hard as well. She could see sweat on the back of his neck.

They kept going. The trees shrank. The shade disappeared. The sun fell on Mara's shoulders and the back of her pack like a warm hand pushing her down. She drank from her water bottle and the water was already warm.

A lizard sat on a rock and watched them pass. Its tiny body pulsed with each breath.

Mara's calves burned. Her thighs burned. Her ankles ached where the boots pressed against them. Every step up felt like her backpack got heavier.

She stopped.

"I can't," she said.

Her dad turned around. Sweat dripped from his chin.

"Okay," he said. "Let's stop."

They sat on a boulder beside the trail. Mara pulled her knees up and put her forehead against them. She could feel her heartbeat in her ears. A grasshopper landed on the rock next to her hand, its legs bent at sharp angles.

"How far have we gone?" she asked without lifting her head.

"About a mile and a half."

Three more miles to the top. Three more miles of this. Mara's throat tightened. She felt the stinging behind her eyes that meant tears were close.

"I want to go back," she whispered.

Her dad didn't answer right away. He ate a handful of trail mix, chewing slowly. He offered her some. She took a few raisins and an almond.

"You know what," he said. "Let's just try the next switchback."

Mara looked at the trail. She could see where it turned, maybe fifty steps away. Just fifty steps.

She stood up. Her legs protested. She ignored them.

Fifty steps. She counted each one. At the turn, she could see down into the valley for the first time. Green and brown patches of farmland. A road like a gray thread. Tiny buildings.

"One more?" her dad asked.

She almost said no. But the view pulled at her. She wanted to see what the next turn would show.

"Fine," she said. "One more."

The next switchback revealed a cluster of purple wildflowers growing straight out of a crack in the rock. No soil that Mara could see. Just stone and flowers, bright in the sun.

She touched one petal. It was soft as felt.

"One more," she said, this time before her dad could ask.

They went bend by bend. Mara's world shrank to the small, close things. The color of the dirt changing from red to gray. A beetle crawling across the path, shiny as a button. The sound of wind moving through dry grass. Her own breath. Her own boots. Left, right, left.

Her legs shook on the steep parts. She slipped once and caught herself on her hands. A small scrape opened on her right palm and she wiped it on her shorts and kept going.

The trees disappeared entirely. Only low bushes and rock. The wind got stronger. Mara could feel it pushing against her chest when she faced uphill.

She stopped looking ahead. She just walked.

Then her dad's hand touched her shoulder.

"Mara," he said.

She looked up.

The trail was flat. No, not flat — there was no more trail. She was standing on open rock, and the sky surrounded her on every side. The cloud that had hidden the peak from the parking lot was below her now, a white blanket draped across the mountain's shoulder.

She was above it.

The valley opened in every direction. She could see the parking lot, so small it looked like a gray postage stamp. She could see the river she hadn't known was there, winding silver through the farmland. She could see other mountains, blue and hazy, standing in a long row at the edge of the world.

The wind pressed against her face, cool and steady.

Mara sat down. Right there on the rock at the top. She unlaced her boots and pulled them off and let her sore feet rest on the cool stone. Her socks had a hole in the left toe.

Her dad sat beside her. He pulled out the sandwiches. They ate without talking. The bread was a little squished. The cheese was warm and soft. It was the best sandwich Mara had ever tasted.

A hawk circled below the ridge, riding the wind without moving its wings.

Mara watched it for a long time. Her legs still ached. Her palm still stung where she'd scraped it. The wind moved through her hair and dried the sweat on her forehead.

She leaned against her dad's arm. He leaned back. The sun was warm on their faces, and the whole wide world spread out beneath them, quiet and gold and open, all the way to the edge of sight.
