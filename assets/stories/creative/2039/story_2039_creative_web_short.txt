Ilene carried her tiredness into the rest stop off I-78 the way you carry a stone in your pocket — always pressing against you, even when you forget the word for what it is.

She was driving home from her mother's assisted living facility. Three hours each way, every other Sunday. Her mother didn't always know who she was anymore, and Ilene had learned to sit with that — pour the tea, tell the stories, not correct the confusion. It was a particular kind of tiredness. Not the body. The self.

She bought coffee from the vending machine and sat at a bolted-down table. The coffee was terrible. She drank it anyway.

At the next table, a man sat with his head in his hands. Rumpled suit, tie loosened, jacket creased from hours in a driver's seat. His briefcase was open, papers fanning out. He wasn't reading them.

They didn't speak. Fifteen feet apart in the fluorescent hum of a rest stop that smelled like floor cleaner and burnt coffee, each holding a different kind of heavy.

Ilene stood to throw away her cup. As she passed, she noticed a sheet of paper had slid to the floor. She picked it up and set it on his briefcase.

He looked up. Red-rimmed eyes. "Thank you."

"Sure."

That was all. She walked to her car. He stayed at his table.

But something shifted as she merged onto the highway. The stone was still there, but lighter by a fraction — the weight of a single sheet of paper, maybe. As if the smallest gesture had redistributed something she couldn't name.

She didn't know the man felt it too. That he sat up straighter after she left. Gathered his papers. Drove the remaining sixty miles to his apartment and, instead of collapsing on the couch, opened the kitchen window and let the evening air in.

Neither could have explained it. A piece of paper. A two-word exchange. The presence of another tired person in a place where tired people stop.

Sometimes that's all rest requires — not sleep, not escape, but the quiet evidence that you're not the only one carrying something heavy through an ordinary Sunday.
