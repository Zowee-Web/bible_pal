Fatigue has a weight that doesn't show up on a scale, and Ilene Garza carried it into the rest stop off I-78 the way you carry a stone in your pocket — always pressing, even when you forget the word for what it is.

She was driving home from her mother's assisted living facility. Three hours there, three hours back, every other Sunday. Some Sundays her mother knew her. She'd say "Tell me about the garden" and they'd talk the way they always had. Other Sundays, her mother looked at her with polite confusion and said, "Are you from the church?"

Today had been a church Sunday. Ilene had poured the tea, told the stories, smiled at the right moments, and driven away with a tiredness that had nothing to do with the body. It was the self that was tired — the part of her that kept showing up for a woman who couldn't remember her name.

She bought coffee from the vending machine and sat at one of the bolted-down tables. The coffee was terrible. She drank it anyway, for the warmth.

At the next table, a man sat with his head in his hands. Charcoal suit, tie loosened, briefcase open with papers fanning out. He wasn't reading them. He was staring at the table surface, at the bolts that held it to the floor, at nothing.

They didn't speak. They sat fifteen feet apart in the fluorescent hum of a building designed for passing through. The air smelled like floor cleaner and burnt coffee.

After a few minutes, Ilene noticed one of his papers had slid to the floor. She picked it up on her way to the trash can and set it on the edge of his briefcase.

He looked up. His eyes were red-rimmed. He was younger than she'd assumed — maybe forty. The suit aged him.

"Thank you," he said. His voice was hoarse.

"Sure," Ilene said.

She walked to the parking lot. The afternoon light was softer than expected, the sun turning the parked cars amber. She sat behind the wheel and felt something shift. The stone was still there, but lighter by a fraction — the weight of a single sheet of paper, maybe. As if the smallest gesture had redistributed something she couldn't name.

She didn't know that the man felt it too.

His name was Kenji. He was driving home from a meeting where he'd been told his position was being eliminated. Twelve weeks. He'd sat in the conference room, nodded, shaken hands, and driven to the rest stop because he couldn't face the apartment yet.

When Ilene bent down and picked up his paper without being asked, without reading it, something in him rearranged. Not the paper — a benefits summary he'd memorized. The act. The quiet willingness to set right something that belonged to a stranger.

After she left, Kenji sat up straighter. He gathered his papers, closed his briefcase, and walked to his car with a posture slightly different from the one he'd arrived with. He drove sixty miles to his apartment and, instead of collapsing on the couch, opened the kitchen window. The evening air came in, carrying the smell of someone's cooking and the sound of children on the street below.

Neither of them could have explained what happened. A piece of paper. A two-word exchange. Two people carrying different loads through the same ordinary Sunday.

But weariness responds to witnesses. Not to solutions. Just to the presence of another person who is also carrying something and still moving forward. They drove home slightly less heavy, carrying each other's rest without knowing it, the way two candles in the same room share light without negotiating which flame came first.
