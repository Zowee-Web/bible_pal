Hannah Hope stood at the back of the line and counted the kids ahead of her. Fourteen. Her number was fifteen.

The auditorium at Maplewood Elementary smelled like floor wax and old curtains. Rows and rows of parents filled folding chairs that stretched all the way to the back wall and then curved around the sides. Some held phones up to record. Some whispered behind their programs. A baby fussed somewhere near the double doors, and a father bounced it gently on his knee.

The stage was wide and bare except for a single microphone on a silver stand, a long table where three judges sat with papers and pencils, and a row of sixteen chairs where the spellers waited. Hannah was in the last chair. She could feel the wood through her dress.

A boy named Marcus stepped up to the microphone first. He was tall and wore a blue tie.

"Your word is 'necessary,'" the head judge said.

Marcus spelled it right, no hesitation. Everyone clapped. He walked back to his seat and let out a long breath that puffed his cheeks.

Hannah's stomach turned.

She pressed her fingers against the index card in her pocket. She had written her name on it that morning, just to practice holding something steady. Her letters had come out wobbly. Her mom had taped the card to the fridge next to a grocery list and said, "For when you get home."

The second speller stood. A girl with red shoes.

"Your word is 'dolphin.'"

She got it right. More clapping.

Hannah watched each kid rise, walk to the microphone, and stand under the bright lights. Some of them rocked on their heels. Some of them asked the judge to repeat the word twice. One boy asked for the word in a sentence, and the judge said, "The climate in the desert is very dry." The boy nodded and spelled "climate" like he was reciting his own phone number.

Then a girl named Priya stepped up. Her word was "rhythm."

"R-H-Y-T-H-M," Priya said. Then she stopped. "Wait. R-H-Y —" She shook her head.

A small bell rang. The sound was thin and final.

"I'm sorry," the judge said. "That is incorrect."

Priya walked off the stage with her chin tucked down. Her mother met her at the side steps and put a hand on her back. They disappeared through a door near the water fountains. Hannah watched them go and felt her own throat tighten.

Nine kids left. Then seven.

Hannah closed her eyes. She thought about her kitchen table at home, the one with the scratch across the middle where her brother had dragged a toy truck years ago. Every night after dinner, her dad cleared the plates and set a list of words between them. He would say a word. She would spell it out loud. When she got one right, he would nod once and move to the next word. When she got one wrong, he would just say, "Try it again, Hannah."

That was all. No frowning. No sighing. No throwing his hands up. Just try it again.

She had gotten "courage" wrong three times last Tuesday. The first time she put the "a" before the "r." The second time she forgot the "e" at the end. The third time she mixed up the "o" and the "u." Each time her dad waited with his hands flat on the table. Each time she tried again. On the fourth try, every letter landed in the right place, and he tapped the table once with his knuckle. Just once.

Five kids left.

Her hands were cold. She rubbed them together and tucked them under her arms. The chair felt harder now.

Three kids left. A girl with braids got "beautiful" right. A boy with glasses missed "separate," and the bell rang again. He walked off slowly.

Two kids. Then one. A boy named David spelled "adventure" and sat down.

"Number fifteen," the judge called.

Hannah stood. Her knees felt strange, like they belonged to someone else. She walked across the stage. Her shoes — the black ones her mom had polished that morning — tapped on the wood floor, and the sound bounced up into the high ceiling where metal beams crossed back and forth.

The microphone was thin and silver, taller than she expected. A woman in a black shirt reached over and lowered it. Hannah wrapped her fingers around the cool metal stand. It was smooth and slightly damp from the kid before her.

She could see the whole auditorium now. Hundreds of faces stared back at her. The lights were warm and white and bright, and beyond them the exit signs glowed red at every door. She could see the clock on the back wall. She could see the banner that read "34th Annual Maplewood Spelling Bee" in green and gold letters.

She found her dad in the fourth row. He was sitting between her mom and her little brother, who was drawing on the back of a program. Her dad's hands were folded in his lap. He didn't wave. He didn't mouth any words. He didn't hold up a phone. He just looked at her the way he looked at her across the kitchen table every evening — steady, quiet, there.

"Your word is 'courage,'" the judge said.

Hannah breathed in. The air tasted like dust and warm stage lights and something sweet drifting from the bake sale table out in the hallway.

She leaned toward the microphone.

"Courage," she repeated. Her voice came out small but clear through the speakers mounted on the walls. She could hear it bounce off the back of the room and return to her like a soft echo.

"C," she said.

She paused.

"O-U-R-A-G-E."

She swallowed. "Courage."

The judge looked down at his paper. He adjusted his glasses with one finger and traced a line of text. The woman beside him leaned over to check.

A long second passed. Someone coughed near the doors. The baby had stopped fussing.

"That is correct."

The audience clapped. The sound filled the room from the floor to the ceiling beams, rolling forward and folding back on itself. Hannah stepped away from the microphone. She turned toward her chair.

Her hands were still shaking. She could feel her pulse in her fingertips. But as she walked back across the wide stage, she held her hands open at her sides, palms turned up, the way you might stand in a backyard after a long dry spell — face lifted, arms loose, feeling the first drops of rain land one by one in the cup of each hand.
