"That's not how you peel an orange," Nico said.

His grandfather looked down at the fruit in his hands, turning it slowly. He'd been pulling the rind off in tiny, careful pieces, making a pile of orange fragments on the porch railing.

"I've been peeling oranges for seventy-one years," his grandfather said mildly.

"You're doing it wrong, though. Watch." Nico dug his thumb into the navel, hooked under the rind, and pulled the entire skin off in one long spiral. He held up the peel. "One piece. Eight seconds."

His grandfather examined the spiral. "Very efficient."

"Yours takes forever."

"Yes." The old man pulled another small piece free and added it to his pile. The scent of citrus oil hung between them — sharp, sweet, alive. "But mine smells like this the whole time."

Nico paused. He looked at the tiny fragments on the railing, each one catching the afternoon light. Each had released its own small burst of scent. His grandfather's method turned one orange into a five-minute experience. His own had been over before it started.

He sat on the porch step. The wood was warm. The afternoon stretched out lazy and unhurried. Bees worked the jasmine along the fence. A cardinal landed on the birdbath and left immediately, too dignified to share it with the sparrows.

His grandfather's hands continued their slow work. Pinch, pull, place. His fingers were thick and spotted with age, but they moved with a precision that suggested thousands of oranges, each given the same patient attention.

"Grandpa?"

"Hmm."

"Do you do everything this slow?"

The old man laughed — deep and unguarded, the kind that uses the whole chest. "Not everything. I eat cake quite fast. Ask your grandmother."

Nico grinned. Something in that laugh cracked open a door he hadn't known was closed. He pulled out another orange. Felt the dimpled skin, the weight of it.

He pressed his thumb against the rind and pulled off one small, fragrant piece. Then another.

His grandfather didn't comment. He just kept peeling while the jasmine hummed with bees and the porch shadow moved an inch across the boards and the afternoon took its own sweet time becoming evening.

The quiet between them wasn't empty. It was full — the way a kitchen is full of dinner even before anyone sits down. Two people peeling oranges on a porch. That was more than enough.

When they'd finished, they ate slowly, juice running down their fingers, seeds spit into the grass. The pile of rinds on the railing was enormous — a fragrant mountain of small curved pieces, each one proof that someone had taken their time.

"Okay," Nico said, licking his thumb. "Maybe your way is better."

His grandfather shook his head. "It's not better. It's just longer. And sometimes longer is where the sweetness hides."

He handed Nico another orange. Nico took it, turned it once, and began — slowly, piece by piece — to peel.
