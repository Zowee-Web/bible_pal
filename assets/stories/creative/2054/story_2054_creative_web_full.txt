"You ever eat a mango on a Tuesday?" Curtis asked.

Rodney looked up from the engine block. He had a socket wrench in his right hand and grease up to his elbows. "What?"

"A mango. Specifically on a Tuesday. Have you done it?"

"Curtis. I'm trying to get this head gasket off."

"Just answer the question."

Rodney sighed. He and Curtis had been working on this Buick for three Saturdays. Curtis had a theory that the car could be saved. Rodney had a theory that it was a waste of time, but Curtis was his brother-in-law and the garage was Curtis's, and Rodney didn't have anywhere else to be since Diane left.

"No," Rodney said. "I have never eaten a mango on a Tuesday."

"That's the problem."

Curtis set down his wrench, wiped his hands on a rag, and walked to the cooler by the garage door — the old white cooler with the broken latch that Curtis kept stocked with water and whatever fruit was on sale at the market. He came back holding a mango. He pulled a folding knife from his pocket, opened it, and cut the mango in half right there over the engine block. Juice ran down his wrist and dripped onto the intake manifold.

"Curtis. That's going to get sticky."

"It'll burn off." He held out half the mango. "Eat."

Rodney stared at him. Then he set the socket wrench on the fender, wiped his hands on his coveralls, and took the fruit.

He bit into it. The mango was perfectly ripe — bright, sweet, cold from the cooler. Juice ran into his beard and down the front of his shirt. He didn't care.

"See?" Curtis said, chewing his half. "A mango on a Tuesday. Different."

"It's the same mango it'd be on a Wednesday."

"No. Wednesday mangoes are regular. Tuesday mangoes are earned. You got through Monday. The week is still ahead. A Tuesday mango says: you're here, and it's sweet."

Rodney looked at him. Curtis was completely serious. He was also dripping mango juice onto his sneakers.

"That's the dumbest thing you've ever said," Rodney said.

"Top five, maybe."

They stood in the garage eating mango halves while the Buick sat between them with its guts exposed. The box fan in the corner turned its head slowly left and right, pushing warm August air around. A radio on the shelf above the drill press played something Rodney's father would have known the words to.

Curtis finished his half. He eyed the trash can six feet away. "Watch this." He tossed the mango skin in a high arc. It dropped clean into the can without touching the rim.

He raised both hands above his head.

"You practiced that," Rodney said.

"Every Tuesday."

Rodney laughed. It came out of his chest before he could stop it — a real laugh, the kind that opens your ribs and makes your eyes water. The sound bounced off the cinder block walls and the concrete floor and the hood of the dead Buick.

Curtis grinned. He picked up his wrench. "Let's get that gasket off."

Rodney wiped the mango juice from his beard with the back of his hand. He picked up the socket wrench. The radio played. The fan turned. The garage smelled like fruit and engine grease and a Tuesday that had turned out better than either of them expected.
