"You ever eat a mango on a Tuesday?" Curtis asked.

Rodney looked up from the engine block. "What?"

"A mango. On a Tuesday. It's different."

"It's not different."

"It is. I'll prove it." Curtis set down his wrench and walked to the cooler by the garage door. He came back with a mango, pulled a knife from his pocket, and cut it right there over the engine block. Juice ran down his wrist.

He handed half to Rodney.

Rodney wiped his hands on his coveralls and took it. He bit into the fruit. Juice ran into his beard. The mango was perfectly ripe — sweet and bright and cold from the cooler.

"See?" Curtis said.

"It's a good mango."

"On a Tuesday."

"Fine. On a Tuesday."

They stood in the garage eating mango halves while the engine sat open between them. The fan in the corner pushed warm air around. A radio played something old and scratchy from the shelf above the drill press. Outside, the afternoon sun hit the parking lot and threw a stripe of light across the oil-stained floor.

Curtis finished his half and tossed the skin into the trash can from six feet away. It went in clean. He raised both hands like he'd just sunk a three-pointer.

Rodney shook his head. "You practiced that."

"Every Tuesday."

Rodney laughed. It came up from his chest and filled the garage — a sound the cinder block walls hadn't heard in months. Not since before the diagnosis. Not since the waiting rooms and the quiet drives home. But here it was, pulled back into the world by a mango and a perfect throw.

Curtis grinned. The radio played. The engine waited.
