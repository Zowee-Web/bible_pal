Marcus never asked for the soup.

That was the thing Ray thought about sometimes, driving home in the dark with two empty thermoses rattling on the passenger seat. In twelve weeks the man had never once asked.

They worked a medical office building on the east side of town, a sixteen-week steel and concrete frame going up floor by floor against the November sky. Ray operated the boom lift. Marcus set rebar on the second deck, his hands quick and sure, his movements careful in a way that suggested he'd done this work a long time. They didn't talk much. A nod at the trailer in the mornings. A word about wind speed or the crane schedule. That was enough between them.

It started in September.

Marcus had come on the crew with clean paperwork and a good reference from a shop in Tennessee. The foreman put him on rebar and forgot about him, which was a compliment in this trade. Ray noticed him the second week — the way Marcus sat apart at lunch with nothing in front of him. No cooler. No bag. Once, Ray watched him count coins at the roach coach, study the prices on the handwritten board, and put the coins back in his pocket.

That evening Ray told Colleen about it while she stood at the stove.

"How much soup does a thermos hold?" she asked.

"About two bowls."

She pulled a second pot from the cabinet. "I'll make extra."

The next morning Ray climbed out of his truck at six-fifteen carrying two thermoses. The red one was his. The silver one he set on the plywood stack where Marcus always sat. He didn't say anything. Marcus looked at the thermos, looked at Ray, and unscrewed the cap. Steam rose into the cold air. He drank.

That became the rhythm. Every day, two thermoses. Colleen rotated through a quiet catalog — chicken and rice on Mondays, black bean with cumin, beef vegetable with barley, lentil on the weeks she went to the market early. Thursdays it was almost always potato soup, thick and pale with green onion, because the first Thursday she'd made it, Ray came home and said, "He finished every drop of that one."

She had never met Marcus. She knew only what Ray carried home in small observations. The thin coat. The early arrivals. The way he worked without stopping, as though stillness cost him something.

The crew noticed. Darren leaned against the boom lift one afternoon and squinted at Ray.

"You his mama now?"

"My wife makes extra," Ray said.

Darren shrugged and let it go.

December came hard. Frost on the rebar at dawn, ice in the puddles around the port-a-johns. The crew moved slower, hunched inside layers. Marcus still arrived first, breath visible, boots laced tight.

One morning Ray found him standing by the plywood stack, holding something. A small paper bag. He set it down when he saw Ray approach.

"For your wife," Marcus said. His voice was low and rough, like a man not used to using it.

Ray opened the bag. Inside was a jar of honey, the kind sold at the weekend stands near the highway, hand-labeled in black marker. Wildflower.

"Tell her thank you," Marcus said. He looked at the ground when he said it.

Ray nodded. He set the silver thermos in its usual place. Marcus picked it up and unscrewed the cap. The steam rose and vanished in the gray air.

That evening Ray set the honey on the counter beside the stove. Colleen turned it in her hands, reading the label. She didn't say anything for a moment.

Then she reached for the pot and began Thursday's soup.
