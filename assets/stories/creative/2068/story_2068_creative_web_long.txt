Marcus never asked for the soup.

That was the thing Ray turned over in his mind sometimes, driving home in the dark with two empty thermoses rattling on the passenger seat. Twelve weeks now, and the man had never once asked. Never mentioned it to anyone on the crew. Never made it into something it would have to become if words got attached to it.

They worked a medical office building on the east side of town, a sixteen-week steel and concrete frame going up floor by floor against the sky. The general contractor was out of Little Rock. The steel crew was local. Ray operated the boom lift, ferrying materials and men to the upper decks where the wind came steady off the flats. Marcus set rebar on the second level, his hands quick and sure, his movements deliberate in a way that suggested he'd learned this work a long time ago and in a place where mistakes were not forgiven.

They didn't talk much during the day. A nod near the job trailer at six-fifteen. A word about the crane schedule or whether the concrete pour would hold. That was enough between them. Some men needed more. Ray and Marcus were not those men.

It started the second week of September.

Marcus had come on the crew with clean paperwork and a reference from a fabrication shop in Knoxville. The foreman, a thick-armed man named Guillermo, put him on rebar and moved on. Being forgotten by Guillermo meant your work was good. Marcus's work was good.

Ray noticed him at lunch. The crew scattered across the site at noon — some to the roach coach that parked on the access road, some to their trucks with coolers, a few to the shade of the half-finished stairwell with sandwiches from the gas station across the highway. Marcus sat on a stack of plywood near the south elevation. He sat with nothing in front of him. No cooler. No bag. His hands rested on his knees. He looked out at the traffic on the access road like a man watching a river pass.

One afternoon Ray saw him walk to the roach coach. He stood behind Darren and two of the pipefitters. When he reached the window he pulled coins from his front pocket and counted them in his palm, studying the handwritten menu board wired to the side of the truck. Tacos three dollars. Burrito four. Water one-fifty. He looked at his palm. He put the coins back and walked away. He didn't look at anyone as he returned to the plywood.

That evening Ray stood in the kitchen doorway while Colleen diced onions.

"There's a guy on the crew," he said.

She looked up.

"He doesn't eat lunch. I don't think he eats much at all."

Colleen set the knife down. "How much soup does a thermos hold?"

"About two bowls."

She opened the lower cabinet and pulled out the large pot, the one she used for holidays. "I'll make extra."

Ray brought two thermoses the next morning. The red one was his, dented on the side from the time it rolled off the dash. The silver one was new — Colleen had driven to the hardware store the night before and bought it. He set the silver thermos on the plywood where Marcus sat every day. He didn't say anything. He turned and walked to the boom lift.

At noon Marcus came to the plywood and found the thermos standing there, warm to the touch. He looked across the site. Ray was sitting on his truck's tailgate sixty yards away, pouring from the red thermos into its cap. Marcus unscrewed the silver lid. Steam rose into the September air, carrying the smell of chicken and rice and something sharp like lemon.

He drank.

The next day Ray set the thermos down again. And the next. And every day after that.

Colleen kept a quiet rotation. She didn't write it down, but Ray could tell she held a system in her head. Mondays were chicken and rice. Tuesdays she made a black bean soup with cumin and a little heat. Wednesdays varied — sometimes beef vegetable with barley, sometimes tomato with herbs from the pots on the back porch. Thursdays were potato, thick and pale, with flecks of green onion floating on the surface. Fridays she made lentil, because lentils were cheap and the pot stretched further.

She had never met Marcus. She knew only what Ray brought home — small observations offered while she stirred. The coat that was too thin for the season. The boots held together with electrical tape around the left sole. The way he arrived before anyone, as though he had nowhere better to be, or nowhere else at all.

"Does he have family?" she asked once.

"I don't know."

"Does he say anything when you give it to him?"

"I don't give it to him. I just set it there."

She nodded. She understood the difference.

The crew noticed eventually. Darren, who welded headers on the third deck and talked enough for three men, leaned against the boom lift one afternoon in October.

"What's with the thermos thing?"

"What thermos thing?"

"You bring that guy soup every day. I've seen it."

"My wife makes extra."

Darren studied him. "Every day though."

Ray checked the hydraulic gauge. "She likes to cook."

Darren let it go. He was a loud man but not an unkind one.

November came and the temperature dropped. The crew wore double layers. Frost formed on the rebar overnight and had to burn off before the ironworkers could grip it safely. Marcus still arrived first, breath visible in the dark, a knit cap pulled low over his ears. He worked without stopping. Ray watched from the lift and thought that Marcus moved like a man for whom stillness was expensive, like something would catch up to him if he ever stood still long enough.

The soup got thicker as the weather turned. Colleen added potatoes to things that hadn't needed potatoes. She bought bones from the butcher and simmered stock overnight, and the kitchen smelled like someone's grandmother's house when Ray came down the stairs at five in the morning. The silver thermos stood by the door beside his boots, full and hot.

One morning in early December, Ray arrived to find Marcus already at the plywood stack. He was holding something. A small brown paper bag, folded over at the top.

"For your wife," Marcus said.

His voice was low and rough, the voice of a man who used it sparingly and with effort, like drawing water from a deep well.

Ray took the bag. Inside was a jar of honey. The kind sold at roadside stands — local wildflower, the label handwritten in black marker. The glass was cold from the morning air.

"Tell her thank you," Marcus said. He looked at the ground. He looked at the sky. He looked anywhere but at Ray, and Ray understood that this was the most the man could give, and that giving it had cost him something.

"I'll tell her."

Ray set the silver thermos in its place. Marcus picked it up and held it between both palms. The warmth moved into his hands. He unscrewed the cap and the steam rose and dissolved against the gray December sky, smelling of potato and green onion.

That evening Ray set the jar on the counter next to the stove. Colleen picked it up and turned it slowly, reading the handwritten label. She held it for a long moment. The kitchen was quiet except for the tick of the cooling oven.

She set the jar down, reached for the large pot, and began Thursday's soup.
