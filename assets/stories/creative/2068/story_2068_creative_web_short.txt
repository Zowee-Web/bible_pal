Marcus never asked for the soup.

That was the thing about it. Every morning Ray climbed out of his truck at six-fifteen with two thermoses instead of one. The red one was his. The silver one was for Marcus.

The crew worked a medical office building on the east side of town, twelve weeks into a sixteen-week frame. Ray operated the boom lift. Marcus set rebar on the second deck, his hands quick and steady despite the cold. They didn't talk much during the day. A nod at the trailer. A word about the weather. That was enough.

At lunch, Ray would set the silver thermos on the stack of plywood where Marcus sat. No explanation. Marcus would unscrew the cap and pour the soup into it and drink it slowly while the other men ate sandwiches from gas stations.

Some days it was chicken and rice. Some days black bean with cumin. Thursdays it was almost always potato, thick and pale with flecks of green onion.

The other men on the crew noticed. Darren asked once. "You his mama now?"

Ray shrugged. "My wife makes extra."

That was partly true. His wife Colleen did make the soup, but she made it on purpose, every night, portioned into the silver thermos and set by the door beside his boots. She had never met Marcus. She knew only what Ray had told her — that the man ate nothing at lunch, that his coat was too thin for November, that he never complained about either thing.

Marcus had come on the crew in September. He didn't say where he'd been before. His paperwork was clean and his work was good and that was all the foreman needed. Ray saw the way Marcus counted coins at the roach coach one morning, then put them back in his pocket and walked away.

The next day there were two thermoses.

Now it was December. Frost on the rebar at dawn. Marcus arrived early as always, breath visible, boots laced tight. He found the silver thermos already sitting on the plywood, steam curling from its mouth into the gray air.

He wrapped both hands around it. He closed his eyes. He drank.
