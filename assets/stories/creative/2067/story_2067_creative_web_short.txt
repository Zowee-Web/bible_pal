The substitute arrived on a Tuesday in March. She carried a canvas bag and wore reading glasses on a chain around her neck. Room 14 smelled like dry-erase markers and floor wax.

Marcus sat in the third row, hood up, arms crossed. He had not turned in homework in six weeks. His regular teacher, Mrs. Alderman, had stopped asking.

The substitute wrote her name on the board. Mrs. Ellison. Her handwriting was neat and unhurried.

She taught the lesson from the textbook. Fractions. Nothing remarkable. She called on students by pointing gently with an open hand, not a finger. When Marcus didn't answer, she waited three seconds, then moved on without comment.

At the end of class, she handed back a worksheet Mrs. Alderman had left in the grading pile. Marcus got a forty-one. He crumpled the paper under his desk.

The bell rang. Students pushed toward the door.

"Marcus."

He stopped. Turned halfway.

Mrs. Ellison was erasing the board with long, even strokes. She didn't look at him.

"You have a careful mind," she said. "I noticed it in the way you read the problems. You read every word before you pick up your pencil."

He stood in the doorway. The hallway noise swelled behind him.

"That's not common," she said. She set the eraser down and looked at him. "Don't let anyone rush you."

He left. She was gone the next day. Mrs. Alderman returned with her coffee thermos and her routines.

Marcus did not become a different student that week. He did not suddenly turn in every assignment. In April he failed another test.

But in May he stayed after school and asked Mrs. Alderman to explain denominators again. She looked surprised. She pulled a chair beside her desk and showed him, step by step. He read each problem fully before he touched his pencil to the page.

Seven years later, Marcus sat in a college library with a engineering textbook open in front of him. The radiator clicked against the wall. Snow fell past the tall windows.

He read every word of the problem. Then he picked up his pencil.
