Harriet had been fighting the garden hose for twenty minutes when she finally dropped it in the mud and sat down on the porch step.

The hose was fifteen years old. It had a permanent kink three feet from the nozzle that returned the moment she let go. The nozzle leaked from a crack in the plastic collar. And the brass connection to the spigot was stripped — every time she turned the water on, a fine spray shot sideways and soaked her arm.

She had tried pliers. She had tried duct tape. She had tried Teflon tape the way the man at the hardware store told her. None of it worked.

The garden needed water. The tomatoes had gone three days without any, their leaves curled inward and yellowing. The basil looked defeated. The pepper plants drooped.

Harriet sat on the porch step with mud on her knees and her left shoe soaked through. She was tired. Not just of the hose. Of the house, which needed a new roof she couldn't afford. Of the car, which made a sound on left turns her mechanic couldn't identify. Of the insurance forms piled on the kitchen table. Of being the only person who could fix things, now that Martin was gone.

The hose lay in the grass like a green snake that had won its fight.

Her neighbor, Arthur Potts, appeared at the fence. He was eighty-one. He rested his forearms on the top rail and looked at Harriet on the step. He looked at the hose. He looked at the garden.

"You could use a watering can," he said.

"I have a hose, Arthur."

"You had a hose. What you've got now is a problem with a kink in it." He tilted his head toward the tangled mess. "My watering can holds two gallons. Your garden's maybe ten gallons total. Five trips."

"Five trips is a lot of trips."

"Five trips is five trips. Better than sitting here getting bit by mosquitoes."

Arthur disappeared behind the fence. A minute later he reappeared, carrying a dark green watering can with a wide spout. He set it on the ground on Harriet's side of the fence, handle facing her.

"Bring it back when you're done," he said. He went back inside.

Harriet sat on the step for another minute. The watering can sat in the grass. Two gallons. A wide spout. No nozzle to crack. No threads to strip. No kinks.

She got up. She walked to the spigot and filled the can directly from the tap. Water poured in clean and steady. She carried it to the tomato bed and poured. The water soaked into the cracked soil with a soft hissing sound.

Second trip. The peppers. Third trip. The basil. Fourth. The herbs along the fence. Fifth. The marigolds her daughter had planted in April and asked her to keep alive.

Each trip took ninety seconds. No fighting. No pliers. Just water in a can, carried by hand, poured where it needed to go.

When she finished, Harriet set the watering can by the spigot and sat back down on the porch step. The garden was watered. The soil was dark. A bead of water sat on a tomato leaf, catching the evening light.

She left the hose in the grass. She'd deal with it tomorrow. Or she wouldn't.
