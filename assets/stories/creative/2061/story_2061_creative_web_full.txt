Iris's best friend Kaya was moving to California on Saturday. The truck was already parked in Kaya's driveway — a huge orange moving truck with boxes stacked inside. Iris could see it from her bedroom window, three houses down.

They had known for three weeks. Kaya's mom got a new job. The house was already sold. Kaya's little brother was excited. Kaya was not.

But every time Kaya tried to talk about it, Iris changed the subject. She didn't do it on purpose — not exactly. Her brain put up a wall the second the word California appeared. She'd ask about the math test. She'd bring up the cafeteria switching to square pizza. Anything to keep the conversation away from the thing she couldn't look at directly.

On Friday after school — the last day before Saturday, before the truck, before everything — they walked to Greenfield Park. They didn't plan it. They just turned that direction at the corner and kept walking.

The park was mostly empty. They sat on the swings. The chains were cold through their gloves. They swung slowly, feet dragging through the wood chips, not talking at first.

"I made you something," Kaya said.

She pulled out a bracelet. It was braided from purple and green thread — Iris's two favorite colors — with a small silver bead woven into the middle.

Iris stopped swinging. She took the bracelet and held it in her palm.

"You made this?"

"It took three tries. The first one looked like a worm." Kaya held up her own wrist. She was wearing an identical bracelet, blue and yellow thread. "I made one for me too."

Iris tied it on her left wrist. She had to use her teeth to pull the knot tight. The silver bead rested on top of her wrist bone.

"I don't want you to go," Iris said. The words came out before she could stop them. Her voice was thick.

Kaya kicked at the wood chips. "I don't want to go either."

They sat there. The swings creaked.

"My mom says I'll make new friends," Kaya said. "She says that like it's supposed to help."

"It doesn't help."

"I know."

"Will you call me?" Iris asked.

"Every day."

"You won't call every day."

Kaya looked at her. "Okay, not every day. But a lot. And we can video chat. I'll send you pictures of the lemon tree."

"I don't care about the lemon tree."

"You will when I send you lemons."

Iris almost laughed. The laugh got stuck somewhere between her chest and her throat.

She looked at the bracelet on her wrist. Purple and green. The silver bead. She looked at Kaya — dark curly hair spilling from under her hat, the blue coat she wore every single day, the gap between her front teeth.

"Okay," Iris said.

"Okay what?"

"Okay, send me the lemons."

They walked home the long way — past the library, past the ice cream place closed for winter, past the empty lot where they once found a turtle and carried it to the pond. They took every detour. Every extra block. Every possible way to make the last walk longer than it needed to be.

At Kaya's driveway, they stopped. The orange truck sat half-packed.

"Bye," Kaya said.

"Bye."

Kaya went inside. Iris walked the three houses to her own door, up the stairs, into her room. She sat on the bed and looked out the window at the orange truck.

She touched the bracelet on her wrist. The silver bead was warm now. She left the curtain open.
