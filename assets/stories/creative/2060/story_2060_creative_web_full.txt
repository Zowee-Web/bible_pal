Hazel didn't think much about the lunch her dad packed until the day he forgot.

Every school morning, before Hazel came downstairs, her dad would stand at the kitchen counter and build her lunch. Turkey sandwich on wheat bread — three slices of turkey, one leaf of lettuce, mustard on the bottom piece only, because Hazel didn't like mustard on both sides. A small apple. A bag of pretzels, always the thin twisty ones. And a note.

The note was the thing that made the lunch different from a lunch. Her dad wrote it on a yellow sticky square and stuck it to the inside of the paper bag. Sometimes it said "Have a great day, Haze." Sometimes it said "You're my favorite Hazel (don't tell the other Hazels)." Sometimes it was just a drawing of a smiling sun with sunglasses.

Hazel read the note every day. She usually smiled. She usually stuck it to the table briefly, then peeled it off and threw it away with the bag. She never saved them. They were just there, like the sandwich and the apple. Part of the routine.

On Thursday, her dad's alarm didn't go off. He woke up twenty minutes late, grabbed his keys, kissed Hazel on the head as she was eating cereal, and ran out the door.

Hazel finished her cereal. She picked up her backpack and looked at the counter where the lunch bag always sat.

It wasn't there.

She grabbed a granola bar from the pantry and walked to school.

At lunch, she sat at her usual table. Mika had tomato soup and a roll. June had a bento box. Oliver had two PB&Js and chips.

Hazel had a granola bar and the water fountain.

"That's it?" Mika asked.

"My dad forgot."

"That stinks."

"It's fine." She unwrapped the granola bar. Chocolate chip. It was fine.

But all through lunch, she kept doing something she didn't expect. She kept reaching for the paper bag that wasn't there. Her hand would move to the side — the spot where the bag always sat — and find empty space. Each time, she felt something small and hollow in her chest. Not hunger. Something else.

She missed the sandwich. She missed the pretzels. But mostly she missed the note. The yellow square that she had read a hundred times and thrown away a hundred times without thinking about what it meant: someone stood at a counter every morning and thought about her before the day even started.

That evening, her dad came home with Chinese takeout and an apology. He set the white boxes on the counter and looked at Hazel with his sorry face — eyebrows up, mouth twisted sideways.

"I'm really sorry about lunch."

"It's fine, Dad."

"It's not fine. You had a granola bar for lunch."

"A chocolate chip one."

"That doesn't make it fine."

They ate orange chicken at the kitchen table. Her dad asked about school. Hazel told him about the science quiz and the new kid who could solve a Rubik's cube in forty seconds.

Before bed, Hazel walked past the kitchen. The counter was clean. But sitting on the edge, already made for the next morning, was a paper lunch bag. Her dad had packed it early.

She opened it. Turkey sandwich. Apple. Pretzels. And a yellow sticky note.

"Never again. Love, Dad."

Hazel peeled the note off the bag. She carried it upstairs. She stuck it on the inside of her locker mirror the next morning, next to the small photo of her and her dad at the lake.

She didn't throw it away. She kept every one after that.
