Hazel didn't think much about the lunch her dad packed until the day he forgot.

Every morning, her dad made the same lunch: a turkey sandwich on wheat bread, an apple, a bag of pretzels, and a note. The note was always on a yellow sticky square, stuck to the inside of the bag. Sometimes it said "Have a great day." Sometimes it said "You're my favorite Hazel." Sometimes it was just a smiley face.

Hazel usually read the note, smiled, and threw it away.

On Thursday, her dad overslept. He rushed out the door to work before Hazel was even downstairs. There was no lunch bag on the counter.

She grabbed a granola bar and shoved it in her backpack.

At lunch, she sat at her usual table and opened the granola bar. Her friends unpacked their lunches — sandwiches, thermoses of soup, containers of cut fruit. Hazel had a granola bar and a water fountain.

It wasn't a big deal. People forget things. But Hazel kept reaching for the lunch bag that wasn't there, and every time her hand found empty space, she felt the absence of something she hadn't known she was paying attention to.

The sandwich. The apple. The pretzels.

The note.

That evening, her dad came home with takeout and an apology. "I'm sorry about lunch. Crazy morning."

"It's fine," Hazel said. And it was.

But the next morning, she came downstairs and found the lunch bag on the counter. She opened it. Turkey sandwich. Apple. Pretzels. And a yellow sticky note: "Never again. Love, Dad."

Hazel peeled the note off the bag. She didn't throw it away. She stuck it on the inside of her locker, next to the mirror, where she could see it every time she opened the door.
