The apartment was loud all morning. Isla's baby brother screamed in his high chair because he didn't want applesauce. Her older sister practiced trumpet in the hallway — the same eight notes, over and over, with a squeak on the high one every time. The dishwasher rumbled and clanked like it had swallowed something metal. And outside the kitchen window, a garbage truck beeped as it backed up, slow and endless.

Isla sat at the kitchen table with her cereal going soggy. She covered her ears with her hands. It didn't help much. She could still feel the trumpet notes vibrating in her teeth.

Her mother rushed past with a washcloth, heading for the applesauce disaster. "Isla, honey, eat your breakfast."

But Isla wasn't hungry anymore. She was full of noise. It pressed against the inside of her head like too much air in a balloon.

She slipped away from the table. Nobody noticed. She walked down the hall, past her sister and the terrible trumpet, past the bathroom where the faucet dripped, all the way to the back of the apartment. There was a small door there, painted white, that opened onto the fire escape. Her mother said Isla could sit on the fire escape as long as she didn't climb the ladder.

Isla pushed the door open and stepped outside.

The metal grating was cool under her bare feet, and she could feel the pattern of it pressing into her skin — little diamond shapes, neat and even. The fire escape faced the back of the building, away from the street and the garbage truck and the traffic. Below was a narrow alley with a single tree growing up from a square of dirt in the concrete. The tree was old. Its trunk was thick and dark, and its branches reached wide. The top branches came up to Isla's level, three floors above the ground.

She sat down cross-legged on the grating and leaned her back against the warm brick wall.

It was quiet here. Not perfectly silent — she could hear birds in the tree, and somewhere far away a siren rose and faded. A dog barked once, then stopped. But the sounds were soft and distant, like listening to rain from inside a car.

A breeze came through the alley. It moved through the tree's leaves with a sound like a hundred people whispering kind things all at once. It touched Isla's face and arms, cool and gentle, carrying the smell of green — something alive and growing in the middle of all this concrete and brick.

She watched the leaves move. They were different shades — dark green on top, lighter underneath — and when the breeze turned them, they flickered like tiny flags. Sunlight came through the gaps and made bright spots that danced on the alley floor below.

A sparrow landed on a branch close to her. It was so near she could see its small black eye, round and bright as a bead. It tilted its head to one side, looked right at her, and sang three clear notes. Then it flew away, but the notes stayed in the air for a moment, hanging there like something you could almost touch.

Isla breathed in slowly. Her chest expanded. The air tasted clean and cool.

She breathed out. Her shoulders dropped. She hadn't realized she'd been holding them up near her ears.

She sat there for a long time. She didn't count the minutes. She watched a cloud move across the narrow strip of sky above the alley, changing shape as it went — first like a boat, then like a pillow, then like nothing at all. She listened to the leaves whisper. She felt the sun warm her knees.

Inside the apartment, she could still hear the trumpet start up again. The baby had stopped crying, at least. The garbage truck was gone.

But out here, on the fire escape, everything was different. Everything was still and calm and exactly right. The tree breathed in and out with the wind. The sparrow came back and sang again.

Isla closed her eyes. She let the quiet settle over her like a blanket.

She didn't need to go anywhere. She didn't need to do anything. She just needed this — the leaves, the breeze, the small brave tree growing up through the concrete.

When she finally went back inside, the apartment was still noisy. But Isla carried the stillness with her, tucked away somewhere behind her ribs, warm and safe and hers.
