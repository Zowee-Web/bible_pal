"Does anyone actually read these things?"

Vivian was talking to herself. She was sitting in a folding chair behind a table at the community center, and in front of her were forty copies of the neighborhood newsletter she had written, printed, folded, and stapled by hand.

Nobody had taken one. The event had been going for two hours.

A woman stopped at the table. She was maybe seventy, silver hair, a canvas bag over one shoulder. She picked up a newsletter and read the front page.

"You wrote this?"

"Every month," Vivian said.

The woman sat down in the other folding chair. "The piece about the oak tree on Maple Street. That was you?"

Vivian nodded. She had written about the city's plan to remove a two-hundred-year-old oak to widen the road. She'd interviewed the arborist. She'd measured the trunk. She'd talked to the woman who lived next door and remembered climbing it as a girl.

"That article saved the tree," the woman said. "The city council reversed the decision after it went around. My granddaughter read it to me over the phone."

Vivian blinked. "I didn't know that."

"Well." The woman folded the newsletter and put it in her bag. "Now you do." She stood up. "Keep writing."

After she left, Vivian sat for a while in the folding chair. The community center was loud — kids, music, the smell of grilled corn. Thirty-nine newsletters still sat on the table.

She picked one up and read her own words. They looked different now.
