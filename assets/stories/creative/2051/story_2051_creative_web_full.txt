"Does anyone actually read these things?"

Vivian was talking to herself — a habit she'd had since her husband died and the apartment got too quiet. She was sitting in a folding chair behind a card table at the Holbrook Community Center's summer block party, and spread in front of her were forty copies of the Holbrook Herald — the neighborhood newsletter she had written, edited, printed, folded, and stapled by hand, every single month, for the past three years.

Nobody had taken one. The block party had been going for two hours. People walked past her table on their way to the face-painting station or the grilled corn. A few glanced at the stack. Most didn't.

Vivian was sixty-four. She had been a copy editor at a newspaper for twenty-eight years until the paper closed. After that she worked at a library, then retired. When she started the Herald, she thought of it as a small thing — something to keep her hands and her brain busy. She wrote about potholes and park cleanups and the woman on Cedar Lane who grew the biggest tomatoes in the county.

Nobody had ever told her they read it.

She was considering packing up when a woman stopped at the table. Silver hair, brown skin, a canvas tote bag with a library logo on it. She looked at the stack of newsletters, then picked one up and read the front page.

"You wrote this?"

"Every month," Vivian said.

The woman turned to the inside page. Her eyes moved across the paragraphs. Then she looked up.

"The piece about the oak tree on Maple Street. Last October. That was you?"

Vivian nodded. The oak was two hundred years old — older than the neighborhood, older than the city. The city council had voted to remove it to widen the road by eight feet. Vivian had gone to the tree and measured the trunk with a tape measure — fourteen feet around. She'd interviewed the arborist who said it could live another century. She'd talked to Agnes DuPree, the eighty-three-year-old woman who lived next door and remembered climbing the tree as a girl.

The article ran in the October issue. Vivian had stapled forty copies and left them at the laundromat and the coffee shop and the community center, same as always.

"That article saved the tree," the woman said.

Vivian stared.

"The city council reversed the decision. They redesigned the road to go around it. Three council members cited the article in their statements." The woman sat down in the other folding chair. "My granddaughter lives on Maple. She read the article to me over the phone. She said, 'Grandma, someone actually cared enough to write about our tree.'"

Vivian didn't know what to say. She had never seen a council statement. She didn't know the decision had changed. She had written the article and moved on to the November issue.

"I didn't know any of that," Vivian said.

The woman folded the newsletter and put it in her tote bag. "Well. Now you do." She stood, shouldered the bag. "Keep writing, honey. People are reading."

After the woman left, Vivian sat in the folding chair for a long time. The block party moved around her — kids screaming, a DJ playing too loud, the smell of grilled corn and sunscreen. Thirty-nine newsletters sat on the table, untouched.

Vivian picked one up and read her own words. They looked different now. Not like a hobby or a habit. Like something that had reached further than she knew, into a granddaughter's phone call and a city council meeting and the roots of a two-hundred-year-old oak that was still standing.

She put the newsletter down and straightened the stack. Then she sat up a little straighter herself.
