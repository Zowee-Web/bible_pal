"I can't do it," Wren said, staring up at the high dive.

The ladder reached toward the sky like a metal beanstalk. The diving board at the top looked thin and lonely. The pool below shimmered, impossibly far away. Other kids climbed up and jumped off like it was nothing. Like jumping from that height was as easy as stepping off a curb.

Wren's hands were sweating. She wiped them on her towel.

Her older brother, Caleb, stood beside her with his arms crossed. He didn't push or tease. He just said, "You don't have to."

But Wren wanted to. She had watched all summer from the shallow end, her chin resting on her folded arms at the pool's edge. She had counted the jumps. She had imagined herself up there a hundred times. She had promised herself she would try before August ended.

And today was the last day of August. The pool would close tomorrow for the season.

She grabbed the first rung of the ladder. The metal was warm from the sun. She climbed one step. Then another. Her arms trembled, but they held. She kept her eyes on her hands and didn't look down.

Halfway up, she stopped. She could see the whole pool from here. The blue water sparkled with tiny diamonds of light. Kids splashed and laughed below, their voices small and far away. The lifeguard's whistle glinted in the sun. A row of towels hung on the fence like colorful flags.

"You're doing great," Caleb called up. His voice sounded farther away than she expected.

She kept climbing. The ladder vibrated slightly with each step. At the top, she stepped onto the board. It was rougher than she imagined, like sandpaper under her bare feet. The board stretched out over nothing. The wind blew against her wet swimsuit and raised goosebumps on her arms.

She walked to the edge. Her toes curled around the rough lip.

She looked down. Her stomach flipped like a pancake on a griddle. The water was so far below that the lane lines looked like thin blue ribbons.

She almost turned around. Almost. Her body wanted to retreat, step by step, back down the ladder to the safe, warm concrete.

But she thought about all those days watching from below. Eighty-seven days of summer. Eighty-seven days of wanting. She thought about what it might feel like to fly, even for just one second.

Wren bent her knees. She took a breath that filled her whole chest, all the way down to her belly.

She jumped.

The air rushed past her ears like a wind tunnel. The sky flashed above her, wide and blue. Her arms spread open. For one perfect second, she was weightless.

Then the water swallowed her in one cool, blue gulp.

She sank down, down, into the quiet. The sounds of the pool disappeared. Everything was still and soft.

She kicked to the surface and burst through, gasping and grinning so wide her cheeks hurt.

"I did it!" she shouted. Water streamed down her face.

Caleb was clapping. His grin matched hers. The lifeguard gave her a thumbs-up from his tall white chair. A little girl standing by the ladder looked up at the board and then at Wren, eyes wide.

Wren swam to the side and climbed out. Water dripped from her hair onto the hot concrete, making dark spots that disappeared almost instantly. She stood in the sunshine and looked back up at the high dive.

It didn't look so tall anymore. It was just a board and a ladder and a little bit of sky.

"Want to go again?" Caleb asked.

Wren was already walking toward the ladder.
