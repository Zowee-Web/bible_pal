Peace is not always found in beautiful places. Sometimes it shows up in a laundromat at ten o'clock on a Wednesday night, under fluorescent lights, next to a row of humming dryers.

The woman folding towels wasn't performing mindfulness. She wasn't practicing a technique or following a method. She was simply doing the work in front of her — fully, carefully, with the kind of attention that turns an ordinary task into something that steadies the air around it.

Corinne didn't participate in that peace. She witnessed it. And witnessing was enough. Sometimes the most calming thing in the world is watching someone else be calm — seeing that it's possible, that a person can move through the small necessities of life without hurry or frustration, and that the quiet they carry is available to anyone in the room.

There is an old idea — woven through scripture and ordinary life alike — that peace is less a destination than a way of handling what's in your hands. The towel. The pillowcase. The fitted sheet that won't cooperate. You smooth it, fold it, press the crease, and move on to the next one. Not because the task is important, but because the way you do it shapes the person you become.

The laundromat is still open. The dryers are still turning. And somewhere in the warm hum of ordinary things, there is rest available — if you're willing to sit still long enough to notice it.
