The laundromat on Hess Avenue stayed open until midnight, and by ten o'clock on a Wednesday it was nearly empty.

Corinne sat in a molded plastic chair near the front window, waiting for her last load to dry. The fluorescent lights hummed a note somewhere between B-flat and patience. Two dryers tumbled in slow, overlapping rhythm. The air was warm and smelled like clean cotton and someone else's lavender detergent.

She wasn't in a hurry. Her apartment was four blocks east with nothing waiting except a gray cat named Pencil and a stack of mail she'd already sorted. She had a battered paperback from the library's free shelf, but it lay open on her knee, pages down, forgotten.

She was watching the woman at the folding table.

The woman was maybe seventy. Small and straight-backed, with dark brown skin and hands that moved with the precision of ten thousand repetitions. Navy cardigan buttoned to the collar, house slippers with rubber soles that whispered on the linoleum. She was folding towels.

Not quickly. Not absently. She folded each towel the way a person wraps a gift meant for someone particular. Smoothed the fabric flat with both palms. Matched corner to corner with her fingertips. Pressed the crease with the heel of her hand, firm and slow, then turned and folded again. The stack grew with the quiet certainty of bricks in a well-laid wall.

Corinne had never seen anyone fold laundry like it mattered. Like the towels deserved the full attention of the person handling them.

The woman moved to pillowcases. Same rhythm. Smooth, fold, press, stack. Her face was quiet — not blank, but quiet the way a pond is quiet when the wind has stopped. No tension in her shoulders. No impatience in her hands. She was not waiting for the task to be over. She was in it.

A dryer buzzed three machines down. The woman didn't flinch. She finished the pillowcase in her hands, set it on the stack, then walked to the dryer and opened the door. Steam rose around her fingers. She pulled out a fitted sheet and held it wide between outstretched arms.

Then she began the ancient negotiation of folding a fitted sheet alone. Tuck this corner into that one. Shake. Try again. She was patient with the fabric's resistance, working with it rather than against it.

Corinne realized she'd been holding her breath. She let it out slowly.

The woman glanced up and smiled — small, unhurried, expecting nothing in return. The kind of smile that didn't perform. It just arrived.

"Peaceful in here at night," the woman said.

"It is," Corinne said. "I come late on purpose sometimes."

The woman nodded. "Best hour of my week." She turned back and finished the fold — somehow, impossibly, the fitted sheet came out in a neat rectangle.

They didn't speak again. The woman folded the last of her laundry — cloth napkins, flannel pajamas — and placed everything into a worn canvas bag. She zipped it closed, looped the handle over her shoulder, and walked to the glass door.

The bell chimed once as she left. Through the window, Corinne watched her walk to a blue sedan under the streetlight, moving slowly, in no rush at all.

Corinne sat in the warm quiet. Her dryer was still running. She picked up her book but didn't open it. She listened to the tumble and hum, the small reliable sounds of an ordinary place doing its ordinary work at an hour when most of the city had moved on.

Something she hadn't felt in weeks settled into the center of her chest. Not happiness. Not resolution. Something simpler and older than both.

Rest. Not sleep. Not escape. Just rest — the kind that arrives when you stop reaching for it and let the quiet do what it was already doing.
