Priya woke up grumpy. Rain streaked the window in long, crooked lines. Her favorite cereal was gone — the box sat on the counter, empty except for crumbs. Her little sister, Meera, had used the last of the milk on the kitchen floor, making what she called "a lake for ants."

"This day is already terrible," Priya said, dropping into her chair at the kitchen table.

Her grandmother, who was visiting from out of town, sat across from her drinking chai from a blue cup. She had silver hair pulled back in a braid and hands that moved slowly, like everything deserved patience.

"Hmm," she said. She didn't agree or disagree.

"It really is terrible," Priya repeated, in case Grandma hadn't heard.

"I believe you," her grandmother said. "But tell me three good things."

"There aren't any. The cereal is gone and the milk is on the floor."

"Look harder." She said it gently, like a suggestion.

Priya sighed. A big, dramatic sigh that moved her bangs. She looked around the kitchen. The overhead light was warm and yellow, making everything glow. Rain tapped on the roof in a steady rhythm, almost like fingers drumming a soft song.

"The light is nice," she said reluctantly.

"That's one."

She listened to the rain. It had a pattern — tap-tap-tap, pause, tap-tap. "The rain sounds okay, I guess."

"That's two."

Meera ran through the kitchen wearing mismatched socks — one purple, one green — and giggling at nothing. She stopped and hugged Priya's leg with both arms. Her hair smelled like strawberry shampoo.

Priya's hand went to Meera's head without thinking. She felt the soft curls under her fingers.

"My sister is funny," she said quietly.

"Three." Her grandmother sipped her tea and smiled.

They made toast with honey because there was no cereal. Her mother spread the honey thick, and it dripped over the edges in golden threads. Priya bit in, and the sweetness burst warm across her tongue. She decided toast might actually be better. She would never have known if the cereal hadn't run out.

After breakfast, her grandmother washed the dishes and Priya dried them. They worked side by side at the sink, not talking much. Through the window, the rain slowed. A crow sat on the telephone wire, black and shining, looking perfectly content.

"Grandma?" Priya said, holding a wet plate. "Can we do the three things again at dinner?"

Her grandmother handed her a cup to dry. "We can do it at every meal if you want."

"What about bedtime too?"

"Bedtime too."

The rain stopped by noon. The clouds pulled apart like cotton, and blue sky appeared. Priya went outside and stood on the front porch. The world smelled washed and new.

She found a puddle in the driveway that reflected the clearing sky — a perfect circle of blue on the dark asphalt. She stepped around it carefully so she wouldn't break the reflection. It was too beautiful to step on.

The day that started terrible turned out to be full of small, good things she hadn't noticed at first. They had been there all along, quiet and patient, waiting for her to look.
