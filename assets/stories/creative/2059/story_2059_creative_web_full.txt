Gabe's neighbor Mrs. Flores had a piano that nobody played anymore.

He knew this because he walked past her house every morning on the way to school, and through the front window he could see it — a dark brown upright with a tall stack of sheet music that never moved. The stack had been sitting in the same position since September.

Mrs. Flores used to play every evening. Gabe could hear it from his bedroom — his wall shared a wall with her living room. Between seven and eight o'clock, piano music would come through, sometimes fast and complicated, sometimes slow and sad. He didn't know the names of the songs, but the sound was part of his routine, like brushing his teeth or the train that passed at nine-fifteen.

Then one day the music stopped. No warning. Just silence where the piano used to be.

That was four months ago.

One Saturday morning in January, Gabe was helping his dad carry grocery bags from the car. Mrs. Flores came out her front door to get her mail.

Her hands shook as she pulled the envelopes out. Not a lot — just a tremble. But it was more than cold.

"Mrs. Flores," Gabe said, setting his bag on the trunk, "how come you stopped playing the piano?"

His dad shot him a look — the kind that means that's personal, don't ask. Gabe pretended not to see it.

Mrs. Flores held her mail against her coat. "My hands," she said. She held them up. Her fingers were bent at the middle knuckles, swollen and stiff. "Arthritis. I can't reach the keys the way I used to."

"Can you reach some of the keys?"

"Some. Not enough for the pieces I know."

"But some."

Mrs. Flores tilted her head. She had silver hair and sharp brown eyes. "What are you getting at, young man?"

"I just think some notes are better than no notes."

Mrs. Flores didn't answer. She looked at her hands, then at Gabe. Then she took her mail inside and closed the door.

That evening, Gabe was on his bed doing math homework. The house was quiet. His sister was at a friend's house. His dad was watching TV downstairs with the volume low.

Then he heard it. Through the wall.

A single note. Then silence. Then another note, a little higher. Then silence again.

Then three notes in a row, slow, with long pauses between them. Like someone testing the water with their toes before getting in.

Then a short melody. Five notes, played with one hand, simple and clear. It stopped. It started again. The same five notes. Then six.

It wasn't the music Mrs. Flores used to play — those complicated, beautiful pieces that filled both houses. This was smaller. Simpler. Careful.

But it was music.

Gabe closed his notebook. He pulled his knees up to his chest and leaned against the wall — the wall that connected his room to her living room — and listened.

The notes kept coming. Slow. Patient. One hand finding its way across keys it hadn't touched in four months. Not a performance. Just someone playing what their fingers could still reach.

Gabe sat against the wall until the music stopped. Then he opened his notebook and went back to fractions. Through the wall, he could hear the piano bench creak as Mrs. Flores stood up.
