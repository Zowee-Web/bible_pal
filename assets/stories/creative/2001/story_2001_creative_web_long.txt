Maren's workshop sat at the end of a gravel lane in the village of Carden, tucked between a stone wall and a row of wild hawthorn bushes that bloomed white every spring. The building itself was old — thick oak beams overhead, a floor of packed earth covered in wool scraps and stray threads, and one broad window that let in the morning light from the east. The smell of lanolin and dye hung in the air permanently, soaked into the walls themselves, mixed with the faint sweetness of the dried lavender bundles she kept on a shelf near the door. Her loom dominated the center of the room, a massive frame of ash wood that her grandmother had built decades ago, its heddles worn smooth by generations of hands. Outside, sparrows nested in the hawthorn and filled the mornings with a noise so constant Maren barely heard it anymore.

The village itself was small — a baker, a farrier, a woman who kept bees, and a handful of farmers whose fields stretched toward the river to the south. People came to Maren for blankets, for table runners, for the heavy woolen cloth they used to line winter coats. She had a reputation for precision. Her edges were always clean, her patterns symmetrical, her tension even across every row. Neighbors said you could hold one of Maren's pieces up to a window and not find a single flaw in the weave. She had built that reputation over fifteen years, and she carried it the way some people carry a family name — with pride, and with the quiet terror of losing it.

Three months ago, the trouble started. She'd been working on a commission for a merchant in the next town — a tapestry panel in deep blue and cream, a geometric border surrounding a central motif of interlocking diamonds. She'd drawn the pattern on graph paper, counted every thread, measured her warp with a ruler. But on the fourth day of weaving, she found a section near the center where the threads had somehow shifted. Two cream threads had crossed where they shouldn't have, pulling the diamond slightly out of alignment. She cut back three rows and started again. By evening, the same distortion appeared in a different spot.

She stayed up that night reworking the section by lamplight, her fingers moving carefully, her breath shallow. The oil lamp threw long shadows across the loom, and the room felt smaller in the dark, close and warm. She told herself it was a tension problem — maybe the warp threads weren't weighted evenly, maybe the humidity had changed. She adjusted everything she could think of, re-tied the warp, checked each heddle. When she began again the next morning, the weave held for six rows before a thread snapped near the selvedge edge, and the whole section began to pucker and pull. She pressed her palms flat against the cloth and felt her heartbeat in her fingertips.

Over the following weeks, every project she touched seemed to resist her. A simple striped blanket developed a ripple she couldn't press out. A scarf she was weaving for the baker's daughter kept snagging on a burr in the reed that she couldn't find no matter how many times she ran her fingers along the metal teeth. She began checking her work obsessively — every five rows, then every three, then after each single pass of the shuttle. Her shoulders ached from hunching forward. She started waking before dawn with her jaw clenched, her mind already running through the day's weaving, anticipating problems. The sparrows outside would be singing, and she'd lie in bed with her hands balled into fists, dreading the walk to her workshop.

She tried everything methodical she could think of. She bought new yarn from a different spinner, thinking the fiber might be inconsistent. She borrowed a tension gauge from a weaver two villages over and measured her warp threads one by one. She reorganized her shuttles, her bobbins, her reed hooks. She swept the workshop floor and dusted every surface, thinking maybe debris was catching in the mechanism. None of it helped. Each piece she started would hold together for a while — sometimes a day, sometimes only hours — and then something would slip, something would tangle, and she'd find herself cutting threads and pulling rows apart again, her hands trembling with frustration.

Her neighbor Della, the beekeeper, stopped by one afternoon and found Maren sitting on the floor beside the loom with a pile of unraveled yarn in her lap. The workshop smelled of chamomile tea gone cold. Della didn't say anything at first — she just sat down on the wooden stool by the door and waited. Maren's eyes were red. Her fingernails were bitten short. After a long silence, Maren said, "I can't make it listen to me anymore." Della looked at the loom, at the half-finished cloth still stretched across the frame, and said, "When my bees swarm, I used to chase them. Climbed fences, ran through fields. Lost more hives that way than any other."

"What do you do now?" Maren asked, though her voice was flat, like she didn't expect the answer to matter. Della shrugged and said, "I set out an empty box with a little bit of comb inside, and I sit nearby where they can smell me, and I wait. Sometimes they come. Sometimes they don't. But I stopped losing hives the year I stopped chasing." Maren looked down at the yarn in her hands. It was soft — good wool, well-spun, a shade of grey-blue that reminded her of river stones. Della stood, touched Maren's shoulder briefly, and left without adding anything else.

That evening, Maren sat at her loom but didn't thread it. She just sat on her bench and looked at the frame, at the empty heddles, at the way the last light from the window caught the fine dust floating in the air. She noticed things she hadn't paid attention to in months — the way the ash wood had darkened where her grandmother's hands had gripped it, the small carved mark on the crossbeam that might have been a letter or might have been just a gouge from a tool. The room was quiet except for the sound of wind moving through the hawthorn outside. She put her hands on the breast beam and felt the wood, cool and solid, and she breathed out slowly through her mouth.

The next morning, she warped the loom without a pattern. She chose colors the way she might choose what to eat for breakfast — instinctively, without argument. A warm ochre. A strand of undyed cream. A thin line of deep green that she'd been saving for something special and had never used because she could never find the right design for it. She didn't measure the warp with a ruler. She spaced the threads by feel, by the width of her fingers, letting some sit closer together and others breathe. When she began to weave, she didn't count rows. She watched the cloth form under her hands the way you watch a path appear when you walk through tall grass — not planned, but present.

The shuttle moved, and the threads held. An hour passed. Then two. The ochre and cream made a warmth together that she hadn't predicted, and the green appeared in thin lines that drew the eye forward without demanding attention. When a thread crossed slightly out of the rhythm she'd fallen into, she didn't cut it back. She wove the next row around it, and the small irregularity became part of a texture that made the cloth feel alive under her palm. Her shoulders dropped away from her ears. She noticed the sparrows again.

By afternoon, she had woven more cloth in one sitting than she had in the previous two weeks combined. It wasn't symmetrical. The edges weren't ruler-straight. But when she ran her hand across the surface, the weave was firm and the fabric draped with a weight that felt honest — like something that would keep a person warm without pretending to be anything other than what it was. She cut the piece from the loom and carried it outside, where she draped it over the stone wall and stood back. The breeze moved through it gently, and the colors shifted in the outdoor light, richer than they'd looked inside. Della walked past on her way back from the hives, slowed, and nodded once. Maren folded her arms and stood there for a while, watching the cloth move in the wind, listening to the sparrows, her hands finally still.
