Maren had been weaving since she was nine years old, taught by her grandmother in a kitchen that smelled like cardamom and wood smoke. By thirty-two, she ran a small shop on Alder Street where she sold tapestries and table runners, each one planned down to the last thread. She kept notebooks full of gridded sketches, color codes, tension measurements. Every piece she made began as a blueprint, and the blueprint was law.

But for three weeks now, the loom had been fighting her.

It started with a commission for a wedding tapestry — ivory and gold, a geometric border, clean symmetry. She'd done dozens like it. But on the first attempt, a thread snagged in the heddle and pulled a whole row crooked. She cut it out and started over. The second time, the warp threads slipped their tension overnight, and by morning the foundation had gone slack and wavy. The third attempt held for two days before a knot she couldn't explain appeared in the center, puckering the fabric like a scar.

Maren stopped sleeping well. She'd lie in bed running calculations, checking her memory for mistakes. She started arriving at the shop before dawn, double-checking every component — the tie-ups, the treadles, the reed. Her hands shook when she threaded the shuttle. Her jaw ached from clenching.

Her neighbor Deshi, who ran the ceramics studio next door, brought her tea one afternoon and found her sitting on the floor surrounded by cut threads. "I've re-warped this loom four times," Maren said, not looking up. "Four times, Deshi."

He set the tea beside her and sat down on an overturned crate. "You remember my kiln last spring? Kept cracking everything I put in it."

"You replaced the element."

"After three months of trying to fix the firing schedule. I adjusted the temperature up, down, sideways. Tried different clays. Drove myself half-crazy." He picked up a strand of gold thread from the floor and wound it loosely around his finger. "Turned out the kiln was fine at the temperature it wanted to run. I just needed different clay bodies that worked with it instead of against it."

Maren looked at him. "So your advice is to stop fighting the loom."

"My advice is drink your tea before it gets cold."

She almost smiled.

That evening, after Deshi left, Maren sat at the loom without her notebook. She ran her fingers across the warp threads, just feeling them — their weight, their give. Instead of forcing the tension she'd calculated, she let the threads settle where they naturally held. The spacing wasn't even. Some gaps were wider than others. Her hands itched to correct them.

She picked up the shuttle anyway.

For the first twenty minutes, she kept pausing, wanting to measure, wanting to fix. The pattern forming under her hands wasn't the geometric border she'd planned. The uneven warp created something organic — curves where she'd intended angles, gradients where she'd wanted sharp lines. Twice she almost reached for the scissors.

But the threads weren't snagging. No knots appeared. The fabric grew smoothly under the shuttle's path, row after row, the rhythm steady for the first time in weeks.

Maren wove until the shop windows went dark and the only light came from the lamp clamped to her loom. When she finally stopped and leaned back, the piece on the loom looked nothing like her blueprint. The border undulated like a river current. The gold thread caught the lamplight in irregular bursts instead of the uniform shine she'd designed.

She pressed her palm flat against the fabric. It was tight, sound, whole.

The next morning, she pinned the unfinished piece to the display wall where clients could see it. When the couple came in for their consultation, the bride stopped in the doorway and put her hand over her mouth. "That one," she said. "Can we have something like that?"

Maren pulled out a fresh sheet of paper — blank, no grid — and picked up her pencil. "Tell me about yourselves," she said, and listened.
