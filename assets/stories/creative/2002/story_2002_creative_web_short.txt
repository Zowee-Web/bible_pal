Tomás pulled his fourth batch from the oven at 3:47 a.m. and already knew. The crust was pale, the crumb dense and tight. He pressed his thumb into the center of a loaf and watched the dough barely spring back. Heavy. Like everything else lately.

He'd been compensating for weeks now — kneading longer, adding more yeast, checking the proofing drawer every ten minutes, adjusting temperatures by single degrees. His hands ached from the work. The skin between his fingers had cracked and gone raw from flour and water and the constant washing between batches.

His shop opened at six. Three days this week, customers had said nothing wrong, exactly, but he'd watched them chew slowly, set the bread down, reach for butter they didn't usually need.

At 4:15, he mixed the fifth batch. His shoulders burned. He set the dough in the proofing drawer and stood there, hand on the handle, ready to hover. Then his knees gave a little, just enough. He dragged the flour sack against the wall, sat down on it, and closed his eyes. Not to sleep. Just to stop.

He woke at 5:20 in a panic, stumbling to the drawer.

The dough had doubled. More than doubled. It was pillowy and alive under his fingers, warm with its own quiet energy. When he punched it down, it sighed. He shaped the loaves fast, slid them into the oven, and paced the kitchen floor with his arms crossed tight.

At 5:48 he pulled them out. The crust was the color of dark honey. When he tore one open, steam curled up and the crumb inside was open and airy, full of irregular holes — the kind that only forms when the dough has had time, real time, without anyone pulling and pressing and watching.

Tomás stood at the counter holding half a warm loaf. He ate a piece slowly. It was the best bread he'd made in a month.

He looked at the flour sack against the wall, the dent still pressed into it from his back. Tomorrow night, he'd mix the batch, shape it, and sit down again. The dough didn't need his hands every minute. It just needed him to stop reaching for it.
