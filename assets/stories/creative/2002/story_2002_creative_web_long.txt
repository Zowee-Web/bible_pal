The bakery sat on the corner of Mill Street and Harbour Road, wedged between a locksmith's shop and a place that sold used books. Its front window was small and fogged most mornings, and the sign above the door read "Harlan's" in letters that had once been gold but had faded to a tired bronze. The building itself was old brick, the color of dried clay, and the back alley smelled perpetually of yeast and warm flour. Inside, the ovens were industrial but aging, their dials sometimes sticking, their thermostats drifting by ten degrees one way or another. At four each morning, when the rest of the town was still wrapped in silence, the only light on the block came from the narrow kitchen behind that fogged window.

Harlan Goss had owned the bakery for eleven years, and for eleven years he had worked the night shift alone. The neighboring shops were dark when he arrived each evening around nine, carrying his thermos of black coffee and a canvas bag with a change of clothes. The alley cats knew him by the sound of his boots on the wet pavement. He would unlock the back door, flip on the fluorescents that buzzed and flickered before catching, and tie on his apron in the greenish light. The kitchen was narrow — two long steel counters, a rack of proofing trays, the three ovens along the far wall, and a deep sink that always had a slow drip no plumber could fix.

For most of those eleven years, the rhythm had been manageable. He mixed the doughs by ten, shaped the loaves by midnight, proofed them through the early hours, and baked in shifts until the delivery driver came at five-thirty. But in the last year and a half, things had tightened. A new café chain had opened two blocks east, selling machine-made rolls at half his price, and his wholesale orders had dropped by a third. He had started adding a line of pastries to make up the difference — laminated doughs, braided sweet breads, hand-rolled croissants. Each one added another hour to his night, another set of steps he couldn't skip.

His hands showed the cost. The skin across his knuckles was cracked and dusted white no matter how much he washed. His left shoulder had developed a grinding ache from years of kneading, and he'd started holding it at a slight angle without noticing. Sleep came in broken pieces — four hours after his morning deliveries, sometimes two more in the late afternoon if he could quiet his mind. His wife, Delia, had stopped asking him to come to bed at a normal hour. She left him notes on the kitchen counter instead, small things — the dog had a vet appointment, the gutter needed cleaning, their daughter's school play was on Thursday. He read them standing up, already calculating the next night's dough schedule.

The trouble with the bread started in October. His sourdough, which had always been his signature — a dark, crackling crust with a tender, open crumb — began coming out dense. The loaves looked fine on the outside, but when he cut into them, the interior was tight and gummy, the air pockets small and uniform instead of wild and varied. He adjusted the hydration. He changed the flour. He fed his starter more frequently, carrying the jar home and back like a sick child. Nothing worked. The wholesale clients who still ordered from him started returning loaves, and the café customers who came in at six-thirty began reaching past the sourdough for the rye.

He responded the way he always responded to problems: he worked harder. He started arriving at eight instead of nine. He kneaded longer, shaped more carefully, watched the proofing trays like a man watching a pot that refused to boil. He set alarms on his phone in fifteen-minute intervals so he could monitor the rise, and he stood over the dough in the yellow glow of the proofing lamp, willing it upward. The kitchen, which had once felt like a kind of chapel to him — quiet, warm, purposeful — started to feel like a cell. The dripping sink kept its rhythm. The fluorescents hummed their single note. His coffee went cold on the counter, untouched, because he forgot it was there.

One night in early November, around two in the morning, he dropped a full tray of shaped loaves. They hit the tile floor with a soft, collective thud, and he stood looking down at them for a long time. His arms were shaking, not from the weight but from exhaustion so deep it had settled into his tendons. He picked up the loaves one by one, set them back on the tray, and knew he would have to throw them all out. Flour dust hung in the air. He sat down on the stool by the sink, the one with the wobbly leg, and pressed his palms against his eyes until he saw colors.

That was the night Delia came. She appeared at the back door at two-thirty, wearing her coat over her pajamas, her hair pulled back in a loose knot. She had driven the six blocks because she'd woken and found his side of the bed cold and something in her wouldn't let her go back to sleep. She didn't say much. She looked at the trays, the flour on the floor, the coffee sitting cold, and then she looked at him on the stool. She pulled up a crate and sat beside him. The drip from the sink marked the seconds between them.

"When's the last time you slept through a night?" she asked. He couldn't remember. She didn't push. She sat with him in the buzzing fluorescent quiet, and after a while she said something he almost didn't hear: "The dough needs time to do what it does. Maybe you do too." She squeezed his flour-dusted hand and left, because their daughter was asleep at home and shouldn't wake alone. The back door clicked shut behind her, and the alley was dark and still.

He didn't change anything that night. But the next evening, instead of arriving at eight, he stayed home until ten. He ate dinner at the table with Delia and their daughter, Margot, who told him about a frog she'd found near the school parking lot. He listened to the whole story, including the part about the frog's left eye being slightly bigger than its right. After Margot went to bed, he sat on the porch for twenty minutes and did nothing. The air was cold and smelled like wet leaves and chimney smoke from the neighbor's house. When he finally got to the bakery, he mixed his doughs quickly, shaped them with hands that felt looser than they had in weeks, and then — instead of hovering over the proofing trays — he turned off the overhead light, sat on the stool, and closed his eyes. He set one alarm for four hours later. He let the dough be.

When the alarm woke him, his neck was stiff and the kitchen was dark except for the small light above the ovens. He walked to the proofing trays and pulled back the cloth. The loaves had risen — not just adequately, but fully, generously, their surfaces taut and alive with the slow work of fermentation. He could smell the tang of the starter, sharp and clean. He slid the first tray into the oven and stood listening to the soft creak of the crust beginning to form. When he pulled the bread out forty minutes later and broke open a loaf, the crumb was open and wild again, full of irregular holes, the texture almost creamy against his fingers.

He started leaving space after that. Not every night — some nights the orders were too heavy, or the schedule too tight. But most nights, he rested while the dough rested. He brought a blanket to the bakery and folded it on the shelf beside the flour bags. He stopped setting alarms every fifteen minutes. On the mornings when the delivery driver arrived, Harlan was still tired — that didn't change — but his hands were steady, and the bread was good. Delia's notes on the kitchen counter started to change too. Instead of reminders about appointments and chores, she left him small things — a drawing Margot had made of the bakery, a sprig of rosemary from the garden taped to an index card. One morning he came home and found the porch light on, the door unlocked, and a plate of eggs still warm on the table.
