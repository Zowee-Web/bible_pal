Eli had been baking bread for twenty-three years, and for twenty-three years he had fought the dough like it owed him something. His shop sat on the corner of Mill and Havering in a town small enough that everyone knew when the bread was good and when it wasn't. He arrived at the bakery every night at nine, and by the time the first loaves came out at four in the morning, his forearms ached and his lower back felt like someone had driven a railroad spike through it. His wife, Donna, had stopped asking him to come to bed at a reasonable hour sometime around year eleven.

The trouble started — or maybe it had always been there and just got louder — the spring his hands began to shake. Not a lot. Just enough that he noticed when he was trying to score the tops of the sourdough. He compensated by gripping harder, working faster, checking the proofing drawer every few minutes as if the loaves might escape. He started arriving at eight instead of nine. Then seven-thirty. He mixed and kneaded and shaped and watched, and the bread came out fine but never extraordinary. Dense in the middle sometimes. A little tight in the crumb.

One Tuesday night in April, his body simply quit. He'd been on his feet since seven, and around midnight his vision went swimmy and gray. He sat down on the flour-dusted stool by the proofing station and told himself he'd rest for just a minute. When he opened his eyes, the clock read two-fifteen.

He lurched up, cursing, and yanked open the proofing drawer expecting disaster. What he found instead made him stand very still. The dough had risen past the lip of every banneton, pillowy and alive in a way he hadn't seen in months. Maybe years. He slid the loaves into the oven almost afraid to touch them, as if too much handling might undo whatever had happened while he slept.

At four-thirty, when he pulled them out, the crust crackled and sang against the cooling rack. He broke one open. The crumb was open, airy, threaded with irregular holes that caught the overhead light. He ate a piece standing up, and it tasted the way bread is supposed to taste — wheaty and warm, with a faint sourness that lingered.

He told himself it was a fluke. The next night he pushed through as usual, checking and rechecking, kneading an extra round, hovering. The bread was fine. Acceptable. Nothing more.

Thursday he tried the experiment on purpose. He mixed the dough, shaped it, set it in the proofer, and then he sat on the stool and closed his eyes. His mind screamed at him for the first ten minutes — the hydration was probably off, the temperature might be wrong, the starter had looked sluggish. But his body won the argument. He slept for nearly two hours.

The loaves that came out of the oven Thursday morning were the best he'd baked in a decade. Mrs. Peralta from the flower shop next door bought two and came back for a third before lunch. The minister's husband, who never said much about anything, told Eli it was the finest sourdough he'd ever eaten.

Friday night Eli arrived at his usual time, and after he set the dough, he did something he hadn't done since he was a young man. He dragged the old wool blanket down from the shelf above the sink, folded it on the bench by the window, and lay down. The proofing drawer hummed quietly. The shop smelled like flour and warm wood. Through the window he could hear a dog barking somewhere far off, and the faint sound of a truck on the highway.

He let go of the bread. He let it do what it was always going to do without him standing over it, and he rested.

When the timer finally woke him, he walked to the oven with steady hands.
