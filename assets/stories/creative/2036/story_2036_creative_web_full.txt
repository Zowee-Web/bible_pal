Delia caught the 5:40 bus without checking the schedule. Three years of early shifts at St. Anselm's had written the route into her body like a second pulse. Her feet carried her to the stop on Raleigh Street at 5:37 every weekday, and the bus arrived at 5:41 because the driver, Hector, ran one minute late as a matter of principle.

The bus was half full. Shift workers, mostly. A few college students with hoods up and eyes closed. Delia found a window seat near the back. Inside her bag: clean scrubs, a water bottle, a granola bar she'd eat at the first red light.

The morning was still dark. Streetlights slid past in amber streaks. A man walked a large dog along the sidewalk, both moving with the slow determination of creatures not fully awake.

She ate the granola bar. Oats and honey, the cheap kind. It tasted better than it had any right to. She watched the neighborhoods scroll by — the nail salon, the tire shop, the church on Fifth whose sign always had a new message. This week: YOU ARE HERE. That was all. She liked it.

At Vine Street, an older man boarded with a wooden cane and a paper grocery bag held against his chest. He sat across the aisle and arranged the bag on his knees. A loaf of bread poked out, golden crust visible through the plastic.

"Morning," he said — not to her specifically, but to whoever was listening.

"Morning," Delia said.

He looked out his window. She looked out hers. The bus climbed the bridge approach, and for a few seconds the river below caught the first gray light. Flat and silver, like hammered tin, the bridge supports throwing long shadows across it.

Delia almost missed it. She'd crossed this bridge a thousand times. But today the light hit at a particular angle, and the river looked like something from a painting. She pressed her forehead to the cool glass.

On the other side, the city opened up. The hospital campus, the parking garages, a row of coffee carts setting up. Steam rose from the first cart, white and curling. A woman in a red scarf arranged cups on a folding table with the efficiency of someone who still cared about getting it right.

The bus stopped at Memorial. Delia stepped down. The man with the bread nodded through the window. She nodded back.

Two blocks to the east entrance, badge in hand. The doors parted. The lobby smelled like floor wax and burnt coffee from the nurses' station.

At the elevator bank, she paused. Through the glass doors behind her, the sky was turning the color of a peach. Pink along the horizon, gold where the clouds thinned. She hadn't watched a sunrise in months. Always walking toward something — the elevator, the ward, the next task — and never turning around.

She turned around. Stood in the empty lobby and watched the sky change for thirty seconds. The color deepened, flared, and faded into ordinary daylight. A janitor pushed a mop bucket past. She didn't mind.

Thirty seconds. That was all.

She stepped into the elevator and pressed four. The doors closed. She could feel the warmth of the lobby at her back, the weight of the shift ahead — not heavy, not light, just present.

Delia clipped her badge and opened her first chart. But before she read the first line, she let herself hold it for one more moment. The bus. The bridge. Bread in a paper bag. A church sign that said YOU ARE HERE. A river that caught the light. A stranger who said good morning to nobody in particular.

Gratitude was not a feeling you chased. It was a thing you noticed when you stopped moving long enough to look around.

She looked around. Then she started her shift.
