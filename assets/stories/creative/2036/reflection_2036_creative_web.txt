Delia's journey was six miles on a city bus. No wilderness, no mountain trail, no pilgrimage across ancient ground. Just Raleigh Street to Memorial Avenue, the same route she had traveled a thousand times.

But a journey doesn't need to be new to reveal something. Sometimes the most familiar path is the one most likely to surprise you — precisely because you've stopped expecting anything from it. The river was always there, under the bridge, catching whatever light the sky offered. The sunrise happened every morning whether Delia turned around or not. The man with his bread said good morning to a bus that mostly wasn't listening.

Gratitude, in this story, didn't arrive as an emotion. It arrived as attention. Delia noticed. That was the whole thing. She noticed the granola bar tasting good at an hour when nothing should taste good. She noticed a church sign that said three words. She noticed a woman arranging cups with care. And for thirty seconds, she turned around in a hospital lobby and watched the sky do what it does every day without asking for an audience.

There is something gently humbling about gratitude that comes from small things. It suggests that the world has been offering gifts all along — steady, quiet, unremarkable gifts — and the only thing missing was someone to receive them.

You may be on your own familiar route today, moving toward the next task, the next obligation, the next thing that needs doing. The river is still under the bridge. The light is still hitting it at some angle. It might be worth a glance.
