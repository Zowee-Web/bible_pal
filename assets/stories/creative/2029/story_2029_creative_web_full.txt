Tomas sat on the back porch with an empty dog bowl in his lap. The ceramic was cool against his palms. Biscuit had been gone for three days now. The vet said it was time, and his parents agreed, and Tomas had held Biscuit's soft golden ear in the waiting room until the very end.

The bowl still said BISCUIT in wobbly blue letters. Tomas had painted those letters when he was six, on a Saturday when his hands were small and the paintbrush felt too big.

He ran his thumb over the paint. Some of it was chipping away. The B was missing its bottom curve.

His mother opened the screen door. "Lunch is ready, sweetheart," she said softly.

"I'm not hungry."

She sat beside him on the porch step. She didn't say anything about eating. She didn't say it would get easier or that time would help. She just sat. Her shoulder touched his.

The backyard was quiet without Biscuit. No jingling collar. No happy bark at the squirrels. No paws clicking on the porch boards in that familiar tap-tap-tap. The silence was the loudest thing Tomas had ever heard.

"I keep thinking I hear him," Tomas said. His throat felt tight, like the words had to squeeze past something.

"Me too," his mother said. "This morning I almost poured food in his bowl. I had the bag in my hand before I remembered."

A tear slid down Tomas's cheek. Then another. He didn't wipe them.

"Is it always going to feel like this?" he asked.

"No," his mother said. "But it's going to feel like this for a while. And that's okay. Being sad means you loved him."

They sat together for a long time. A breeze came through the maple tree and scattered yellow leaves across the grass. The leaves fell slowly, turning and drifting, taking their time.

One leaf landed in the dog bowl. It was bright gold with edges of orange. Perfect and whole, not crumpled or torn. It sat in the bottom of the bowl like a small gift from the tree.

Tomas picked it up carefully. He turned it over in his fingers. The veins in the leaf made a pattern like tiny rivers. It was warm from the sun.

"Can I keep this?" he asked.

"Of course you can."

He carried the leaf inside and found his favorite book on his nightstand. It was about a boy and his dog who went on adventures in the woods. Biscuit used to sleep on Tomas's feet while he read it, his warm weight pressing down, his breathing slow and steady.

Tomas pressed the leaf between the pages and closed the book gently, like closing a door that held something precious.

That night, he set the dog bowl on his bookshelf next to the book with the leaf inside. The bowl with its wobbly blue letters. The book with its hidden treasure.

He climbed into bed. The house was quiet.

"Goodnight, Biscuit," he whispered.

The quiet pressed in around him. But it didn't feel empty. It felt like love was still there, tucked into the corners of the room, quiet but steady, like the veins of that golden leaf — small, hidden, and holding everything together.
