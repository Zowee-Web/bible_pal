The moving truck arrived before breakfast. Nora heard it from her bedroom — that low, heavy rumble that shook the window glass. She pulled back her curtain and looked.

The truck was enormous. It filled Becca's whole driveway and part of the street. Two men climbed out and stretched their arms and pulled the back door up with a rattling crash.

Nora got dressed. She brushed her teeth. She went downstairs and poured cereal into a bowl but didn't eat it.

Her mom looked at her from across the kitchen.

"You can go sit outside if you want," her mom said.

So Nora went out to the front porch and sat on the top step with her knees pulled up to her chest.

The men started carrying things out of Becca's house. First the small stuff — lamps and boxes and trash bags full of clothes. Then the big stuff. The bookshelf from Becca's room, the one covered in stickers. The rocking chair from the living room. The kitchen table with the wobbly leg that Becca's dad was always going to fix but never did.

Every piece disappeared into the mouth of the truck.

Nora's chest felt tight, like someone had wrapped a belt around it and pulled.

She and Becca had been best friends since kindergarten. Five whole years. They had learned to ride bikes on this street. They had caught fireflies in Becca's backyard. They had buried a time capsule under the oak tree — a mason jar with drawings and a penny and a note that said OPEN IN 100 YEARS.

Now one of the men was carrying Becca's mattress through the door, bent sideways to fit through.

Becca came out behind him. She stood on her porch for a second, looking around like she was trying to memorize everything. Then she saw Nora.

She crossed the street.

She was holding a shoebox with a rubber band around it, and her eyes were red.

"Here," Becca said. She sat down and put the box between them.

Nora lifted the lid. Inside were all the friendship bracelets they had ever made. Dozens of them. Blue and purple and yellow and green and some with beads and some without, all tangled together.

"I kept the ones you made me," Becca said. "They're already packed."

Nora picked up a green one. She remembered making it at Becca's kitchen table on a rainy Saturday. Becca's mom had given them hot chocolate with extra marshmallows.

"What if you forget me?" Nora said quietly.

Becca turned and looked at her. "Nora. I have seventeen of your bracelets."

Nora almost smiled. Almost.

They sat together on the step and watched the men work. They counted the boxes coming out — twenty-two, twenty-three, twenty-four. A lamp shaped like a dolphin. A rolled-up rug. A garbage bag that clanked like it was full of pots.

"Becca!" Her dad stood by the truck, keys in his hand.

Becca stood up slowly. She hugged Nora, and Nora hugged back, and neither of them let go for a long time.

Then Becca ran across the street and climbed into the car behind the truck. Her hand pressed flat against the window.

Nora raised her hand too.

The car pulled out. The truck followed, groaning under all that weight. They turned the corner, and the street went quiet.

Nora looked down at the shoebox. She pulled out a purple bracelet and tied it around her wrist. Then a blue one. Then a yellow one.

She sat there on the porch step in the morning light with her arm full of color. The oak tree swayed across the street. Somewhere underneath it, a mason jar waited in the dark soil with their drawings and their penny and their note.

A cardinal landed on Becca's empty mailbox, tilted its head, and sang.
