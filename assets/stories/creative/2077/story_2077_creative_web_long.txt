The moving truck came on the first Tuesday of summer. Nora heard it before she saw it — a deep rumble that vibrated through the floor of her bedroom and made the glass of water on her nightstand tremble.

She pulled back the curtain.

The truck was the biggest one she had ever seen, white with orange letters on the side. It backed slowly into Becca's driveway across the street, beeping with each inch, and two men in gray shirts climbed out and stretched their arms over their heads.

Nora stood at the window for a long time. Then she got dressed and went downstairs.

Her dad was making eggs. Her little brother was banging a spoon on the table. Everything smelled like toast and butter, and it all felt wrong.

"Morning, sweetheart," her dad said.

Nora sat down. She picked up a piece of toast and held it but didn't eat.

Through the kitchen window, she could see the men pulling the truck's ramp down. It hit the pavement with a clang.

"Can I go outside?" Nora asked.

Her dad looked at her with soft eyes. "Of course."

She went out to the front porch and sat on the top step. The concrete was already warm from the sun. She pulled her knees up and wrapped her arms around them.

The men started carrying things out of Becca's house.

First came the small boxes — brown cardboard with words written in black marker. KITCHEN. BOOKS. BECCA'S ROOM. Each box had a piece of blue tape on the corner.

Then came the lamps and the pictures from the hallway. Nora recognized the one with the sunflowers that hung above Becca's stairs. She had looked at that painting a thousand times, running up to Becca's room after school.

The bookshelf came next. The tall white one from Becca's bedroom, covered in sparkly stickers they had put on together in second grade. A unicorn. A rainbow. A cat wearing sunglasses. The men tilted it sideways to fit through the door, and some of the stickers peeled at the edges.

Nora's stomach hurt.

She and Becca had been best friends since the first day of kindergarten, when Becca dropped her crayon box and Nora helped pick them up. Every single crayon, even the ones that rolled under the cubbies. That was five years ago. Five years of riding bikes and catching fireflies and sleeping over and making up songs and whispering secrets under blanket forts on Becca's brown couch.

Now the brown couch was coming through the front door.

The two men carried it carefully down the porch steps. The cushions were wrapped in plastic that crinkled and shone in the sun. Nora could see the little stain on the armrest from when they spilled grape juice last Thanksgiving. Becca's mom hadn't even been mad. She just laughed and threw a towel at them.

The couch slid into the truck and vanished in the dark.

More things came. The kitchen table with the wobbly leg. Becca's bike, purple with white streamers. A box that rattled with dishes. The rocking chair from the living room where Becca's mom read to them when they were little.

Piece by piece, the house emptied out.

Nora's mom came and sat beside her. She had two glasses of lemonade. She handed one to Nora without a word.

They sat together, drinking lemonade, watching.

"I keep thinking maybe they'll change their mind," Nora said.

Her mom put a hand on her back. She didn't say anything, but her hand was warm and steady.

After a while, Becca came outside.

She stood on her porch and looked around — at the street, at the yard, at the big oak tree between their houses. Then she looked at Nora. Her eyes were red and puffy, and she was holding a shoebox with a rubber band around it.

She crossed the street slowly, like her feet were heavy.

She sat down next to Nora on the step. Their shoulders touched.

"I brought you something," Becca said. She put the shoebox on Nora's lap.

Nora pulled off the rubber band and lifted the lid.

Inside were friendship bracelets. Dozens of them. Blue and purple and yellow and green, some with tiny letter beads, some with patterns, some just simple knots. They were tangled together in a big colorful pile, like a nest made of thread.

Every single bracelet they had ever made. Five years of Saturday afternoons and rainy recesses and summer days on this very porch with the embroidery floss spread out between them.

"I kept the ones you made me," Becca said. "They're in my suitcase. In the zipper part so they won't get lost."

Nora's chin started to wobble. She pressed her lips together hard.

"What if it's different?" Nora asked. "What if everything changes?"

Becca was quiet for a moment. She picked at a thread on her shorts.

"We could write letters," Becca said. "Real ones. With stamps and envelopes. Not just texts."

"On paper?"

"On paper."

Nora looked down at the tangle of bracelets. She pulled out a green one — the first one she'd ever made. It was messy and uneven, and the knots were all different sizes. She remembered sitting at Becca's kitchen table, her fingers clumsy, Becca showing her how to loop the thread over and pull it tight. Becca's mom had given them hot chocolate with extra marshmallows even though it was April.

"Becca!" Her dad was standing by the car, jingling his keys. The truck was closed up now, latched and locked. The house behind him looked strange with the curtains gone. The windows stared like empty eyes.

Becca stood up. She hugged Nora.

Nora hugged back. She could smell Becca's strawberry shampoo and feel the bones of her shoulders and hear her breathing, short and shaky. She held on.

"I'll write first," Becca whispered.

Then she pulled away and ran across the street. She climbed into the back seat of the car and pressed her hand flat against the window.

Nora raised her hand too.

The car started. The truck growled to life in front of it. They pulled onto the street, slow and heavy, and rolled past Nora's house. She could see Becca's face in the window, blurry through the glass, and then the car turned the corner and the street went still.

The quiet was enormous.

Nora sat on the step for a long time. A squirrel ran across Becca's empty driveway and stopped, confused, like it knew something was different. A sprinkler clicked on two houses down.

She looked at the shoebox. She pulled out the green bracelet, the messy first one, and tied it carefully around her wrist. Then a purple one. Then a blue one with tiny gold beads. She lined them up, one after another, until her wrist was covered in color.

She stood and walked across the street. Becca's yard was quiet. The porch was bare. But around the side, under the big oak tree, she stopped and crouched down and pressed her palm flat against the dirt.

Somewhere under there — maybe a foot down, maybe two — was a mason jar. Inside it were two drawings, a shiny penny, and a folded piece of notebook paper that said OPEN IN 100 YEARS in Becca's round handwriting and Nora's wobbly handwriting, side by side.

Nora patted the ground once, gently, the way you'd pat something you were keeping safe.

She walked back across the street and sat on her porch. She held her arm up. The bracelets caught the light — green, purple, blue, gold — bright against the warm brown of her skin.

Down the street, a mailbox flag was up. Nora looked at it for a long moment.

Then she went inside, found a piece of paper, and picked up a pen.
