Every Tuesday morning, Estelle Kovac set the kettle on the stove, pulled out a sheet of cream-colored stationery, and uncapped the same fountain pen she'd used to grade essays for thirty-one years. The pen had a nick in the barrel from the time a seventh grader knocked it off her desk during a particularly heated debate about *To Kill a Mockingbird*. She kept it anyway. Some things earned their scars.

The kitchen table in her small duplex in Braddock, Pennsylvania, held a shoebox full of index cards. Each card had a name, a graduation year, and whatever address she'd last been able to find. Some of those addresses were ten years old. Some were probably wrong by now. She wrote the letters anyway.

This particular Tuesday, she pulled the card for DeShawn Hollis, Class of 2009. He'd been the kind of kid who sat in the back row and drew elaborate mazes in the margins of his notebook while she taught grammar. She'd kept one of those mazes — it was pinned to the corkboard above her desk even now, the pencil lines still sharp. DeShawn had never caused trouble. He'd just been quiet in a way that made her pay attention.

She wrote: *Dear DeShawn, I don't know if this will reach you. I've been thinking about those mazes you used to draw — the ones with multiple exits. Most kids only drew one way out. You always drew three or four. I thought that was something worth mentioning, even fifteen years later.*

She paused, sipped her tea, and continued. She told him about the cardinal that had been visiting her bird feeder all month, how it arrived at the same time every morning like it had somewhere to be. She told him her neighbor's daughter had just started seventh grade and was terrified of it, and that Estelle had told the girl it was okay to be terrified of things that mattered.

She signed the letter *With fondness, Mrs. K*, folded it into an envelope, and pressed a stamp in the corner.

Most of her letters went unanswered. Out of the two hundred or so she'd sent over the past four years, she'd received maybe twenty responses. A few came back marked *Return to Sender*. One arrived six months after she'd sent it, forwarded three times, with a note scrawled on the back of a grocery receipt: *Mrs. K — I can't believe you remembered me. I'm doing okay. I'm a welder now. Thank you.*

That grocery receipt was pinned to the corkboard too.

Her daughter, Lina, asked her once why she kept writing when so few people wrote back. Estelle had been washing dishes at the time. She'd turned off the faucet and dried her hands slowly on the towel that hung from the oven handle.

"When I was twenty-four and brand new at teaching," she said, "I almost quit. I thought I was terrible at it. Then one morning I found a note in my mailbox from another teacher down the hall. She wrote that she'd overheard one of my students telling a friend that my class was the only reason he came to school. That teacher didn't have to write that note. She probably forgot about it by lunchtime." Estelle hung the towel back on the handle. "I didn't forget."

Lina hadn't asked again.

On this Tuesday, after she finished DeShawn's letter, Estelle pulled on her coat and walked four blocks to the post office. The February air bit at her ears. The mailbox was the old blue kind, the paint chipped along the edges. She held the envelope for a moment at the slot, then let it drop.

Walking home, she passed the elementary school on Kirkpatrick Street. Through the chain-link fence, she could see kids on the playground, their breath coming out in small clouds. One boy sat alone on the bench near the swings, drawing something on his knee with his finger.

Estelle stopped. She watched him for a few seconds, then kept walking. When she got home, she pulled the shoebox toward her again and picked up another card.
