Every Tuesday morning, Colette sat at the oak desk her husband had built thirty years ago and uncapped the same fountain pen. The ink was always blue-black. The stationery was always cream-colored, slightly textured, bought in bulk from a shop in town that kept it in stock just for her.

She kept a list in a leather notebook — names, last known addresses, years taught. Some entries had been crossed out and rewritten with updated information, forwarded by parents or siblings or sometimes the students themselves, writing back after years of silence.

This week: Darren Okafor, class of 2006. He'd been the boy who drew fighter jets in the margins of his vocabulary worksheets. Quiet, steady, easy to overlook in a room full of louder kids.

She wrote about the mockingbird that had taken up residence in the magnolia outside her kitchen window. She mentioned the weather, the way the creek behind her house had risen after last week's rain. She told him she still remembered his drawing of a plane breaking through a cloud bank — how the detail in the wings had stopped her mid-grading. She wrote that she hoped he was still making things, whatever form that took now.

She didn't know if Darren still lived at the address in her notebook. She didn't know if he'd open the envelope or toss it. Fourteen years of weekly letters had taught her that some came back marked "Return to Sender," and some disappeared into silence, and once in a while — maybe four or five times a year — her phone would ring or a letter would arrive, and a voice or a hand she hadn't encountered in decades would say, "I got your letter. I didn't know anybody remembered."

Colette folded the page into thirds, slid it into the envelope, and pressed the seal flat with her thumb. She set it on the small stack by the door — one letter, ready for the mailbox.

The fountain pen went back in the drawer. The notebook stayed open, a pencil resting in the spine, already marking next week's name.
