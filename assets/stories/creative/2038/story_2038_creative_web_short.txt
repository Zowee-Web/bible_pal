Paloma walked into Kessler's Hardware on a Tuesday afternoon with a list: wood screws, painter's tape, a replacement washer for the kitchen faucet. Her shoes were muddy.

The store was quiet. She knelt in the plumbing aisle, sorting through bags of O-rings, when she heard it — music from a phone speaker in the next aisle. Old Motown. Horns and a bass line that moved in your chest before you recognized the melody.

She stood and looked over the shelf divider. A man in the paint section held two sample cards — one in each hand, arms extended — and swayed. Not performing. Just moving his hips to the beat while comparing shades of yellow.

Maybe sixty-five, work boots, flannel with the sleeves rolled. He turned, saw her, and stopped. The music kept going.

"Sorry," he said. "The Temptations."

"Don't apologize."

He grinned — the kind that cracked his whole face open like a window somebody forgot was painted shut. "My wife wants the kitchen yellow. I've been here twenty minutes and I can't tell Buttercream from Honeycomb."

Paloma walked around to his aisle. "Honeycomb. Buttercream looks pink in evening light."

"You sure?"

"I painted my bathroom Buttercream last year and spent three months wondering why it looked like the inside of a seashell."

He laughed. The sound bounced off concrete and metal shelves and came back bigger than it left.

The song changed — faster, piano intro running before the drums caught up. His feet moved again, involuntary. Paloma's did the same. She felt it in her ankles, then her knees. Two strangers in muddy shoes, surrounded by primer and tape, dancing to a phone speaker that didn't have the wattage for the moment but delivered anyway.

Forty-five seconds. Then the song ended. They looked at each other and laughed — not politely, but the kind that comes from the belly and doesn't ask permission.

"I'm Paloma."

"Vernon." His handshake was warm and paint-flecked.

She paid for her washers at the front. Vernon was still in the paint aisle, humming, the Honeycomb card tucked in his shirt pocket.

Paloma sat in her car. The parking lot was bright. The sky was absurd with blue. She laughed once more — a private gift from a Tuesday she'd expected nothing from.
