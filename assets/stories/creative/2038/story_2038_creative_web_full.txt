What makes a person start dancing in a hardware store on a Tuesday afternoon?

Paloma didn't know the answer when she walked into Kessler's Hardware on Route 12. She had a list: wood screws, painter's tape, a replacement washer for the kitchen faucet. Her shoes were muddy from the yard. Her hair was held back with an elastic band from the junk drawer.

The store was quiet. Mid-afternoon, mid-week. She found the plumbing aisle and knelt in front of the drawer of O-rings, sorting through labeled bags, matching sizes to the number she'd written on her hand.

That's when she heard it. Music — tinny, from a phone speaker in the next aisle. An old Motown song. The Temptations. Horns first, then that bass line that settled into your chest before you recognized the melody.

She stood and looked over the shelf divider. A man was standing in the paint section, holding two sample cards — one in each hand — and swaying. Not performing. Just moving to the beat with the ease of someone whose body had been doing this for forty years.

He was maybe sixty-five. Work boots, flannel shirt, sleeves rolled. His phone sat on the shelf beside a row of primer cans, playing the song into the empty aisle.

He turned, saw her, and stopped. The music kept going.

"Sorry," he said.

"Don't apologize." She meant it.

He grinned — the kind that cracked his whole face open. "My wife sent me for paint. She wants the kitchen yellow. I've been here twenty minutes and I cannot tell the difference between Buttercream and Honeycomb."

Paloma walked around to his aisle. She held both cards under the light. "Honeycomb. Buttercream shifts pink in evening light. I painted my bathroom Buttercream last year and spent three months wondering why it looked like the inside of a seashell."

He laughed. The sound bounced off the concrete floor and came back bigger. He tucked the Honeycomb card into his shirt pocket.

"You just saved my marriage. My wife would have blamed me for the pink."

"She would have been right to."

The song changed. Faster, brighter, a piano intro running ahead of the drums. His feet moved again — involuntary, like his shoes had made a decision his brain hadn't approved.

Paloma's feet did the same thing. She felt it in her ankles first, then her knees. She stood in the paint aisle holding a bag of rubber washers, and her body decided this was a moment for dancing.

They danced for forty-five seconds. Two strangers in muddy shoes, surrounded by primer and tape, moving to a phone speaker that didn't have the wattage for the moment but delivered anyway.

Then the song ended. They stopped. They looked at each other and laughed — the kind that comes from the belly and doesn't ask permission.

"I'm Paloma."

"Vernon." His handshake was warm and paint-flecked. "Thank you for the color advice and the dancing."

She paid for her washers and walked to the parking lot. The sky was absurd with blue. She sat in her car and laughed one more time — a private laugh, a gift from a Tuesday she'd expected nothing from.

Joy, she decided, didn't always announce itself. Sometimes it just showed up in the paint aisle, wearing work boots, holding two shades of yellow, already dancing.
