Three weeks before the wedding, Rachel arrived at the house with a portable speaker and a pair of leather-soled shoes in a shopping bag.

"Size eleven," she said, setting the bag on the kitchen counter.

Samuel looked inside. The shoes were smooth-bottomed, the color of dark honey.

"What's wrong with my boots?"

"Everything. Put those on."

He sat at the kitchen table and laced them. They fit well. He stood and slid slightly on the tile.

"That's the point," Rachel said. "You need to glide, not grip."

She'd already moved the table against the wall. The chairs were stacked by the refrigerator. The kitchen floor was the biggest open space in the house — twelve feet by fourteen, cream-colored tile that Elena had picked out the year before she got sick.

Rachel set the speaker on the counter and scrolled through her phone. The song began — a waltz in three-quarter time, strings and piano. Warm and slow.

"This is it?" he asked.

"This is the father-daughter dance. You and me. Two and a half minutes."

"Two and a half minutes."

"You've endured longer."

She took his right hand and placed it against her back, between her shoulder blades. She lifted his left hand to the height of her ear.

"Frame," she said. "Keep this shape. Don't collapse."

He held the position. His shoulder ached almost immediately.

"Now. Step back with your left when I come forward with my right."

She stepped. He stepped. They moved six inches.

"Good. Side step right."

He went left.

"Your other right, Dad."

He corrected. They completed one box step — a small, careful square on the kitchen tile. Rachel nodded.

"Again. Don't look at your feet."

He looked at his feet. She put her hand under his chin and tilted his face up.

"Look at me. Trust your feet."

He looked at her. She had Elena's eyes — dark brown, almost black, steady as a carpenter's level. He stepped back when she stepped forward. He stepped right when she moved left. The box turned slowly on the tile.

The song ended. She played it again.

By the fourth repetition he could complete the box without stopping, though his frame kept dropping and his steps were too small. Rachel corrected him each time with the same patient firmness, adjusting his elbow, nudging his foot with hers.

"Bigger steps. You're shuffling."

"I don't want to step on you."

"I'll move. That's my job. Your job is to lead."

"I thought you were leading."

"I'm teaching. There's a difference. When we're out there, you lead."

He tried a larger step and nearly lost his balance. The leather soles betrayed him on the tile and he grabbed the counter edge. Rachel caught his other arm.

"Okay," she said. "Let's try the garage."

They moved to the garage. She'd prepared it the day before — workbench pushed aside, sawhorses stacked, a sheet of plywood on the floor covered with a vinyl remnant. It gave slightly under his feet, more forgiving than tile.

The speaker sat on a shelf between paint cans. The waltz began again.

This time the space felt different. Larger. The garage door was open, and the evening air came in carrying the smell of the neighbor's grill and the jasmine that climbed the east wall. Samuel's shoulders dropped. His frame held.

They completed eight measures without error.

"Better," Rachel said.

"I feel like a marionette."

"You look like one too. But a improving marionette."

They practiced for an hour. She taught him a simple turn — guiding her under his raised arm and catching her hand again on the other side. He fumbled it three times. On the fourth, she spun through cleanly and came back to position, and he felt something click into place like a tenon finding its mortise.

"Mom tried to teach me once," he said. "At the Hendersons' party."

"Nineteen ninety-four. She told me. She said you stepped on her feet."

"She exaggerated."

"She said eleven times."

"Eight. Maybe nine."

Rachel smiled. It was Elena's smile — the left side lifting first, the right catching up.

They practiced every evening after that. Tuesday through Sunday, six o'clock, the garage. Samuel came straight from work, ate a sandwich standing up, and laced the honey-colored shoes. Rachel brought her speaker and her patience.

On the third evening, she added music she hadn't planned — a faster piece in four-four time, something with guitars.

"This isn't the wedding song," he said.

"No. This is for range. Feel the difference in the count."

He felt it. Four beats instead of three. His body adjusted faster than he expected.

On the fifth evening, she brought Daniel. Her fiancé stood in the doorway of the garage and watched Samuel lead Rachel through the waltz, turn and all, without a single missed step.

"He's good," Daniel said.

"He's adequate," Rachel said. But she was grinning.

On the seventh evening, the Saturday before the wedding, they practiced one last time. The garage was warm. Samuel had strung a work light from the ceiling, and it cast a yellow circle on the vinyl floor. Moths tapped against the bulb.

Rachel played the song. They danced.

His frame held. His steps were wide and sure. The turn came and she spun beneath his arm, her dark hair swinging, and he caught her hand without looking. They moved around the plywood floor in slow circles — past the workbench, past the shelves, past the open door where the night air came in.

Halfway through, he closed his eyes. He didn't need to count anymore. The rhythm lived in his feet now, in his knees, in the hand that rested on his daughter's back.

The song ended. They stood still. The moths tapped overhead. The jasmine on the wall breathed its scent into the garage.

"Ready?" she asked.

"Ready."

She picked up the speaker and kissed his cheek. At the door she turned.

"Dad."

"Yeah."

"Wear the good shoes."

He nodded. She walked across the driveway to her car, keys jingling. He heard the engine start and the tires roll over gravel.

Samuel stood alone on the vinyl floor in the yellow light. He lifted his hands — right hand at shoulder blade height, left hand raised — and held the frame. The garage was quiet. He stepped back with his left foot, then to the side, then forward, then together. The box turned in silence. The moths circled the bulb. The jasmine climbed the wall outside, white blossoms open in the dark.
