The kitchen table had been pushed against the wall. The chairs sat stacked in the hallway. Samuel stood in his socks on the linoleum, arms stiff at his sides, while his daughter counted.

"One two three, one two three. Dad, your feet."

He looked down. His right foot had gone somewhere it shouldn't.

"Sorry."

"Don't apologize. Just feel it."

Rachel pressed play on her phone again. The melody filled the small kitchen — strings and piano, the song she'd chosen months ago. She took his hand and placed it on her shoulder blade. Her other hand found his.

"You're gripping," she said.

He loosened his fingers. She stepped toward him and he stepped back, and for two full measures they moved together without collision.

"There," she whispered.

He lost it on the turn. His heel caught the edge of the rug and he stumbled, laughing, and she laughed too, steadying him with both hands.

"We have three weeks," she said.

"I'm aware."

She reset his arms. Her grip was patient and firm, the way her mother's had been when teaching someone a thing that mattered. Samuel noticed this and said nothing.

They tried again. The music carried them past the refrigerator, past the window where evening light came through. He counted under his breath. She stopped correcting. Somewhere around the second verse his shoulders dropped and his back straightened and the counting went quiet.

Rachel rested her chin near his collar.

"Mom would have led," she said.

"Mom would have taken over the whole floor."

Rachel smiled against his shirt.

The song ended. They stood still in the kitchen, her hand on his shoulder, his hand on her back. The phone screen dimmed. Outside, a neighbor's sprinkler ticked across the sidewalk in the warm air, steady as a metronome.

Rachel reached for the phone and pressed play again.
