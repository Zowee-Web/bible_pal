The garage had been cleared. Samuel's workbench stood pushed against the far wall, and the sawhorses leaned in the corner under a tarp. Rachel had swept the concrete floor twice and laid down a plywood sheet topped with an old vinyl remnant from the basement.

"This is the dance floor?" he asked.

"This is where we practice. The dance floor will be much nicer."

He stood at the edge of the vinyl in his work boots. She pointed at them.

"Shoes off."

He pulled them off and set them by the door. His socks had a hole near the left toe.

"Okay," Rachel said. She held up her phone and a waltz began — strings first, then piano joining. The song she and Daniel had chosen for the reception. "Right hand here." She placed his palm against her shoulder blade. "Left hand up."

He raised it like he was taking an oath.

"Not that high."

She adjusted him. He felt like a man being fitted for a suit, turned and positioned and measured.

"Now. Step back with your left foot when I step forward with my right."

She stepped. He didn't.

"Dad."

"I'm thinking."

"Don't think. Move."

He stepped back. She followed. He stepped to the side. She mirrored. For one full measure the geometry held, and then he turned the wrong direction and they collided gently, chest to shoulder.

"Sorry, sorry."

"You're doing fine. Again."

They reset. The music started over. This time he watched her feet and she told him to look up, and when he looked up he drifted left, and when he corrected he went too far right.

Rachel stopped and put both hands on his arms.

"You built a deck last summer," she said. "You re-plumbed the upstairs bathroom. You can learn a box step."

"Pipes don't move."

She laughed — the same laugh her mother had, sudden and bright, like something dropped from a height. Samuel felt it land in his chest.

They tried again. And again. The phone replayed the song seven times. On the eighth, something shifted. His body stopped arguing with the rhythm. His feet found a groove in the vinyl, and the counting in his head faded, and he felt the music the way you feel current in a river — not thinking about it, just moving inside it.

Rachel's hand tightened on his.

"There you go," she murmured.

They passed the workbench and the stacked lumber. They turned near the shelves of paint cans. The garage smelled like sawdust and motor oil and the jasmine climbing the wall outside the open door.

"Your mother tried to teach me once," he said. "At the Hendersons' anniversary party. Nineteen ninety-four."

"How'd that go?"

"She gave up and danced with your uncle."

Rachel grinned. "She told me. She said you stepped on her feet eleven times."

"It was eight."

The song ended. They stopped near the center of the makeshift floor. The evening had cooled the garage, and a breeze came through the open door carrying the smell of cut grass. Rachel leaned her head against his shoulder for a moment — just a moment — and then straightened.

"Same time tomorrow?" she asked.

"I'll be here."

She picked up her phone and her keys. At the door she turned back. He was still standing on the vinyl in his torn socks, arms at his sides, a faint rhythm still moving in his shoulders.

She pressed play one more time and set the phone on the workbench. The waltz filled the garage. She left without a word.

Samuel lifted his hands to the empty air and stepped back with his left foot. The music carried him past the sawhorses, past the shelves, into the square of fading light that lay across the floor from the open door.
