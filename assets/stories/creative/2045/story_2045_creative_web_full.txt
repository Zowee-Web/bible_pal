The house felt different without Grandpa's chair in the corner. Zeke noticed it the moment he walked in. The big green recliner was gone, and in its place was just a square of carpet darker than the rest — the part that had been hidden under the chair for years.

His mother said they had given the chair to his uncle. Zeke didn't say anything. He just looked at the dark square on the floor.

Grandpa had been gone for three weeks. People kept saying things like "He's in a better place" and "Time heals everything." Zeke didn't know what to do with those words. They bounced off him like rain off a window.

The thing about missing someone is that it comes in small, sharp moments. When Zeke reached for the phone to call Grandpa and remembered he couldn't. When he heard a baseball game and thought, Grandpa would know the score. When he smelled coffee and expected to hear that rumbling laugh from the green chair.

He went outside and sat on the back steps. The yard was the same — the oak tree, the tire swing, the vegetable garden Grandpa had planted last spring. The garden. Nobody had looked at it in weeks.

Zeke walked over. Weeds had crept in at the edges. But the tomato plants were still growing, tall and heavy with fruit. Some tomatoes had turned deep red, bending the stems down with their weight.

He crouched in front of the biggest plant. He remembered Grandpa planting it — a Saturday in April. Those big, rough hands pressing tiny seeds into the soil, patting the earth over them gently, watering from the old green can.

"You just give them what they need," Grandpa had said, "and then you trust the growing to happen."

Zeke touched a fat red tomato. It was warm from the sun. He twisted it gently, the way Grandpa showed him, and it came free with a soft snap.

He held it in both hands. It smelled like summer — green and sharp and alive. Grandpa's hands had planted the seed that became this. The rain watered it. The sun ripened it. And here it was, warm and real, even though the one who planted it was gone.

It wasn't Grandpa. Nothing was. But it was something Grandpa had made, still growing, still giving fruit.

Zeke's eyes stung. He picked three more ripe tomatoes and carried them inside.

"From Grandpa's garden," he said, setting them on the counter.

His mother looked at them for a long moment. Then she picked one up and held it gently, with both hands.

"Let's have them with dinner," she said softly.

They sliced the tomatoes and ate them with salt, just the way Grandpa liked. No bread, no lettuce. Just the tomato and the taste of what the garden had given.

It tasted exactly right.
