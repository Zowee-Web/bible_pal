The house felt different without Grandpa's chair in the corner. Zeke noticed it the moment he walked in. The chair was gone, and in its place was just a square of carpet that was darker than the rest — the part that had been hidden under the chair for years and years.

His mother said they had given the chair to his uncle. Zeke didn't say anything. He just stood there looking at the dark square on the carpet.

Grandpa had been gone for three weeks now. People kept saying things like "He's in a better place" and "He lived a good long life." Zeke didn't know what to do with those words. They bounced off him like rain off a window.

He went outside and sat on the back steps. The yard was the same as always — the oak tree, the tire swing, the vegetable garden Grandpa had started last spring. The tomato plants were still growing. Nobody had picked them in a while, and some of the tomatoes had turned deep red and heavy, bending the stems down.

Zeke walked to the garden. He crouched down and looked at a fat red tomato. Grandpa had planted this one. His big, rough hands had pressed the tiny seed into the soil and patted the dirt over it and watered it from the green watering can.

Zeke picked the tomato carefully. It was warm from the sun and smelled like summer — green and sharp and alive.

He held it in both hands. It wasn't Grandpa. Nothing was Grandpa. But Grandpa's hands had planted it, and the sun had grown it, and here it was, warm and real and ready.

Zeke carried it inside and set it on the kitchen counter.

"From Grandpa's garden," he said.

His mother looked at the tomato for a long moment. Then she picked it up and held it the same way Zeke had, gently, with both hands.

"Let's have it with dinner," she said softly.

They sliced it and ate it with salt, just the way Grandpa liked. It tasted exactly right.
