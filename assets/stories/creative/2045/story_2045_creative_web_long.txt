The house felt different without Grandpa's chair in the corner. Zeke noticed it the moment he walked through the front door after school. The big green recliner was gone, and in its place was just a square of carpet that was darker than the rest — the part that had been hidden underneath for years and years, protected from feet and sunlight and spills.

His mother was in the kitchen. "We gave the chair to Uncle Roy," she said. "He wanted something to remember Dad by."

Zeke didn't say anything. He stood in the living room looking at the dark square on the floor. The shape of absence. That's what it looked like — the exact outline of something that used to be there and wasn't anymore.

Grandpa had been gone for three weeks. Twenty-three days. People kept coming to the house with casseroles and saying things like "He's in a better place" and "He lived a good, long life" and "Time heals everything." Zeke didn't know what to do with those words. They were meant to help, he knew that. But they bounced off him like rain off a window, leaving no mark at all.

The thing about missing someone is that it doesn't come all at once. It comes in small, sharp moments — when Zeke reached for the phone to call Grandpa and remembered he couldn't. When he heard a baseball game on someone's radio and thought, Grandpa would know the score. When he smelled coffee brewing and expected to hear that low, rumbling laugh from the green chair.

Zeke put his backpack down and went outside. He sat on the back porch steps and looked at the yard. Everything was the same as always — the old oak tree with the tire swing hanging from its lowest branch, the wooden fence with the gate that never latched right, and the vegetable garden along the south side that Grandpa had dug and planted last spring.

The garden. Zeke hadn't really looked at it in weeks. Nobody had.

He walked across the grass. The garden was overgrown at the edges, where weeds had crept in between the pepper plants and the basil. But the tomato plants were still growing — tall and green and heavy with fruit. Some of the tomatoes had turned deep, dark red. They hung from the stems like small lanterns, bending the branches down with their weight. A few had fallen to the ground and split open, spilling seeds into the rich soil.

Zeke crouched down in front of the biggest plant. He remembered the day Grandpa planted it. A Saturday in April. Grandpa's hands — big and rough, with dirt permanently under the nails — had pressed tiny seeds into the soil, one by one. He'd patted the earth over each one gently, like tucking something into bed. Then he'd watered them from the old green watering can with the dented spout.

"You just give them what they need," Grandpa had said, "and then you trust the growing to happen."

Zeke reached out and touched a fat red tomato. It was warm from the afternoon sun. He cupped it in his hand and twisted gently, the way Grandpa had shown him, and it came free from the vine with a soft snap.

He held it in both hands. It was heavy and smooth and smelled like summer — green and sharp and deeply alive. Grandpa's hands had planted the seed that became this. The rain had watered it. The sun had ripened it. And here it was, real and warm and ready, even though the one who planted it was gone.

It wasn't Grandpa. Nothing was Grandpa, and nothing would be. But it was something Grandpa had made, still growing, still giving fruit, still alive in the garden he had loved.

Zeke's eyes stung. He blinked and a tear ran down his cheek and landed on the tomato's red skin. He wiped it away with his thumb.

He picked three more tomatoes, choosing the ripest ones, and carried them inside cradled against his chest. He set them on the kitchen counter in a row.

"From Grandpa's garden," he said.

His mother turned from the stove. She looked at the tomatoes sitting there in the late afternoon light, red and shining. She was quiet for a long moment. Then she picked one up and held it the same way Zeke had — gently, carefully, with both hands, as if it were something precious.

"Let's have them with dinner," she said. Her voice was soft and a little unsteady, but she was almost smiling.

They sliced the tomatoes and ate them with just a sprinkle of salt, on a white plate, at the kitchen table. That was how Grandpa always ate the first ripe tomatoes of summer. No bread, no lettuce, nothing fancy. Just the tomato and the salt and the taste of what the garden had given.

It tasted exactly right. Like sun and soil and something good that keeps on growing.
