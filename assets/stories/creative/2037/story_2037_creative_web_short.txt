The mug sat on the third shelf, behind the everyday cups, where Theo had placed it the week after the funeral.

Dark blue ceramic. A chip on the rim his wife had made with a serving spoon eleven years ago. She'd laughed when it happened. He said he'd fix it, and they both knew he wouldn't.

For seven months, Theo hadn't touched it. He drank from a plain white cup and left the blue one facing the wall, handle turned inward like a door that didn't want to be opened.

His daughter, Simone, came by on a Saturday in March. Groceries and a casserole and the silence of someone who wanted to say something but hadn't found the words.

They put the groceries away together. When Simone opened the cabinet above the sink, she saw the mug. She reached for it.

"Don't," Theo said. Sharp.

She pulled her hand back. Didn't ask why.

They ate the casserole at the kitchen table without talking much. March light came through the window. A cardinal sat on the fence outside, red against gray wood.

After dinner, Simone washed the dishes. Theo dried. When everything was put away, she looked at him.

"Dad. The mug isn't her."

"I know that."

"Then why can't I touch it?"

He gripped the counter's edge. "Because when I look at it, I can hear her laughing about the chip. I'm afraid if I use it, that sound will wear off. Like the glaze."

Simone was quiet. Then she took the mug down gently and set it on the counter between them. The chip caught the light — a small crescent of rough clay.

"You don't have to drink from it," she said. "But it shouldn't face the wall."

Theo picked it up. The weight was familiar — heavier than the white cups, warmer somehow, though it held nothing. He turned it handle-outward and placed it back on the shelf. Front row. Visible.

Simone squeezed his arm and went for her coat. At the door she paused. "She'd want you to use the good mugs, Dad."

After she left, the house was quiet. But the mug was on the shelf facing forward, the chip was showing, and the laughter it carried hadn't worn off at all.
