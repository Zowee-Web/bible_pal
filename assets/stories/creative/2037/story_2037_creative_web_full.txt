The mug sat on the third shelf, behind the everyday cups, where Theo had placed it the week after the funeral.

It was ceramic, dark blue, handmade by a potter at the farmers' market in Covington. Joanna had bought it twelve years ago. She'd loved the color — the blue of deep water, she said.

There was a chip on the rim. She'd made it with a serving spoon eleven years ago, swinging it too fast while telling a story. The chip was small — a crescent of rough clay where the glaze had broken away. She had laughed. He had said he'd fix it. They both knew he wouldn't.

Now Joanna had been gone for seven months, and Theo drank his coffee from a plain white cup that meant nothing. The blue mug faced the wall, handle turned inward like a door that had decided to close.

His daughter, Simone, came by on a Saturday in March. She brought groceries and a casserole that smelled like rosemary. They put things away together. Theo handed her cans; she placed them on the shelves with the labels facing out — a habit from her mother.

When she opened the cabinet above the sink, she saw the blue mug. She reached for it.

"Don't," Theo said. The word came out sharp.

Simone pulled her hand back. She didn't ask why.

They ate dinner at the kitchen table without talking much. The March light came through the window in pale rectangles. A cardinal landed on the fence and sat there for a full minute, red against gray wood.

After dinner, Simone washed the dishes. Theo dried. The rhythm was old and known. When everything was put away, she stood at the counter and looked at him.

"Dad. The mug isn't her."

"I know that."

"Then why can't I touch it?"

Theo gripped the counter edge. "Because when I look at it, I can hear her laughing about the chip. That particular laugh — surprised at herself. And I'm afraid if I use it, that sound will wear off. Like the glaze wore off where the spoon hit."

The kitchen went quiet. The refrigerator hummed.

Simone opened the cabinet. She reached past the front row and took down the blue mug. She set it on the counter between them.

The chip caught the light. That small crescent of rough clay where the story had left its mark.

"You don't have to drink from it," she said. "But it shouldn't face the wall. She wouldn't want to face the wall."

Theo picked it up with both hands, the way Joanna used to hold it on cold mornings — palms around the belly of it, fingers laced, drawing warmth from whatever was inside.

It was empty. It was heavier than the white cups. It was warm in a way that had nothing to do with temperature.

He turned the handle outward and set it back on the shelf. Front row. Visible.

Simone squeezed his arm and went to get her coat. At the door, she paused. "She'd want you to use the good mugs, Dad. She always said life was too short for the boring ones."

After she left, Theo stood in the kitchen. The house was quiet the way it always was now. But the mug was on the shelf, facing forward, and the chip was showing, and the laughter it carried was still there — precise and clear, undimmed by the handling.

He filled the kettle. He didn't reach for the blue mug. Not tonight. But he left it where it was — front row, handle out, ready for the morning he would.
