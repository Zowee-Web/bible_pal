The notification had been there for eleven days.

Marcus saw it every time he picked up the phone. A small red circle. One voicemail. His mother's number beneath it.

They hadn't spoken in seven months. Not since the kitchen, the raised voices, the door he'd pulled shut behind him hard enough to rattle the frame. He remembered the sound of dishes shifting in the cabinet from the force of it.

Eleven days the message sat there. He'd almost played it twice — once in the parking lot after work, thumb hovering, then a coworker knocked on his window. Once at two in the morning, sitting on the edge of his bed in the dark, the phone warm in his hand. Both times he set it down.

The not knowing was its own kind of sound. A low hum beneath everything. He brushed his teeth and heard it. He drove to work and heard it. He sat across from friends at dinner and laughed at the right moments and heard it underneath, steady and close, like a second heartbeat.

She could be calling to fight. She could be calling to say she was done. She could be sick. She could be sorry. Every possibility lived in that small red circle, and as long as he didn't press play, none of them were true yet. None of them were final.

On the twelfth morning he stood at the kitchen sink. Coffee dripped behind him. Rain tapped against the window above the faucet. He held the phone and looked at the notification. His reflection floated faintly in the glass — just the outline of his jaw, his shoulder, the shape of someone standing still.

He thought about a story he'd heard once, a long time ago, about a man who'd been afraid to open a door. He couldn't remember the ending. Only that the door had been opened from the other side.

The coffee maker clicked off. The rain kept on. Marcus pressed his thumb to the screen and raised the phone slowly to his ear. The message began with a breath — just his mother breathing — and he closed his eyes and listened.
