The notification had been there for eleven days.

Marcus saw it every time he picked up the phone. A small red circle with a white number inside it. One voicemail. His mother's number beneath it, the ten digits he still knew by heart even though he'd deleted her contact twice and re-added it both times in the middle of the night, typing the numbers from memory with his thumb while the apartment was dark around him.

They hadn't spoken in seven months. Not since the kitchen. Not since her standing at the counter with the dishtowel twisted in her hands and him standing near the door with his coat already on. Not since the words that came fast and hot from both of them, overlapping, neither one listening. He'd pulled the door shut behind him hard enough to rattle the frame and shift the dishes in the cabinet. He heard a glass fall as he walked down the porch steps. He didn't go back to check if it broke.

Seven months.

And then, on a Tuesday, the voicemail appeared.

He noticed it at 6:47 in the morning, half-dressed, reaching for the phone on the nightstand to check the time. The red circle. Her number. A message seventeen seconds long. He stared at it while the alarm buzzed a second time, then silenced the alarm and set the phone down.

He told himself he'd listen to it after work.

He didn't.

He told himself he'd listen to it after dinner. After his shower. Before bed. He didn't do any of those things. Instead he set the phone on the nightstand, screen down, and lay in the dark staring at the ceiling fan turning slow circles above him.

By the fourth day the not-listening had become its own kind of ritual. He'd pick up the phone, see the notification, hold his thumb over the playback button, and then lock the screen. He did it in the parking lot at work, the engine ticking as it cooled, sunlight hot through the windshield. He did it in the cereal aisle at the grocery store, standing beneath fluorescent lights, holding a box of the brand she used to buy. He put the box back on the shelf. He walked out without buying anything.

The not knowing had a sound. A low persistent hum beneath everything else, the way a refrigerator hums and you stop hearing it until someone turns it off and the silence is sudden and enormous. Except this was the opposite. The hum was always growing, never shutting off. He brushed his teeth and heard it. He drove to work with the radio turned up loud and heard it beneath the music, beneath the traffic, beneath the DJ's voice. He sat across from friends at a restaurant downtown and laughed when they laughed and heard it pressing against the back of his ribs like a second heartbeat, close and steady.

His friend Deacon noticed.

They were on Deacon's couch on the eighth night, takeout containers open on the coffee table, a basketball game playing on mute. Marcus had his phone in his hand. He turned it over, looked at the screen, turned it back.

"You keep doing that," Deacon said.

"Doing what."

"Checking your phone and putting it away. You've done it four times since you sat down."

Marcus set the phone on the armrest. "My mom left me a voicemail."

Deacon lowered his fork. "When?"

"Eight days ago."

"You haven't listened to it."

"No."

Deacon leaned back. He was a careful man, broad-shouldered, deliberate. He chewed and swallowed before he spoke again. "You scared of what she said?"

Marcus watched the silent players on the screen running up and down the court, sneakers squeaking on hardwood that made no sound through the muted speakers. "I'm scared of what she said. And I'm scared of what she didn't say."

"Both at the same time."

"Yeah."

Because that was the shape of it. She could be calling to continue the fight, to pick it up where they'd left off seven months ago, her voice tight and sharp the way it got when she felt cornered. She could be calling to say she was done, that the silence had hardened into something permanent, that she was changing the locks or selling the house or simply closing the door of herself the way he'd closed her door. She could be sick. She could need money. She could be sorry.

Every one of those possibilities lived inside that seventeen-second message, and as long as he didn't press play, none of them were true yet. None of them were final. The voicemail was a door that swung both ways, and Marcus stood on his side of it with his hand flat against the wood, feeling the vibration of something on the other side, unable to push it open, unable to step away.

On the ninth night he sat in his car in the parking garage beneath his apartment building. Concrete pillars. Fluorescent tubes buzzing overhead, one of them flickering. His breath fogged faintly in the cold. He held the phone with both hands and looked at the screen.

Seventeen seconds.

A person could say almost anything in seventeen seconds. I love you. I'm dying. Don't call me again. I made your favorite soup and I don't know who to give it to. I'm sorry. I shouldn't have said what I said. You shouldn't have said what you said. Come home.

He locked the screen. He went upstairs. He didn't sleep until after two.

On the tenth day he almost deleted it. His thumb hovered over the trash icon, and something in his chest pulled tight, a wire stretched to its limit, and he shoved the phone into his pocket and walked three blocks in the cold until his face was numb.

On the eleventh night he dreamed about the kitchen. But in the dream there were no raised voices. His mother stood at the counter holding the dishtowel, and he stood near the door, and neither of them spoke. The room was filled with the ticking of the clock above the stove. She looked at him. Her mouth opened as though she were about to say something, and then he woke up, his heart beating fast, the apartment silent around him, the phone glowing on the nightstand with its small red circle.

On the twelfth morning he stood at the kitchen sink.

Rain tapped against the window above the faucet. The coffee maker gurgled and hissed behind him, filling the apartment with the heavy smell of dark roast. Gray light came through the glass. He held the phone in his right hand and looked at the notification.

His reflection floated faintly in the window — just the outline of his jaw, his shoulder, the shape of a man standing very still in a small kitchen while the rain came down.

He thought about something he'd heard a long time ago. A story about a father who saw his son walking up the road from a great distance. He couldn't remember who told it to him. A Sunday school teacher, maybe. Or his mother herself, years ago, sitting on the edge of his bed with the hallway light falling across her lap, her voice low and unhurried. He couldn't remember the words she'd used. Only the image — someone standing at the end of a long road, watching, not turning away, not going inside. Waiting for the figure to get close enough to recognize.

The coffee maker clicked off. The rain kept tapping.

Marcus pressed his thumb to the screen. He raised the phone slowly to his ear. There was a pause, a faint hiss of static, and then the message began — with a breath. Just his mother breathing, soft and unsteady, the way a person breathes when they've picked up the phone and don't yet know what to say. Then her voice, quiet, saying his name.

He closed his eyes. He leaned his hip against the counter. The rain ran down the window in thin lines, and he stood in the gray kitchen light and listened.
