The notification had been there for eleven days.

Marcus saw it every time he picked up the phone. A small red circle with a white number inside it. One voicemail. His mother's number beneath it, the digits he still knew by heart even though he'd deleted her contact twice and re-added it both times at three in the morning.

They hadn't spoken in seven months. Not since the kitchen, not since the raised voices, not since the door he'd pulled shut behind him hard enough to rattle the frame and shift the dishes in the cabinet. He remembered hearing a glass fall. He didn't go back to check if it broke.

Eleven days the message sat there.

He'd almost played it three times. Once in the parking lot after work, thumb hovering over the screen, engine ticking as it cooled. A coworker knocked on his window and he locked the phone so fast it slipped into the gap between the seat and the console. Once at two in the morning, sitting on the edge of his bed in the dark, the phone warm in his palm. He'd gotten as far as the playback screen. Then he set it face down on the nightstand and stared at the ceiling fan turning slow circles above him. Once in the cereal aisle at the grocery store, standing beneath fluorescent lights, holding a box of the brand she used to buy. He put the box back and walked out without buying anything.

The not knowing was its own kind of sound. A low hum beneath everything else. He brushed his teeth and heard it. He drove to work with the radio up and heard it underneath the music. He sat across from friends at a restaurant and laughed at the right moments and felt it pressing against the back of his ribs, steady and close, like a second pulse.

His friend Deacon noticed.

"You alright?" Deacon asked one evening, passing him a takeout container on the couch.

"Yeah."

"You keep checking your phone and then putting it away."

Marcus set the container on the coffee table. "My mom left me a voicemail."

"When?"

"Week and a half ago."

Deacon was quiet for a moment. "You haven't listened to it."

"No."

"You scared of what she said?"

Marcus looked at the television. A basketball game played on mute, players running silently up and down the court. "I'm scared of what she said and I'm scared of what she didn't say. Both at the same time."

Because that was the shape of it. She could be calling to fight. She could be calling to say she was finished, that the seven months had hardened into something permanent. She could be sick. She could be sorry. She could be asking for money. She could be asking for him to come home. Every possibility lived inside that small red circle, and as long as he didn't press play, none of them were true yet. None of them were final. The voicemail was a door that swung both ways, and he stood on one side of it with his hand on the knob, unable to turn it, unable to walk away.

On the twelfth morning he stood at the kitchen sink. Rain tapped against the window above the faucet. The coffee maker gurgled behind him, filling the apartment with the smell of dark roast. He held the phone and looked at the notification. His reflection floated faintly in the window glass — just the outline of his jaw, his shoulder, the shape of a man standing very still.

He thought about something he'd heard a long time ago. A story about a father who saw his son coming from a long way off. He couldn't remember who told it to him. A Sunday school teacher maybe, or his mother herself, years ago, sitting on the edge of his bed. He couldn't remember the words. Only the image — someone watching the road, waiting, not giving up on the distance.

The coffee maker clicked off. The rain kept on, tapping gently.

Marcus pressed his thumb to the screen and raised the phone slowly to his ear. The message began with a breath — just his mother breathing, a soft unsteady sound — and then her voice, quiet, saying his name. He closed his eyes and leaned against the counter and listened.
