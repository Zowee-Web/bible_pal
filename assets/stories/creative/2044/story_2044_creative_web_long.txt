"This is the worst Saturday ever," Cora announced to the porch, the yard, and anyone who would listen. She sat on the top step with her chin in both hands, elbows on her knees, wearing the swimsuit she'd put on an hour ago.

The trip to the water park had been canceled. Not because of a big exciting storm with thunder and lightning and drama — just a steady, gray, boring drizzle that started at dawn and showed no signs of stopping. The kind of rain that doesn't even have the courtesy to be interesting.

Her father sat in the wooden rocking chair behind her, reading a thick book with a bent cover. "It's really not so bad, Cor."

"It is so bad. It's the worst. I've been waiting all week."

He smiled into his book but didn't argue. Her father was good at that — knowing when to talk and when to just let things be.

Cora slumped further down on the step. The wood was damp under her bare legs. She watched the rain drip from the porch roof in a steady line, like a beaded curtain. Each drop landed in the flower bed below and made a tiny splash in the dark soil. Drip. Drip. Drip.

She started counting them. She got to forty-seven before she noticed something she hadn't seen before.

A spider had built a web between the corner of the porch railing and the top of the rosebush. It must have been there for days, but Cora had never stopped to look. The rain had settled on every strand in tiny, perfect droplets, and now the whole web sparkled like jewelry — like someone had draped a necklace made of glass beads in the exact shape of a spiral.

Cora leaned closer, careful not to breathe too hard. The web was bigger than her hand, stretched open. Each strand curved out from the center in a pattern so careful, so even, it looked like someone had drawn it with a compass. The spider sat right in the middle, small and brown, legs tucked in, not bothered by the rain at all.

"Dad," Cora whispered. "Come look at this."

Her father put his book face-down on the chair and knelt beside her. "Well," he said softly. "Would you look at that."

"How does something that small make something so perfect?"

"Carefully, I imagine. One strand at a time."

They watched together as a single raindrop ran down one of the long anchor threads, collecting other droplets as it went, growing bigger and brighter until it reached the bottom and fell into the rosebush leaves with a tiny tap.

Cora sat back on her heels and looked around the porch — really looked, for the first time that morning, without the grumpy filter of canceled plans.

The rain had made the grass in the front yard so green it almost glowed, like someone had turned up the color. A fat robin hopped across the lawn, cocking its head sideways, then plunging its beak into the soft earth and pulling up a long worm. Successful. Breakfast.

The old maple tree at the edge of the yard held its leaves out flat, catching rain. Water dripped from leaf to leaf in little cascades, each one a tiny waterfall.

Her cat, Biscuit, was curled in a tight warm ball on the doormat right behind the screen door. His orange fur rose and fell with slow, contented breathing. Cora reached back and scratched behind his ears. He opened one eye, purred louder, stretched one white paw out, and yawned enormously. Then he tucked it back in and went to sleep.

Inside, she could hear her mother in the kitchen. The mixer hummed, and a warm smell drifted through the screen door — cinnamon and sugar and butter. Cinnamon rolls. Her mother only made cinnamon rolls on rainy days.

Cora looked at the sparkling spider web. She looked at the bright green grass and the successful robin and sleepy Biscuit and the rain falling soft and steady on the whole neighborhood, watering everything, cleaning everything, making everything fresh.

She was still wearing her swimsuit. She'd forgotten to be upset about it.

"Okay," she said. "Maybe it's not the worst Saturday."

Her father picked his book back up. "No?"

"The web is really beautiful. And Biscuit is really cozy. And Mom is making cinnamon rolls."

"All true."

"And the grass has never been that green before."

"Mm-hm."

Cora pulled her knees up and wrapped her arms around them. A breeze came across the porch, carrying the smell of wet earth and lavender from the plant by the steps. Somewhere down the street, she could hear kids laughing — playing in the rain, probably, splashing in puddles.

"It might actually be a pretty good Saturday," she said.

Her father rocked his chair and turned a page. Biscuit purred. The rain kept falling, soft and silver, and the spider's web kept shining like a small, perfect gift that had been there all along, waiting for her to stop and see it.
