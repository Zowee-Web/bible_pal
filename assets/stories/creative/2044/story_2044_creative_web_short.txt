Cora had walked past Mr. Kim's house a thousand times. He was always out front, kneeling in his garden, pulling weeds or watering his tomato plants. He never said much. Just a small wave when Cora rode by on her bike.

Today was different. Today Mr. Kim was sitting on his porch steps, not gardening. Just sitting. His hands hung between his knees, and his garden tools lay on the sidewalk in a pile, like he'd set them down and forgotten about them.

Cora slowed her bike. She almost kept riding. But something about the tools on the sidewalk stopped her — they looked wrong there, scattered and loose.

She pulled over. "Are you okay, Mr. Kim?"

He looked up. "Oh. Hello, Cora." He rubbed his face. "My back went out this morning. Can't bend down."

Cora looked at the garden. Weeds had crept in around the tomato cages. Three ripe tomatoes hung heavy on the vine, so red they almost looked like they were glowing.

"I could pick those for you," she said. She wasn't sure why she offered. She'd never done anything like that before.

Mr. Kim studied her for a moment. "You know which ones are ready?"

"The red ones?"

He almost laughed. "Close enough. Twist gently. Don't pull."

Cora knelt in the dirt and twisted the first tomato free. It was warm from the sun and heavier than she expected. She set it in the basket Mr. Kim pointed to and reached for the next one.

It took ten minutes. She picked tomatoes, pulled the weeds she could reach, and watered the plants with the hose while Mr. Kim told her which ones were thirsty.

When she finished, her knees were dirty and her fingers smelled green and sharp. Mr. Kim handed her two tomatoes from the basket.

"For your trouble," he said.

"It wasn't trouble."

He nodded slowly. "I know. That's why I'm giving them to you."

Cora rode home with the tomatoes in her jacket pocket, warm against her side. The street looked the same as always, but her handlebars felt lighter somehow, like the bike wanted to fly.
