Arabella lay in the hammock with her arms behind her head.

The rope net swayed between two oak trees in the backyard. It creaked each time the breeze pushed it. She watched the sky through half-closed eyes.

A cloud shaped like a rabbit drifted over the neighbor's roof. Its ears stretched long and thin. Then the ears became wings, and the rabbit became a bird. Then the bird became nothing at all — just a white wisp melting into blue.

She breathed in. The hammock rocked.

Her bare feet stuck out one end. Grass brushed her toes when the hammock dipped low. She could smell the warm dirt and the honeysuckle vine along the fence.

Another cloud came. This one was thick and round, like a loaf of bread. Arabella smiled. She was a little hungry. The cloud broke slowly in half, and each half became its own shape — one a turtle, one a ship.

The turtle sailed east. The ship sailed west.

"How do they do that?" she whispered.

Nobody answered. That was fine. The backyard was quiet except for a cardinal singing somewhere in the oak above her. Two notes, then three, then two again.

A dragonfly landed on the rope beside her elbow. Its wings caught the light — green, then blue, then green again. It stayed for three breaths. Then it lifted off and was gone.

She thought about how someone had made all of this. The clouds that kept becoming new things. The cardinal that kept singing even though no one clapped. The wind that kept pushing her hammock back and forth, back and forth, like a hand she couldn't see.

The bread cloud had become a castle now. Towers and walls, high and white against the deep blue.

Arabella pulled one arm free and reached up toward it. Her fingers spread against the sky. The castle was a thousand miles away, maybe more, but from here it fit right in the palm of her hand.

She let her arm drop.

The screen door creaked open behind her. Her mother's footsteps crossed the porch.

"You've been out here a long time," her mother said.

"I'm watching the clouds change."

Her mother stood behind the hammock and looked up. "What are they right now?"

"That one's a castle. And that little one over there is a lamb."

Her mother tilted her head. "I see it."

They were both quiet for a moment. The hammock swayed. A leaf spiraled down from the oak and landed on Arabella's knee. She picked it up and twirled it between her fingers — green on one side, pale on the other.

"Can I stay out a little longer?"

"A little longer."

Her mother's footsteps went back across the porch. The screen door closed with a soft clap.

Arabella looked up again. The castle had opened. Its towers had softened and spread into something wide and flat, like a road stretching across the sky. The lamb had drifted closer to it and walked along the edge.

The cardinal sang again. Two notes, then silence.

The wind pressed the hammock sideways, and Arabella closed her eyes. She held the leaf against her chest. Behind her eyelids the sky was still there — wide and blue and full of things becoming other things, one after another, patient and unhurried.

When she opened her eyes, the lamb had crossed the road of cloud and reached the far side. It stood at the edge of blue sky, bright and small and whole.

The hammock swayed. The leaf rested on her chest. The afternoon held still.
