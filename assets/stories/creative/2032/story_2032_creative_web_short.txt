Nadine remembered the ice. That was always where it started — the memory of black ice on Route 9, the sedan spinning, the sound of her daughter screaming from the back seat.

That was seven years ago. Cleo was fine. Nadine was fine. The car had spun into a snowbank and stopped. No injuries, no damage beyond a cracked taillight.

But the fear had rooted itself somewhere deep, and it grew in the dark.

Now Nadine stood in the parking lot of the grocery store, keys in her hand, unable to get into the car. It was November. The forecast said freezing rain by evening. Her hands shook against the key fob.

A woman she didn't know walked past pushing a cart full of bags. The woman glanced at Nadine, then stopped.

"You all right?"

"I'm fine," Nadine said. Her voice sounded thin.

The woman set her cart to the side. "My name is Opal. You don't look fine."

Nadine tried to laugh. "It's a driving thing. I had an accident years ago. It wasn't even bad. I don't know why I still—" She stopped.

Opal nodded slowly. "You don't have to know why."

They stood together in the cold air. Opal didn't rush her. She didn't offer advice or a story of her own. She just stayed.

After a few minutes, Nadine pressed the unlock button. The car chirped. She opened the door, sat down, and gripped the wheel.

"I'm going to drive to my daughter's school," she said. "Six blocks."

"Six blocks," Opal repeated. "That's a real drive."

Nadine pulled out of the lot. The road was dry. The sky was gray but holding. She drove the six blocks with both hands on the wheel and arrived in the pickup line just as Cleo came through the double doors, backpack bouncing.

Cleo climbed in and buckled her seatbelt. "Mom, can we get hot chocolate?"

Nadine looked at her daughter — older now, taller, laughing about something that happened in art class — and felt the tightness in her chest loosen by one degree.

"Yeah," she said. "Let's do that."

She put the car in drive. The road ahead was ordinary and open.
