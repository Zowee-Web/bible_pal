Nadine remembered the ice. That was always where it started — black ice on Route 9, the sedan spinning, the half-second of silence before Cleo screamed from the back seat.

Seven years ago. No injuries. A cracked taillight and a dent in the quarter panel. Everyone was fine.

But the fear had rooted itself somewhere deep, and it grew in the dark like a vine that didn't need sunlight.

Every winter since, her hands went tight on the wheel. She checked the forecast three times before leaving the house. She kept a blanket and flares and a bag of cat litter in the trunk — sensible precautions that had become rituals, each one a small prayer against a thing that had already happened.

Now Nadine stood in the parking lot of the Greenfield grocery store, keys in her hand, unable to get into the car. November. Freezing rain by evening. Her hands trembled against the key fob.

She'd been standing there four minutes. She knew because she'd checked her phone twice.

A woman walked past pushing a cart of grocery bags. She glanced at Nadine, took three more steps, and turned around.

"You all right, hon?"

"I'm fine," Nadine said. Her voice sounded thin.

The woman set her cart aside and walked back. Silver hair in a low bun, green coat that had seen several winters. "My name is Opal. You don't look fine, and I don't mean that unkindly."

Nadine tried to laugh. It came out wrong. "It's a driving thing. I had an accident years ago. Nobody got hurt. I don't know why I still—" She stopped.

Opal stood beside her, hands in her pockets. "You don't have to know why."

The wind picked up across the lot. Somewhere a car horn honked, short and impatient.

"I have to pick up my daughter," Nadine said.

"How far?"

"Six blocks."

Opal nodded. "That's not nothing."

They stood together in the cold air. Opal didn't rush her. She didn't offer advice or a story of her own. She just stayed, present and unhurried, like someone who understood that company was sometimes the only useful thing.

After a few minutes, Nadine pressed the unlock button. The car chirped. She sat down and wrapped both hands around the cold steering wheel.

"Six blocks," she said through the open door.

"Six blocks," Opal repeated, like it was a real distance. Because it was.

Nadine closed the door and started the engine. She pulled out slowly, signaled left, turned onto Maple Street. The road was dry. The sky was gray but holding. She drove with both hands on the wheel and arrived in the pickup line just as the double doors opened.

Cleo spotted the car and jogged over, backpack bouncing. She climbed in and buckled her seatbelt in one practiced motion.

"Mom, can we get hot chocolate? Ava said the marshmallows are huge."

Nadine looked at her daughter. Twelve now. Taller than she expected. Cheeks pink from the cold, laughing about marshmallows.

The tightness in her chest loosened by one degree. Not gone. But shifted, like a knot worked slightly looser by patient hands.

"Yeah," Nadine said. "Let's do that."

She put the car in drive. Cleo found a song on the radio. The heater blew warm air across the dashboard. Outside, the first drops of rain — just rain, not ice — touched the windshield.

Nadine drove. The road ahead was ordinary and open, and the girl beside her was singing.
