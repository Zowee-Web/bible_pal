Warm bread and honey filled the little bakery on Plum Lane. Flour dusted the windowsills like soft snow. Outside, the village of Cloverbrook was waking up slowly. Birds called to each other from the rooftops. A small brown mouse named Pippin sat on a shelf near the oven, nibbling a crumb of rye bread. His whiskers twitched with each tiny bite. The baker, a round-cheeked woman named Gretta, hummed a song while she kneaded dough. Pippin liked mornings here. Everything smelled good and felt safe.

Cloverbrook had a bell tower right in the center of the village square. Every morning, someone rang the bell to start the day. The bell's sound was bright and clear, like a spoon tapping a glass jar. It told the farmers to head to their fields. It told the children that school would begin soon. Old Harlan had rung the bell for thirty years. He climbed the narrow stairs each morning without fail. But last week, Harlan had slipped on a wet step and hurt his ankle. Now he sat in a chair by his garden, his foot wrapped in cloth. Nobody else in the village wanted to climb those stairs. The steps were very narrow and very steep. Some people tried, but they turned back halfway up. The bell had been quiet for five whole days.

Without the bell, Cloverbrook felt different. Farmers overslept and missed the cool morning hours. Children arrived at school at all different times. Gretta burned two batches of rolls because she looking around track of the morning. "I wish that bell would ring again," she said, wiping her hands on her apron. Pippin's ears perked up. He had heard the bell every day of his small life. He missed it too. That evening, Pippin crept out of the bakery and sat on the cobblestones near the bell tower. He looked up. The tower was tall, with a pointed roof and one round window near the top. A thick rope hung inside, attached to the big brass bell. Pippin's heart beat fast. He was very small. But his paws were good at gripping, and he had climbed plenty of shelves and rafters in the bakery.

The next morning, before the first light touched the rooftops, Pippin scurried across the square. He squeezed under the tower door through a gap no bigger than a walnut. Inside, the stairs spiraled upward. Each stone step was cool under his paws. He began to climb. One step, then another. His legs were short, so each step felt like a small mountain. Halfway up, he paused and caught his breath. His tiny chest rose and fell quickly. Part of him wanted to turn around and go back to the warm bakery. But he thought about Gretta and the burned rolls. He thought about the farmers rubbing sleepy eyes. He thought about the children wandering in late. So he kept climbing. His paws gripped the rough stone. Up and up he went, round and round the spiral. Finally, he reached the top.

The bell hung above him, enormous and golden-brown. The rope dangled thick and heavy. Pippin grabbed it with both front paws. He pulled. Nothing happened. He wrapped his tail around it too and swung his whole body downward. The rope moved just a little. Pippin tried again, bouncing with all his weight. A soft sound came from the bell. Not quite a ring, but almost. He took a deep breath, scrambled higher up the rope, and then dropped. The bell swung. A clear, beautiful note rang out across Cloverbrook.

Pippin did it again. And again. Each ring echoed over the rooftops and through the open windows. Down below, Gretta looked up from her dough and smiled wide. A farmer named Josip stretched in his bed and then swung his feet to the floor. Children grabbed their school bags. The whole village began to move, like a music box winding up.

Nobody knew who had rung the bell. Gretta walked to the tower and peered inside, but Pippin had already scurried down and slipped back through the gap. She found tiny paw prints in the flour dust on the bottom step. "Well, I never," she whispered, and laughed softly.

That evening, Gretta left a small plate near the tower door. On it sat a perfect triangle of cheese and one golden raisin. "For our bell ringer," she said quietly. Pippin found the plate after sunset. He ate every bit and licked his whiskers clean.

Each morning after that, Pippin climbed the tower. His legs grew stronger. The stairs felt less steep. The rope felt less heavy. Old Harlan heard the bell from his garden chair and nodded with a peaceful smile. He didn't know it was a mouse, but he was grateful all the same.

One morning, Gretta opened her bakery window just as the bell rang. She spotted a tiny brown shape swinging on the rope through the tower's round window. She pressed her hand to her heart and grinned. "Smallest paws in Cloverbrook," she said, "and the bravest."

Pippin rode the rope down one last time, then scurried home across the square. Morning light touched the cobblestones. The smell of fresh bread drifted from the bakery door. And the bell's last note hung in the air, bright and steady, like a promise that the day had well and truly begun.
