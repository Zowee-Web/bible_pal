Bramble was a small brown mouse who lived inside the wall of the village bakery. His home was a cozy pocket between two old bricks, lined with scraps of cloth and a bit of cotton fluff. Every morning, warm bread smells drifted through the cracks and tickled his whiskers. He could smell rye bread and oat rolls and sometimes cinnamon buns. He liked to sit on the windowsill and watch people walk by with their baskets and bundles.

From that windowsill, Bramble could see the whole main street. He watched the flower seller arrange yellow and purple blooms in tin buckets. He watched children skip past on their way to the schoolhouse. A gray cat named Pepper sometimes napped on the barrel across the lane, and Bramble kept a respectful distance. But mostly, he just watched and listened and enjoyed the busy hum of the village waking up.

At the center of the village stood a tall stone tower. It rose above every rooftop, built from pale gray stones stacked carefully one on top of the other. Green moss grew in patches along its base. A copper bell hung near the top, held by a thick wooden beam. Each Sunday morning, old Mr. Garrick climbed the winding stairs inside the tower and rang the bell. Its bright sound floated over rooftops and gardens, calling everyone together. Bramble loved that sound more than anything. When the bell rang, he would close his eyes and let the hum travel through his whole tiny body, from whiskers to tail.

Mr. Garrick was a tall man with white hair and large, gentle hands. He walked with a wooden cane on weekdays, but on Sundays he always set the cane aside and climbed those stairs with care. Bramble had watched him do it many times, disappearing through the low wooden door at the base of the tower. Minutes later, the bell would sing.

One cool autumn morning, red and orange leaves tumbled along the cobblestones. Bramble scurried across the street toward the tower, his paws tapping lightly on the cold stones. Dew still clung to the grass at the edges of the path. He found a group of neighbors gathered at the bottom of the stairs. Their voices were low and concerned.

Mr. Garrick sat on a wooden bench near the door. His right ankle was wrapped in a thick white bandage. A walking stick leaned against the wall beside him. He looked up at the tower and then back down at his ankle with a quiet sigh.

"Twisted it on the garden path last evening," he told the neighbors. "Stepped wrong on a loose stone."

The stairs inside the tower were narrow and steep. They curved upward in a tight spiral, with no railing, just the rough stone wall to lean against. A woman named Hilde tried the first dozen steps and came back down, gripping the doorway. "Too narrow for me," she said, shaking her head. A young man named Pell made it a bit farther but returned, breathing hard. "The steps get even steeper near the top," he reported. Others peered through the doorway and shifted on their feet. No one else stepped forward.

"The bell won't ring today," someone said softly.

Bramble sat beneath the bench, watching all of this. His small black eyes moved from face to face. He looked up at the tower. It was very, very tall. His paws were very, very small. But he knew every crack and ledge in this village. He had climbed the bakery rafters hundreds of times. He had scurried along rain gutters and window frames and the tops of garden fences. His paws knew how to grip rough stone.

His heart beat fast. He could feel it thumping right behind his ribs. He looked at Mr. Garrick's bandaged ankle. He looked at the open doorway of the tower. He took a breath and whispered a quiet prayer. Then he ran out from under the bench and through the doorway.

The inside of the tower smelled like damp stone and old wood. Bramble's paws — no, his tiny nails clicked softly on the first step. Each stair was about as tall as his whole body. He hopped up one, then the next, then the next. His back legs pushed hard against each stone edge. The stairway curved to the left, and pale morning light came through a small window cut into the wall.

Stone by stone, step by step, Bramble went up. His tiny paws gripped each rough edge. He could feel the grit of the stone under his pads. Cool wind brushed his fur through the open windows of the tower. Each window he passed showed a different slice of the village below. Through one, he glimpsed the bakery roof. Through another, the schoolyard fence.

He did not look down. He just kept going, one paw after the next. His breathing came quick and steady. Twenty steps. Thirty steps. The stairs seemed to go on and on. The walls were closer together up here, and the steps were worn smooth in the middle from years of Mr. Garrick's boots.

Halfway up, his legs felt tired. A deep ache settled into his small muscles. He found a ledge where a stone jutted out a little farther than the rest. He climbed onto it and rested there, pressing his belly flat against the cool surface. He closed his eyes for a moment. His whiskers trembled with each breath. A feeling of warmth filled his chest, steady and full. He opened his eyes and kept climbing.

The last stretch of stairs opened onto a wooden platform at the very top. Bramble pulled himself up over the final step and stood on the worn planks. Wind moved freely up here, ruffling his fur in every direction. The copper bell hung from the thick beam just above him. It was enormous up close, bigger than a rain barrel, green with age in some spots and bright copper in others. A heavy rope hung from its side, dropping down through a hole in the platform.

Bramble ran along the wooden beam. His paws gripped the grain of the wood. He reached the place where the rope attached to the bell and jumped. His front paws caught the rope, and his back paws swung free. He dangled there for a moment, swaying gently. Then he began to swing his whole body back and forth. Forward and back. Forward and back. The rope moved. Just a little at first. Then more.

The bell tilted. Metal scraped softly against the beam. And then it rang.

One clear, beautiful note sailed out over the village.

Bramble kept swinging. His small body arched and curled with each push. The bell tilted again. Another ring, fuller this time, spreading wide across the rooftops and gardens and lanes.

Down below, people looked up with wide eyes. Hilde put her hand over her mouth. Pell pointed at the top of the tower. Then they smiled. Then they laughed and clapped. Children came running from the schoolyard to see. The flower seller stepped out from behind her buckets and shaded her eyes with one hand, grinning.

The bell rang again and again as Bramble swung with all his might. Each note rolled out like a warm wave, reaching every corner of the village. People began walking toward the tower, just as they did every Sunday. They came with smiles and shawls and children holding hands.

Mr. Garrick sat on his bench and listened. His eyes were bright. He tapped his good foot gently on the cobblestones in time with each ring.

When the last note faded into the cool autumn air, Bramble let go of the rope and landed softly on the platform. He sat for a moment, catching his breath. His legs trembled a little. Then he began the long climb back down, step by step, taking his time. The stairs felt easier going down. His paws knew the way now.

At the bottom, he stepped through the doorway into the morning. Mr. Garrick was sitting right there on the bench near the door. He cupped his hands gently, palms up, fingers curved like a little bowl. Bramble hopped right in.

Those hands were warm and steady. Bramble could feel the slow pulse in Mr. Garrick's palms beneath his paws.

"Thank you, little friend," Mr. Garrick said. "That was the bravest thing I've seen all year."

Bramble's whiskers twitched. His heart still thumped from the climb. Neighbors gathered around the bench, still smiling. Hilde set a small piece of oat roll on the bench beside Mr. Garrick's knee, and Bramble leaned over and nibbled it.

Sitting there in those warm, careful hands, with the last echo of the bell still humming in the air and the taste of oat roll on his tongue, Bramble felt perfectly at home.
