When had Phoebe last looked out the window of a bus?

She didn't mean glanced. She didn't mean the half-seeing of someone scrolling their phone with peripheral awareness of passing blocks. She meant looked — the way a child looks, with the whole face turned and the eyes soft and the world sliding past like a film nobody else in the theater is watching.

She was on the 41 crosstown, heading to her mother's apartment to help with the move. The task sat in her chest like a stone — boxes, decisions, the sad arithmetic of sorting a life into keep and donate. Her mother's hands didn't work well enough anymore to pack dishes. Her memory had begun to wander, circling back to rooms that no longer existed.

Phoebe had been dreading this day for weeks.

But the bus was slow. Traffic on Delancey, some construction detour. And because her phone was dead and the book in her bag felt too heavy to hold, she looked.

A man on the corner was teaching a girl to ride a bicycle. The girl wobbled, corrected, wobbled again. The man jogged alongside with his hands out — not touching, just ready. Close enough to catch but far enough to let her balance. The girl didn't fall.

The bus moved on.

A bakery window, fogged from inside, with a woman's hand wiping a clear circle to peer out. For a second, the woman's eye met Phoebe's through the glass. Neither of them waved. It was enough.

The bus turned. A park appeared — small, wedged between buildings, more pavement than grass. But someone had planted marigolds along the chain-link fence. Hundreds of them, blazing orange in the gray light, so vivid they looked defiant. A deliberate act of color in a place that didn't require it.

An old man on a bench was feeding pigeons from a paper bag. He tossed crumbs with precision, aiming for the smaller birds that got pushed to the edges. Keeping score. Making sure the shy ones ate.

Phoebe felt something shift behind her ribs. Not joy exactly. Something quieter. An opening, like a window pushed up an inch in a stuffy room.

She'd been keeping a list — everything she was losing. Her mother's independence. The apartment on Fairfield where she'd grown up. The kitchen with the yellow curtains, where winter light made the whole room feel like the inside of a lantern. That list was heavy and earned and true.

But the world was also doing this. Teaching children to balance. Wiping clear circles in fogged glass. Planting flowers in concrete. Making sure the smallest birds got fed. Nobody was getting paid for any of it.

The bus lurched to her stop. She stepped down onto her mother's block. The air smelled like rain and roasted nuts from the cart on the corner. Puddles held the gray sky in small, wobbly mirrors.

She stood on the sidewalk, not rushing. The apartment waited. The boxes waited.

But both lists were true. The losing and the finding. She could carry them together, and the carrying didn't mean choosing one over the other.
