When had Phoebe last looked out the window of a bus?

She didn't mean glanced. She meant looked — the way a child looks, with the whole face turned and the eyes soft and the world sliding past like a film nobody else is watching.

She was on the 41 crosstown, heading to her mother's apartment to help with the move. Boxes, decisions, the sad arithmetic of sorting a life into keep and donate. She'd been dreading it for weeks.

But the bus was slow today. Traffic on Delancey. And so she looked.

A man on the corner was teaching a girl to ride a bicycle. The girl wobbled, corrected, wobbled again. The man jogged alongside with his hands out, not touching, just ready. The girl didn't fall.

The bus moved on.

A bakery window, fogged from inside, with a woman's hand wiping a clear circle to peer out. The circle was a small portal. For a second the woman's eye met Phoebe's through the glass. Neither of them waved.

The bus turned. A park appeared — small, wedged between buildings, more pavement than grass. But someone had planted marigolds along the fence. Hundreds of them, blazing orange in the gray light. A deliberate act of color.

An old man on a bench was feeding pigeons from a paper bag. He tossed crumbs with precision, aiming for the smaller birds that got pushed aside. Keeping score. Making sure the shy ones ate.

Phoebe felt something shift behind her ribs. Not joy exactly. An opening. The way a fist uncurls when you forget you've been clenching it.

She'd been keeping a list of everything she was losing — her mother's independence, the apartment she'd grown up in, the kitchen where winter light made the whole room glow. The list was real and true.

But the world was also doing this. Teaching children to balance. Planting flowers in concrete. Making sure the smallest birds got fed.

She stepped off the bus. The air smelled like rain and roasted nuts. She stood on the sidewalk for a moment, not rushing.

Both lists were true. The losing and the finding. She could carry them both.
