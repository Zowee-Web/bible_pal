The room held forty-three people and the smell of coffee gone cold.

Mariam sat in the third row, her hands flat on her knees. The fluorescent light above her flickered once every few seconds, a pulse she had been counting since the meeting began.

The company lawyer stood at the front, reading from a prepared statement. Words like "isolated incident" and "no systemic failure" filled the room like smoke. The other employees sat still. Some nodded.

Mariam's supervisor leaned toward her. "We're almost through this," he whispered. "Just let it pass."

She had a mortgage. She had a daughter in braces. She had fourteen years with the company and a pension that vested in eleven months.

The lawyer finished. He adjusted his glasses. "Are there any questions?"

Silence.

Mariam's right hand trembled against her knee. She pressed it harder into the fabric of her slacks. The fluorescent light flickered.

She stood.

Every head turned. The leather chairs creaked as people shifted to look at her. The lawyer's pen stopped moving.

"That's not what happened," Mariam said. Her voice came out thin, and she cleared her throat. "That is not what happened, and there are six of us in this room who know it."

The lawyer set his pen down.

"I have the original report," she said. "Before it was edited. I have the timestamps. I have the emails."

Her supervisor stared at the table in front of him. Two rows ahead, a woman named Diane closed her eyes. The air vents hummed.

"The family deserves to know," Mariam said.

No one spoke. The lawyer reached for his phone. Mariam's hands had stopped trembling. She remained standing, her weight even on both feet, her badge still clipped to her collar, catching the fluorescent light each time it pulsed.
