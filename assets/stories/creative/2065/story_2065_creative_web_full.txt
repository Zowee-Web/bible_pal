The room held forty-three people and the smell of coffee gone cold.

Mariam sat in the third row, her hands flat on her knees. The fluorescent light above her flickered once every few seconds, a pulse she had been counting since the meeting began. Nineteen times so far.

The company lawyer stood at the front behind a portable lectern. He read from a prepared statement, turning pages with the ease of a man who had done this before. Words like "isolated incident" and "no systemic failure" and "moving forward with full confidence" filled the room like smoke. The other employees sat still in their rolling chairs. Some nodded. A few took notes on company-issued pads.

Mariam's supervisor leaned toward her from the next seat. His aftershave was too strong. "We're almost through this," he whispered. "Just let it pass."

She had a mortgage with twenty-two years remaining. She had a daughter in braces who wanted to go to science camp in July. She had fourteen years with the company and a pension that vested in eleven months.

The manila folder sat in her bag beneath her chair. She could feel its weight the way a person feels a stone in a shoe.

The lawyer finished reading. He adjusted his glasses and looked out across the rows. "Are there any questions or comments before we close?"

Silence. The clock on the wall showed 2:47. Someone's phone buzzed in a pocket, muffled and ignored.

Mariam's right hand trembled against her knee. She pressed it harder into the fabric of her slacks until the knuckle ridges went white. The fluorescent light flickered. Twenty.

She stood.

Every head turned. Leather chairs creaked as people shifted to look at her. The lawyer's pen stopped moving. A man in the back row lowered his coffee cup halfway to his mouth and held it there.

"That's not what happened," Mariam said.

Her voice came out thin, and she cleared her throat. The sound was loud in the quiet room.

"That is not what happened, and there are six of us in this room who know it."

The lawyer set his pen down on the lectern with a small click.

"The failure wasn't isolated," she said. "It was reported three times before the accident. I filed the first report myself on March ninth." She reached beneath her chair and pulled the manila folder from her bag. "I have the original version. Before it was edited. I have the timestamps. I have the emails telling me to revise my findings."

Her supervisor stared at the table in front of him. His jaw worked silently. Two rows ahead, a woman named Diane closed her eyes and pressed her fingertips together. The air vents hummed above them all.

"The family deserves to know," Mariam said. "Their son deserved better than a prepared statement."

No one spoke. The lawyer reached slowly for his phone. Somewhere down the hall, a printer started running, its rhythm steady and indifferent.

Mariam's hands had stopped trembling. She held the folder against her ribs. She remained standing, her weight even on both feet, her company badge still clipped to her collar. The fluorescent light pulsed once more and caught the laminated surface of her name, the small photograph, the fourteen-year-old employee number printed in blue ink beneath her face.
