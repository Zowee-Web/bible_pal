Every November, Frank set a loaf of bread on the windowsill of his apartment and left it there until morning. His wife had asked him about it once, early in their marriage. He said it was for the birds.

It wasn't for the birds.

In 1987, Frank was nineteen and living in his car in Tucson. He had forty cents and no plan. One morning he parked behind a bakery and a woman came out the back door carrying a bag of rolls they hadn't sold. She set them on the loading dock and looked at Frank sitting in the driver's seat with his window down.

She didn't say anything. She just left the bag and went inside. Frank ate three rolls and cried.

He never learned her name. He drove to Phoenix the next week, got a job washing dishes, eventually got his GED. Thirty-seven years later he was a retired postal worker with a pension and a two-bedroom apartment and a wife who loved him.

Every November he set bread on the windowsill.

Three blocks away, a woman named Delia walked to work each morning at five. She passed Frank's building in the dark. One November she noticed the bread on the sill — a whole loaf, wrapped loosely in wax paper, left out where anyone could take it.

She didn't take it. But she noticed. And the noticing did something to her. It reminded her of her father, who used to leave coins on the fence post by the road in case someone walking by needed bus fare. He'd been dead ten years.

The next morning, Delia left two bus tokens on the bench at the stop near Frank's building.

Frank never saw the tokens. Delia never saw him set out the bread. But every November, for reasons neither could explain to anyone else, both of them felt less alone.
