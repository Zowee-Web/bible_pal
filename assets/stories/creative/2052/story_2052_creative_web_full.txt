Every November, Frank Alvarez set a loaf of bread on the windowsill of his apartment and left it there until morning.

His wife, Jean, had asked about it the first year of their marriage. The bread on the windowsill was one of those habits you discover slowly.

"It's for the birds," Frank said.

Jean looked at the loaf — whole, wrapped in wax paper, set on the sill facing the alley. "That's a lot of bread for birds."

"They're hungry in November," Frank said, and his voice had something in it that made Jean let it go.

It wasn't for the birds.

In November of 1987, Frank was nineteen and living in a brown Dodge Dart in a parking lot behind a strip mall in Tucson. He had forty cents in his pocket, no job, and a gas tank so empty the needle sat below the E mark like a broken finger.

One morning — the seventh or eighth of not eating — he parked behind a bakery called Rosa's. A woman came out carrying a paper bag of day-old rolls. She was short, dark-haired, wearing a white apron dusted with flour. She set the bag on the concrete loading dock.

Then she looked up and saw Frank sitting in the Dodge with his window cracked, watching her.

She didn't speak. She didn't wave. She set the bag down and went back inside.

Frank walked to the dock and opened it. Six rolls, still soft. He ate three on the bumper of his car, crying while he ate — not loudly, just tears running into the bread, because he hadn't had food in two days and the bread was the best thing he had ever tasted.

He never went back to Rosa's. He drove to Phoenix the next week, got a job washing dishes, earned his GED at twenty-two, took the postal exam at twenty-five. By fifty he had a pension, a two-bedroom apartment, and Jean.

He never learned the woman's name. The bread was there. And he was alive.

Every November he set a loaf on the windowsill.

Three blocks east, a woman named Delia Okafor walked to her hospital shift every morning at five. One November morning she passed Frank's building and noticed the bread on the sill. A whole loaf, wrapped in wax paper, just sitting there. Available. For anyone.

She wasn't hungry. But the sight stopped her — a small act of leaving something out, of trusting that what you set down will reach whoever needs it.

It reminded her of her father. Emmanuel Okafor had been a bus driver in Cleveland for thirty years. Every morning he placed two quarters on the fence post at the end of his driveway. "For whoever's walking by and needs bus fare," he'd say. Delia never saw anyone take the quarters. But every morning they were gone by afternoon.

That evening, she bought two bus tokens and set them on the bench at the stop near Frank's building, under a small rock so the wind wouldn't take them.

Frank never saw the tokens. Delia never saw him set out the bread. Neither knew the other's name or story. But every November, Frank set bread on the sill and Delia set tokens on the bench. The gratitude moved between them like water through underground stone — invisible, steady, connecting wells that would never meet on the surface.

And every November, for reasons neither could fully explain, both of them felt less alone.
