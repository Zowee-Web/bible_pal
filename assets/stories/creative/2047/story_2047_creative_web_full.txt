Theo's backpack weighed a thousand pounds. At least it felt that way climbing the steps to his front door on Thursday evening. His legs were heavy, his eyes scratchy, his brain full of cotton.

It had been the longest day in the history of fourth grade. Math test first thing — fractions that got jumbled on paper. Soccer tryouts at lunch, running drills in the hot sun until his shins ached. Then a group project where he did most of the work while Jaylen and Mia argued about the poster background for forty minutes.

He dropped his backpack inside the front door and left it there. He didn't have the energy to hang it up.

The house was quiet. His mother worked late on Thursdays. Even the dog, a golden retriever named Biscuit, was asleep on the rug and only lifted one ear when Theo came in.

But someone had been here, getting things ready.

The porch light was on. The hallway smelled warm and good. In the kitchen, Theo found a note on the counter written on a yellow sticky note.

It said: Chicken soup on the stove. Cornbread under the foil. You worked hard today and I'm proud of you. Rest. I love you. — Mom

He peeled back the foil. Cornbread, still faintly warm. Butter and honey.

He lifted the lid on the pot. Steam rose, carrying the smell of his mother's soup — thick with egg noodles and carrots and chicken so tender it fell apart. She must have started it before work, early in the morning, chopping carrots in the dark kitchen while thinking about his long day ahead.

Theo ladled soup into a bowl and sat at the kitchen table. He ate slowly.

The warmth spread through his chest and down into his tired legs. The noodles were soft. The broth was salty and rich. Each bite felt like a sentence in a language that meant, I know you're tired. You're home.

He ate cornbread and crumbled the last piece into the soup the way he used to when he was little.

After dinner, he washed his bowl and climbed the stairs. His bed was made — sheets pulled tight, blue blanket folded at the foot. His mother had done that too.

Theo kicked off his shoes and lay down on top of the covers. The pillow was cool against his cheek. Through the window, the sky was turning purple and orange. A bird sang three notes in the maple tree, then silence.

His eyes closed. The math test and the drills and the poster drifted away, getting smaller and softer, like a song being turned down until it was nothing.

The house held him, solid and warm. His mother's note sat downstairs, and even though she wasn't here yet, he could feel her love in the soup and the cornbread and the made bed and the porch light she'd left on so he wouldn't come home to a dark house.

Theo pulled the blue blanket up to his chin. It smelled like laundry soap and home.

He slept, and rest held him gently until morning.
