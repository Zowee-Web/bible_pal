On that day, as the afternoon sun began its westward descent, two men walked the quiet road that stretched from Jerusalem toward Emmaus, which was from Jerusalem about threescore furlongs. The earth was dry beneath their sandals, dust rising with every step, while olive trees stood silent in the golden light. They were called Cleopas and his companion, both followers of Jesus, and sorrow lay heavy upon them, slowing their every stride. Their robes, worn and yet marked by the sorrows of the week, trailed by their ankles. The world round about them seemed muted, the familiar life of the countryside carrying on while their hearts remained troubled and restless. Every footfall blended with the remembrance of recent days—the multitudes in Jerusalem, the shouts, the cross, the darkness that had come after.

They communed one with another quietly, voices low, their words turning again and again unto the things that had come to pass. "Sawest thou how it ended?" the one would ask, and the other would answer, "How could he be gone?" Every question only deepened their confusion. The loss of Jesus was fresh; the hope they once bare was now a painful ache, dull but constant. All the wonders they had beheld, the words of promise, seemed to blur against the final terrible vision of the empty cross.

As they journeyed on the way, another traveller drew nigh. This stranger, walking the same road, came alongside them and matched their pace. The stranger's eyes were gentle yet searching, and his tunic dusted with the same road as theirs. But their eyes were holden that they should not know him. They glanced at the newcomer as he joined them, supposing him to be but another pilgrim leaving the holy city behind.

The stranger broke the gentle silence, saying, "What manner of communications are these that ye have one to another, as ye walk, and are sad?" His voice was calm, inviting, and it seemed to stir the air between them. The two disciples stood still, sadness settling upon them more heavily than before. Cleopas looked upon the man in wonder, and, with a trace of brokenness in his voice, he answered and said unto him, "Art thou only a stranger in Jerusalem, and hast not known the things which are come to pass there in these days?"

The stranger, with quiet patience, said, "What things?" The question seemed wellnigh past belief unto the two travellers, but they answered notwithstanding. They spake of Jesus of Nazareth, which was a prophet mighty in deed and word before God and all the people. They recounted how the chief priests and rulers delivered him to be condemned, how he was crucified, and how their hopes were brought to nought. "But we trusted that it had been he which should have redeemed Israel," they confessed. "And beside all this, to day is the third day since these things were done."

Their voices trembled as they told of the confusion of the morning. They mentioned that certain women of their company had made them astonished, going early unto the sepulchre and finding it empty. The women had said they had also seen a vision of angels, which said that he was alive. And certain of them which were with them went to the sepulchre, and found it even so as the women had said: but him they saw not. The tale poured forth, sorrow mingling with a faint, uncertain hope, but the darkness in their hearts remained unbroken.

The dusk thickened, the hues of the road shifting from bright gold to shadowed ochre. The air grew cool, and the walk grew slower still. The stranger, which had hearkened with deep attention, now spake, his tone gentle yet filled with authority. "O fools, and slow of heart to believe all that the prophets have spoken," said he, "ought not Christ to have suffered these things, and to enter into his glory?" And beginning at Moses and all the prophets, he expounded unto them in all the scriptures the things concerning himself.

Onward they journeyed, compassed about with the quiet, broken now only by the voice of the stranger. The words opened the old prophecies in a new light. He spake of the Servant which should bear griefs and be wounded for transgressions, of the promises hidden within Israel's story. His voice rose and fell, weaving together the promises and hopes of generations. The road seemed less lonely as he spake, and the two disciples felt a warmth begin to press in against the chill of evening air.

As the three travellers drew nigh unto Emmaus, the sky above them deepened into the purples and blues of dusk. The small village, with its clustered houses and lamps flickering into life, came into sight. He made as though he would have gone further, but Cleopas and his companion could not let him go. "Abide with us," they constrained him, saying, "for it is toward evening, and the day is far spent." Here upon the edge of night, they found themselves longing for his presence, moved by the words he had spoken and the peace that seemed to accompany him.

He agreed and went in to tarry with them. Within the house, lamplight cast warm shadows upon the earthen walls. The dust of the day soon settled as they laid aside their burdens and drew near unto the table. Bread was set before them, the savour thereof filling the humble room. And it came to pass, as he sat at meat with them, he took bread, and blessed it, and brake, and gave to them.

In that very moment, as the bread was broken and shared, their eyes were opened. Recognition flooded them—the stranger was Jesus himself, risen from the dead. Their breath was stayed; awe and wonder drove out every trace of grief. Yet even as understanding dawned upon them, he vanished out of their sight. Only the broken bread remained, and the echoes of his words lingered in the lamp-lit stillness.

Cleopas and his companion looked one upon another, still astonished. "Did not our heart burn within us, while he talked with us by the way, and while he opened to us the scriptures?" they said. The warmth that had grown within them now pulsed as fire, a living hope restored. Suddenly there was no thought for weariness or the lateness of the hour. Rising up, they left the house, the cool night air pressing against them as they hastened back toward Jerusalem.

Their steps were swift upon the road now, as the fire of recognition and joy carried them forward. The lights of Jerusalem beckoned in the distance as they retraced the road they once walked in sorrow. Every landmark seemed transfigured; every shadow softened by their new-found hope. Arriving in the city, they found the eleven gathered together, and them that were with them. The room was filled with tidings, as the disciples cried out, "The Lord is risen indeed, and hath appeared to Simon!"

Cleopas and his companion entered in, eager to add their voices unto the growing testimony. They told of all that had befallen them in the way. They told how Jesus had walked with them. They told how he had opened unto them the scriptures. And above all, they told how he was known of them in breaking of bread. Their sorrow and confusion, once so heavy, were now changed for wonder, and for a hope that would not fade. In the quiet of that upper room, faith and astonishment were woven together. Through shared words and remembrances, the truth became firm among them. The Lord was alive — known in the breaking of bread.
