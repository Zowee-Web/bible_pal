King Agrippa came into the audience hall with Bernice, and the chief captains and the principal men of the city stood as they entered. The room had been arranged for hearing. Festus the governor was already seated. Servants moved along the walls with quiet feet. When the king and his sister had taken their places, the doors at the far end opened, and the prisoner was led in.

Paul stood before them in his chains. The iron at his wrists made a small sound when he moved. The hall went quiet.

Agrippa looked at him. "You may speak for yourself."

Paul stretched out his hand. The chain ran a little way along his forearm and stopped. He began.

"I count myself happy, King Agrippa, to make my defense before you today, especially because you are expert in all customs and questions which are among the Jews. I beg you to hear me patiently."

He spoke of his youth among his own nation. He spoke of his life as a Pharisee, the strictest sect of his religion. He said the thing he was being judged for was the hope of the promise God had made to the fathers. He looked steadily at the king as he spoke.

Then he spoke of what he had done before.

"I myself most certainly thought that I ought to do many things contrary to the name of Jesus of Nazareth. I shut up many of the saints in prisons. When they were put to death I gave my vote against them. I punished them in the synagogues. I tried to make them blaspheme. Being exceedingly enraged, I persecuted them even to foreign cities."

The hall listened. Bernice did not move. A chief captain near the wall shifted his weight from one foot to the other.

"Whereupon as I traveled to Damascus with the authority and commission from the chief priests, at noon, O king, I saw on the way a light from the sky, brighter than the sun, shining around me and those who traveled with me."

He paused. The room held still.

"When we had all fallen to the earth, I heard a voice saying to me in the Hebrew language, 'Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me? It is hard for you to kick against the goads.' I said, 'Who are you, Lord?' He said, 'I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting. But arise, and stand on your feet. I have appeared to you for this purpose: to appoint you a servant and a witness, delivering you from the people and from the Gentiles, to whom I send you, to open their eyes, that they may turn from darkness to light.'"

Paul lowered his hand a moment, then lifted it again.

"Therefore, King Agrippa, I was not disobedient to the heavenly vision. I declared it first at Damascus, and at Jerusalem, and throughout all the country of Judea, and also to the Gentiles, that they should repent and turn to God. For this reason the Jews seized me in the temple and tried to kill me. Having obtained the help that is from God, I stand to this day testifying both to small and great, saying nothing but what the prophets and Moses said would happen — how the Christ must suffer, and how, by the resurrection of the dead, he would be first to proclaim light both to these people and to the Gentiles."

Festus rose half from his chair. His voice broke across the hall.

"Paul, you are crazy! Your great learning is driving you insane!"

Paul turned to him. He did not raise his voice.

"I am not crazy, most excellent Festus, but boldly declare words of truth and reasonableness. The king knows of these things, to whom I speak freely. This has not been done in a corner."

He turned then to Agrippa. His chained hand fell back to his side. The room narrowed to two men.

"King Agrippa, do you believe the prophets? I know that you believe."

Agrippa's mouth tightened. He gave a half smile that did not reach his eyes.

"With a little persuasion are you trying to make me a Christian?"

"I pray to God," Paul said, "that whether with little or with much, not only you, but also all that hear me today, might become such as I am, except for these bonds."

The chain stirred at his wrist as he lowered his arm.

The king rose. The governor rose. Bernice rose. Those who sat with them rose. They withdrew from the hall together. In the corridor beyond, away from the prisoner's hearing, the chief captains spoke to one another.

"This man does nothing worthy of death or of bonds."

Agrippa stopped, and looked at Festus, and spoke quietly.

"This man might have been set free if he had not appealed to Caesar."

The doors closed behind them, and the hall fell still.
