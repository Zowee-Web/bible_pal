The audience hall at Caesarea had been prepared for a hearing. Lamps burned along the side walls. Festus the governor was already set down at the front when the doors opened and king Agrippa came in with Bernice. They came in with great pomp. Their attendants walked before them. Their robes carried the colors of the house of Herod. The chief captains rose. The principal men of the city rose. The room watched them take their seats.

When they had settled, Festus turned and gave the order, and the prisoner was led in.

Paul stood before them in his chains. The iron at his wrists made a small sound when he moved. A guard stood at his shoulder. The hall went quiet.

Agrippa looked upon him for a long moment. Then he spake.

"Thou art permitted to speak for thyself."

Paul stretched forth his hand. The chain ran a little way along his forearm and stopped at the limit of its length. He began.

"I think myself happy, king Agrippa, because I shall answer for myself this day before thee touching all the things whereof I am accused of the Jews, especially because I know thee to be expert in all customs and questions which are among the Jews. Wherefore I beseech thee to hear me patiently."

His voice carried evenly across the hall. Bernice sat without moving. Festus listened with his chin lowered.

"My manner of life from my youth, which was at the first among mine own nation at Jerusalem, know all the Jews. They knew me from the beginning, if they would testify, that after the most straitest sect of our religion I lived a Pharisee. And now I stand and am judged for the hope of the promise made of God unto our fathers, unto which promise our twelve tribes, instantly serving God day and night, hope to come. For which hope's sake, king Agrippa, I am accused of the Jews! Why should it be thought a thing incredible with you, that God should raise the dead?"

He paused. Agrippa's eyes did not leave his face.

Then Paul spake of what he had done before.

"I verily thought with myself that I ought to do many things contrary to the name of Jesus of Nazareth. Which thing I also did in Jerusalem. Many of the saints did I shut up in prison, having received authority from the chief priests. And when they were put to death, I gave my voice against them. And I punished them oft in every synagogue, and compelled them to blaspheme. And being exceedingly mad against them, I persecuted them even unto strange cities."

The hall listened. A chief captain near the wall shifted his weight from one foot to the other. The lamps hissed faintly along the walls. Bernice's fan lay folded on her lap. No man whispered.

Paul drew a breath. When he spake again, his voice changed. It was lower. It was steadier. He was no longer reciting his history. He was telling a story he had told before, and the room could hear that he had never wearied of telling it.

"Whereupon as I went to Damascus with authority and commission from the chief priests, at midday, O king, I saw in the way a light from heaven, above the brightness of the sun, shining round about me and them which journeyed with me."

He let the words hang. Festus's mouth pressed a little tighter.

"And when we were all fallen to the earth, I heard a voice speaking unto me, and saying in the Hebrew tongue, 'Saul, Saul, why persecutest thou me? It is hard for thee to kick against the pricks.'"

The Hebrew name sat strangely in the Greek hall. The chief captains looked one upon another.

"And I said, 'Who art thou, Lord?' And he said, 'I am Jesus whom thou persecutest. But rise, and stand upon thy feet: for I have appeared unto thee for this purpose, to make thee a minister and a witness both of these things which thou hast seen, and of those things in the which I will appear unto thee, delivering thee from the people, and from the Gentiles, unto whom now I send thee, to open their eyes, and to turn them from darkness to light, and from the power of Satan unto God, that they may receive forgiveness of sins, and inheritance among them which are sanctified by faith that is in me.'"

Paul lowered his hand. The chain settled. The room sat with the strangeness of what it had just heard. A voice on a road, speaking from a light. A man upon the ground, knelt low, hearkening. A commission given. None of it belonged in the orderly columns of a Roman record. None of it could be made small.

He lifted his hand again.

"Whereupon, O king Agrippa, I was not disobedient unto the heavenly vision. But shewed first unto them of Damascus, and at Jerusalem, and throughout all the coasts of Judaea, and then to the Gentiles, that they should repent and turn to God, and do works meet for repentance. For these causes the Jews caught me in the temple, and went about to kill me."

He paused once more. Then he spake the heart of it.

"Having therefore obtained help of God, I continue unto this day, witnessing both to small and great, saying none other things than those which the prophets and Moses did say should come — that Christ should suffer, and that he should be the first that should rise from the dead, and should shew light unto the people, and to the Gentiles."

For a moment the hall was very still. The lamps burned. The chain at Paul's wrist did not move.

Then Festus brake.

He rose half from his chair. His voice came out loud, almost ragged.

"Paul, thou art beside thyself; much learning doth make thee mad."

The chief captains turned to look upon the governor. Agrippa did not turn. He kept his eyes upon Paul.

Paul did not lift his voice.

"I am not mad, most noble Festus; but speak forth the words of truth and soberness. For the king knoweth of these things, before whom also I speak freely. For I am persuaded that none of these things are hidden from him, for this thing was not done in a corner."

Then he turned unto Agrippa. His chained hand fell back unto his side. The room narrowed. The chief captains were still there. Bernice was still there. Festus was still there. But the air between the king and the prisoner had gone tight, and the rest of the hall had become a watching shadow.

"King Agrippa, believest thou the prophets? I know that thou believest."

Agrippa's mouth tightened. He gave a half smile that did not reach his eyes. He answered carefully.

"Almost thou persuadest me to be a Christian?"

Paul did not move. He spake as a man who had nothing left to spend and was spending it.

"I would to God, that not only thou, but also all that hear me this day, were both almost, and altogether such as I am, except these bonds."

The chain stirred at his wrist as he lowered his arm. Bernice looked upon the floor.

The king arose.

The governor arose. Bernice arose with them, gathering her robe. They that sat with them arose, and the room arose, and the audience hall emptied row by row through the wide doors at the far end. The guard at Paul's shoulder put a hand to his elbow and waited.

In the corridor beyond, where the prisoner could not hear them, the chief captains and the principal men spake among themselves in low voices. The same words moved through them, repeated, weighed.

"This man doeth nothing worthy of death or of bonds."

Agrippa walked a few steps further. Then he stopped. He turned unto Festus. His voice was low and even, and it carried the weight of a verdict that came too late.

"This man might have been set at liberty, if he had not appealed unto Caesar."

The governor did not answer. They walked on together down the corridor, and the great doors of the audience hall closed behind them, and within the empty hall the lamps along the walls burned on in the quiet evening still.
