The writer of the letter to the Hebrews said this to the people of the church.

He spoke of two mountains.

He spoke of the first mountain — the one their fathers had come to in the wilderness. Mount Sinai.

You have not come to that mountain, the writer said. You have not come to the mountain that might be touched, and that burned with fire, and to blackness, and to darkness, and to storm. You have not come to the sound of the trumpet, and the voice of words, which those who heard it begged that not one more word should be spoken to them.

For they could not stand the commandment that had been given to them then — "If even an animal touches the mountain, it shall be stoned."

So fearful was the appearance of the mountain, the writer said, that even Moses himself had said, "I am terrified and trembling."

That was the mountain their fathers had come to.

But you have not come to that mountain.

You have come to another.

You have come to Mount Zion, and to the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem. You have come to innumerable multitudes of angels, to the festal gathering and to the assembly of the firstborn who are enrolled in heaven. You have come to God the Judge of all, and to the spirits of just men made perfect. You have come to Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant, and to the blood of sprinkling that speaks better than that of Abel.

The writer set the two mountains before them and let them be seen at the same time.

This was the gathering they had come to. This was the city.

The fire of Sinai was no longer the gate where the people stood. The gathering of Zion was where they had come.
