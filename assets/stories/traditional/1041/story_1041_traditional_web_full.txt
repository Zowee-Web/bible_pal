A man from the family of Levi married a woman from the same family. They lived in Egypt, where all the Hebrew people were slaves. The king of Egypt — Pharaoh — was cruel. He was afraid the Hebrew people were becoming too many, too strong. So he gave a terrible order: every baby boy born to the Hebrews must be thrown into the river.

Into that dangerous time, a baby was born. A boy. His mother held him and looked at his face. He was beautiful. She made up her mind — she would not let anyone hurt him.

She hid him in the house for three months. When he cried, she rocked him. When he fussed, she fed him quickly. She kept the windows covered and the door shut tight.

But babies grow. They get louder. Their cries carry farther. After three months, she could not hide him anymore. Someone would hear. Someone would report them. She had to think of something else.

She found a basket made of papyrus reeds — the same kind of reeds that grew thick and tall along the riverbank. She covered the outside with tar and pitch, pressing it into every gap and crack until no water could get through. She lined the inside with soft cloth.

Then she did the hardest thing a mother could do. She laid her baby in the basket. She wrapped him snug and warm. She kissed his forehead. Then she carried the basket down to the river and set it gently among the reeds at the water's edge.

The basket bobbed on the slow current. The tall reeds hid it from anyone walking by. The baby looked up at the blue sky and the green stalks swaying over him.

The baby's older sister stayed behind, hiding nearby. She crouched in the reeds where no one could see her. She kept her eyes on the basket. Her job was to watch and wait.

The sun climbed higher. The river sparkled. Dragonflies hummed over the water. The baby was quiet for now.

Then voices. Footsteps on the path. Pharaoh's daughter — the princess herself — came down to the river to bathe. Her servant girls walked along the bank beside her, carrying towels and oils.

The princess stopped. She had spotted something in the reeds. A small basket, floating among the green stalks. "What is that?" she said. She sent one of her servants to wade in and bring it to her.

The servant carried the basket up the bank and set it down. The princess knelt and opened the lid. Inside was a baby boy, crying. His face was scrunched up and red. Tears ran down his round cheeks. His tiny fingers opened and closed.

The princess's heart melted. She reached in and touched his soft skin. "This is one of the Hebrews' children," she said quietly. She knew what her father had ordered. But she could not turn away from a crying baby.

That was when the big sister made her move. She had been watching everything from her hiding place. Her heart beat like a drum in her chest. She was just a girl — talking to a princess was terrifying. But she stepped out of the reeds and walked right up to Pharaoh's daughter.

"Shall I go and call a nurse for you from the Hebrew women," she said, "so she may nurse the child for you?"

The princess looked at this brave girl. "Go," she said.

The sister turned and ran. Her sandals slapped the dirt path. She ran all the way home and burst through the door. "Come!" she told her mother. "Come quickly!"

The mother came. She stood before Pharaoh's daughter, trying to keep her face calm, trying not to stare at her own baby in the princess's arms.

"Take this child away and nurse him for me," the princess said. "I will give you your wages."

The mother took the baby. Her baby. She held him against her chest and felt his warmth, his breathing, his heartbeat against hers. He was safe. He was alive. And she would be the one to raise him, at least for now.

The mother took the child home and nursed him. She fed him and sang to him and watched him grow from a baby into a little boy. She told him about God. She told him about his people.

When the child was old enough, his mother brought him to Pharaoh's daughter. He became her son. She named him Moses. "Because I drew him out of the water," she said.

The baby from the basket now lived in the palace. But he would never forget where he came from — or the brave mother and sister who saved his life.
