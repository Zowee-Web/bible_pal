The third year of Belshazzar was full of feasting, and the night of the feast was the loudest of them all. A thousand of his lords reclined along the long tables. The lamps stood in rows above their heads. The music carried from the corners of the hall, the horn and the lyre and the harp, and the wine went round among the couches without pause. The king sat at the high seat with his cup in his hand. He lifted it up. He drank wine before them all, and his lords drank with him.

While he tasted the wine, a thought came into him. He gave the commandment. Servants ran from the hall and down the long stair into the storehouses beneath the palace, and they came back through the hall slowly, their arms full of the gold and silver vessels of the house of God, which Nebuchadnezzar his father had taken out of the temple in Jerusalem. The lords turned to behold them as they came. The vessels were set among the cups of the feast. The lamplight caught them. The king lifted up one of the holy cups. His lords lifted theirs. They drank the wine therein, and as they drank they praised the gods of gold, and of silver, of brass, of iron, of wood, and of stone. The names of the dead gods went up into the painted ceiling. The music kept time beneath them.

In the same hour came forth fingers of a man's hand.

They came out beside the candlestick, against the plaister of the wall of the king's palace. There was no arm behind them. There was no body. There were only the fingers, moving in the light, and they began to write. The king saw the part of the hand that wrote. The cup stayed at his lip and moved not.

The fingers worked deliberately, unhurried, forming letters in the soft plaister as a scribe might form them in wax. One letter. Then the next. The lamp beside them flickered not. The hand cast no shadow.

His hands began to shake against the gold of the cup. A drop of wine ran down the back of his wrist and pooled in the hollow above the cup's rim. His countenance was changed in him. The flush of the wine drained away. His thoughts troubled him. The joints of his loins were loosed beneath his robe. His knees smote one against another. He could not hear them smite above the music, yet he heard it in his own body.

He cried aloud. His voice brake across the hall. The music stopped mid-note. The harpers laid their harps against the columns. The pipers lowered their pipes. The chamber fell into a quiet that was not yet silence — the cups yet upon the tables, the lords yet half-reclined, the wine yet in the holy vessels — and into that quiet the king's voice went forth again. "Bring in the wise men. Bring in the enchanters and the astrologers and the soothsayers. Bring them now."

Servants ran. The lords stayed upon their couches. Their cups were yet in their hands. Their eyes were upon the wall. The fingers had finished and were no more there. Only the letters remained, dark in the soft plaister beside the candlestick.

The wise men of Babylon came in. They came in from their houses and their watch-chambers, robed and stumbling, blinking against the lamplight of the hall. The king sat forward upon his seat as they came. His voice was loud and unsteady. "Whosoever shall read this writing, and shew me the interpretation thereof, shall be clothed with scarlet, and have a chain of gold about his neck. He shall be the third ruler in the kingdom."

The wise men gathered before the wall. They lifted up their hands toward the letters. They turned one to another. They turned back. They studied the shapes thereof. They could not read them. They could not make known the interpretation unto the king. One after another they stepped away and stood with their heads bowed.

The king was greatly troubled. His countenance was changed in him a second time. His lords were astonied. The wine in the holy vessels was untouched now, and the lords nearest the king began to lower their eyes from his face. From his face to the table. From the table to the floor. They lifted them not again. One of them set down his cup without looking. The cup rang once against the wood and was still. No man turned at the sound.

Then came the queen into the banquet house.

She had heard the cry from her own chambers, and she crossed the long hall between the tables of the lords. Her step was even. She looked not upon the vessels. She looked not upon the wine. She came up before the king, and her voice was steady against the trembling of his.

"O king, live for ever. Let not thy thoughts trouble thee, neither let thy countenance be changed. There is a man in thy kingdom in whom is the spirit of the holy gods. In the days of thy father, light and understanding and wisdom were found in him. The king, Nebuchadnezzar thy father, made him master of the magicians and the enchanters. Now let Daniel be called. He will shew the interpretation."

The king sent for him.

He came up through the hall, an old man brought through the wine-stained lords until he stood before the young king. He was unhurried. He looked upon the vessels on the table. He looked upon the candlestick and the wall beside it. Then he looked upon the king.

The king spake first. His voice yet had the unsteadiness on it. "If thou canst read the writing, and make known unto me the interpretation thereof, thou shalt be clothed with scarlet, and have a chain of gold about thy neck, and shalt be the third ruler in the kingdom."

Daniel moved not toward the gifts. He answered the king, and his voice rose not. "Let thy gifts be to thyself, and give thy rewards to another. Yet I will read the writing unto the king, and make known unto him the interpretation."

He spake of Nebuchadnezzar. He spake of the greatness that the most high God had given the king's father, and of the humbling that came when the father's heart was lifted up. He spake of grass like oxen and of the dew of the sky and of a man learning, in the long hours under heaven, who ruleth in the kingdom of men. Then he turned his face unto Belshazzar.

"Thou, his son, hast not humbled thine heart, though thou knewest all this. They have brought the vessels of his house before thee, and thou and thy lords have drunk wine therein. Thou hast praised the gods of silver and gold, of brass, iron, wood, and stone, which see not, nor hear, nor know. The God in whose hand thy breath is, and whose are all thy ways, hast thou not glorified. Then was the part of the hand sent from before him, and this writing was inscribed."

He stepped toward the wall.

The lamplight fell across the letters. Daniel read them aloud, one by one, into the hush of the hall. "Mene. Mene. Tekel. Upharsin."

He turned back unto the king. His voice carried its verdict into a room that knew it was true. "Mene: God hath numbered thy kingdom, and brought it to an end. Tekel: thou art weighed in the balances, and art found wanting. Peres: thy kingdom is divided, and given to the Medes and Persians."

The hall was quiet. The king spake not for a long moment. Then he gave the commandment. They clothed Daniel with scarlet. They put a chain of gold about his neck. The proclamation was made concerning him. Daniel received the scarlet and the chain, and he put not on the look of a ruler.

The king sat on at his high seat among the holy vessels. The lamps burned above the table. The letters were yet upon the wall beside the candlestick. Beyond the painted ceiling the city of Babylon lay open under the night, and the long quiet of what was coming settled over the hall, and the wine in the cups of the sanctuary cooled untouched in the lamplight where the music had been.
