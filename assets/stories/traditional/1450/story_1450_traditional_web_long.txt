The third year of Belshazzar was full of feasting, and the night of the feast was the loudest of them all. A thousand of his lords reclined along the long tables. The lamps stood in rows above their heads. The music carried from the corners of the hall, the horn and the lyre and the harp, and the wine went around among the couches without pause. The king sat at the high seat with his cup in his hand. He lifted it. He drank wine before them all, and his lords drank with him.

While he tasted the wine, a thought came into him. He gave the command. Servants ran from the hall and down the long stair into the storerooms beneath the palace, and they came back through the hall slowly, their arms full of the gold and silver vessels of the house of God, which Nebuchadnezzar his father had taken out of the temple in Jerusalem. The lords turned to watch them come. The vessels were set among the cups of the feast. The lamplight caught them. The king lifted one of the holy cups. His lords lifted theirs. They drank the wine from them, and as they drank they praised the gods of gold and of silver, of bronze, of iron, of wood, and of stone. The names of the dead gods went up into the painted ceiling. The music kept time beneath them.

In the same hour the fingers of a man's hand came forth.

They came out beside the lampstand, against the plaster of the wall of the king's palace. There was no arm behind them. There was no body. There were only the fingers, moving in the light, and they began to write. The king saw the part of the hand that wrote. His cup stayed at his lip and did not move.

The fingers worked deliberately, unhurried, forming letters in the soft plaster as a scribe might form them in wax. One letter. Then the next. The lamp beside them did not flicker. The hand cast no shadow.

His hands began to shake against the gold of the cup. A drop of wine ran down the back of his wrist and pooled in the hollow above the cup's rim. His face was changed in him. The flush of the wine drained out of it. His thoughts troubled him. The joints of his hips were loosened beneath his robe. His knees struck one against another. He could not hear them strike above the music, but he heard it in his own body.

He cried aloud. His voice broke across the hall. The music stopped mid-note. The harpists set their harps against the columns. The pipers lowered their pipes. The room fell into a quiet that was not yet silence — the cups still on the tables, the lords still half-reclined, the wine still in the holy vessels — and into that quiet the king's voice went again. "Bring in the wise men. Bring in the enchanters and the astrologers and the soothsayers. Bring them now."

Servants ran. The lords stayed on their couches. Their cups were still in their hands. Their eyes were on the wall. The fingers had finished and were no longer there. Only the letters remained, dark in the soft plaster beside the lampstand.

The wise men of Babylon came in. They came in from their houses and their watch-rooms, robed and stumbling, blinking against the lamplight of the hall. The king sat forward on his seat as they came. His voice was loud and unsteady. "Whoever shall read this writing, and show me its interpretation, shall be clothed with purple, and have a chain of gold around his neck. He shall be the third ruler in the kingdom."

The wise men gathered before the wall. They lifted their hands toward the letters. They turned to one another. They turned back. They studied the shapes. They could not read them. They could not make the interpretation known to the king. One after another they stepped away and stood with their heads bowed.

The king was greatly troubled. His face was changed in him a second time. His lords were perplexed. The wine in the holy vessels was untouched now, and the lords nearest the king began to lower their eyes from his face. From his face to the table. From the table to the floor. They did not lift them again. One of them set down his cup without looking. The cup rang once against the wood and was still. No one turned at the sound.

Then the queen came into the banquet house.

She had heard the cry from her own rooms, and she crossed the long hall between the tables of the lords. Her step was even. She did not look at the vessels. She did not look at the wine. She came up before the king, and her voice was steady against the trembling of his.

"O king, live forever. Let not your thoughts trouble you, nor your face be changed. There is a man in your kingdom in whom is the spirit of the holy gods. In the days of your father, light and understanding and wisdom were found in him. The king, Nebuchadnezzar your father, made him master of the magicians and the enchanters. Now let Daniel be called. He will show the interpretation."

The king sent for him.

He came up through the hall, an old man brought through the wine-stained lords until he stood before the young king. He was unhurried. He looked at the vessels on the table. He looked at the lampstand and the wall beside it. Then he looked at the king.

The king spoke first. His voice still had the unsteadiness on it. "If you can read the writing and make known to me its interpretation, you shall be clothed with purple, and have a chain of gold around your neck, and shall be the third ruler in the kingdom."

Daniel did not move toward the gifts. He answered the king, and his voice did not rise. "Let your gifts be to yourself, and give your rewards to another. Yet I will read the writing to the king, and make known to him the interpretation."

He spoke of Nebuchadnezzar. He spoke of the greatness that the Most High God had given the king's father, and of the humbling that came when the father's heart was lifted up. He spoke of grass like oxen and of the dew of the sky and of a man learning, in the long hours under heaven, who rules in the kingdom of men. Then he turned his face to Belshazzar.

"You, his son, have not humbled your heart, though you knew all this. They have brought the vessels of his house before you, and you and your lords have drunk wine from them. You have praised the gods of silver and gold, of bronze, iron, wood, and stone, which do not see, nor hear, nor know. The God in whose hand your breath is, and whose are all your ways, you have not glorified. Therefore the part of the hand was sent from before him, and this writing was inscribed."

He stepped toward the wall.

The lamplight fell across the letters. Daniel read them aloud, one by one, into the hush of the hall. "Mene. Mene. Tekel. Upharsin."

He turned back to the king. His voice carried its verdict into a room that knew it was true. "Mene: God has numbered your kingdom, and brought it to an end. Tekel: you are weighed in the balances, and are found wanting. Peres: your kingdom is divided, and given to the Medes and Persians."

The hall was quiet. The king did not speak for a long moment. Then he gave the order. They clothed Daniel with purple. They put a chain of gold around his neck. The proclamation was made concerning him. Daniel received the purple and the chain, and he did not put on the look of a ruler.

The king sat on at his high seat among the holy vessels. The lamps burned above the table. The letters were still on the wall beside the lampstand. Beyond the painted ceiling the city of Babylon lay open under the night, and the long quiet of what was coming settled over the hall, and the wine in the cups of the sanctuary cooled untouched in the lamplight where the music had been.
