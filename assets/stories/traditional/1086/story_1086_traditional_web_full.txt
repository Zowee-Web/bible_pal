As Jesus walked through Jerusalem with his disciples, he passed a man who had been blind from birth. The man sat by the side of the road near the temple, his hands resting on his knees, his face turned toward the sun. He had never seen it — had never seen anything. Not the stones of the temple, not the faces of his parents, not the sky above the city. His world was darkness, and it had always been darkness.

The disciples looked at the man and asked Jesus a question. "Rabbi, who sinned — this man or his parents — that he was born blind?"

Jesus answered, "Neither this man sinned, nor his parents. He was born blind so that the works of God might be displayed in him."

He stopped walking. He knelt down in the dust beside the man. He spat on the ground and mixed the dirt and the moisture with his fingers, working it into a smooth clay. Then he spread the clay over the man's eyes, pressing it gently against his closed lids.

"Go," Jesus said. "Wash in the pool of Siloam."

The man rose to his feet. He stood for a moment in the road, the clay drying on his face, the warmth of the sun on his skin. Then he turned and made his way through the streets of Jerusalem — guided by sound, by the press of bodies around him, by the slope of the ground under his feet. He reached the pool of Siloam. The water was cool and still. He knelt at the stone edge, cupped water in his hands, and washed the clay from his eyes.

And he could see.

Light poured in. The world sharpened around him — the blue of the water, the gray of the stone, the green of the trees along the wall. He saw his own hands, wet and trembling, for the first time. He saw the sky. He stood at the edge of the pool, blinking, turning his head slowly, taking in the astonishing flood of shapes and colors that was the world.

He walked back through the streets, and his neighbors were the first to notice. "Isn't this the man who used to sit and beg?" they said. Some said it was him. Others said no, it was only someone who looked like him.

The man said, "I am the one."

"Then how were your eyes opened?"

He told them everything. "A man called Jesus made clay and put it on my eyes, and told me to go to the pool of Siloam and wash. I went and washed, and now I can see."

They brought him to the Pharisees. The Pharisees questioned him carefully — how it had happened, who had done it, what the man had said to him. Some of the Pharisees said, "This man Jesus cannot be from God, because he does not keep the Sabbath." Others said, "How can a man who is a sinner do such signs?" They were divided among themselves.

They turned to the man again. "What do you say about him, since it was your eyes he opened?"

The man answered simply. "He is a prophet."

The Pharisees did not accept this. They called the man's parents and questioned them. "Is this your son? Was he born blind? How does he now see?"

The parents were afraid. "We know that this is our son," they said, "and that he was born blind. But how he now sees, we do not know. Ask him. He is of age."

The Pharisees called the man back a second time. "Give glory to God," they said. "We know that this man Jesus is a sinner."

The man who had been blind answered them. "Whether he is a sinner, I do not know. One thing I do know: I was blind, and now I see."

They argued with him. They pressed him. They accused him. And finally, because he would not change his answer, they threw him out.

When Jesus heard that the man had been cast out, he went and found him.

"Do you believe in the Son of God?" Jesus asked.

The man looked at him — looked at him with the eyes that had been opened, eyes that had never seen a face until that morning. "Who is he, Lord," he said, "that I might believe in him?"

Jesus said, "You have seen him. He is the one speaking with you."

The man's face changed. Something settled in his expression — not surprise, but recognition. As though the light that had entered his eyes had now reached something deeper.

"Lord, I believe," he said.

And he worshiped him.
