The streets of Jerusalem were busy that morning. People moved between the market stalls and the temple gates, carrying bread, leading animals, calling to one another across the narrow lanes. The sun was already warm, and the light fell in sharp angles between the stone buildings, leaving pools of shadow along the walls.

Jesus and his disciples were walking through the city when they came upon a man sitting by the side of the road. He had been blind from birth. His eyes were closed, his face turned slightly upward, as though listening to the world around him. His hands rested on his knees. A bowl sat in the dust beside him. He was a beggar — known to the people of the neighborhood, seen every day in the same spot, part of the landscape of the street the way a stone or a doorpost is part of it. He had never seen the temple. He had never seen the sky. He had never seen the face of another human being. His world was sound and smell and touch, and it had always been that way.

The disciples looked at him and asked Jesus a question. "Rabbi," they said, "who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?"

It was a common question. People assumed that suffering like this had a cause — that someone had done something wrong, and the blindness was the consequence.

Jesus answered, "Neither this man sinned, nor his parents. He was born blind so that the works of God might be displayed in him. We must do the works of him who sent me while it is still day. Night is coming, when no one can work."

He stopped walking. He knelt down in the dust beside the man. He spat on the dry ground and mixed the dirt with the moisture, working it between his fingers until it became a smooth, dark clay. Then he leaned forward and spread the clay over the man's eyes, pressing it gently against his closed lids. The man did not flinch. He sat still, the clay cool and wet on his skin.

"Go," Jesus said. "Wash in the pool of Siloam."

The man rose. He stood in the road for a moment, the clay drying on his face, the warmth of the sun on his shoulders. Then he turned and began to walk. He moved through the streets of Jerusalem guided by sound — the tap of a stick, the scrape of sandals on stone, the voices of vendors and children. He reached the pool of Siloam, which was cut into the stone at the lower end of the city. The water was cool and clear. He knelt at the edge, dipped his hands, and washed the clay from his eyes.

And he could see.

Light flooded in. The world sharpened around him in a rush of color and shape — the blue of the water, the gray of the stone, the green leaves of the trees along the wall, the brown of his own hands dripping in front of his face. He looked up and saw the sky for the first time — vast, bright, impossibly open. He looked down and saw his reflection trembling in the surface of the pool. He stood and turned in a slow circle, looking at everything, his mouth open, his breath fast and shallow.

He walked back through the streets, and his neighbors were the first to notice. "Isn't this the man who used to sit and beg?" some of them said. Others looked at him and shook their heads. "No, it just looks like him." But the man said, "I am the one. It's me."

"Then how were your eyes opened?" they asked.

He told them plainly. "A man called Jesus made clay, put it on my eyes, and told me to wash in the pool of Siloam. I went, I washed, and now I can see."

"Where is he now?" they asked.

"I don't know," he said.

They brought him to the Pharisees, because the healing had happened on the Sabbath. The Pharisees questioned him: how had it happened, who had done it, what had been said. The man told them what he had told the neighbors — the clay, the washing, the sight. Some of the Pharisees said, "This man Jesus cannot be from God, because he does not keep the Sabbath." Others said, "But how can a man who is a sinner perform such signs?" They could not agree.

They turned to the man. "What do you say about him, since it was your eyes he opened?"

The man said, "He is a prophet."

The Pharisees did not accept this. They sent for his parents. "Is this your son?" they asked. "Was he really born blind? How is it that he can now see?"

The parents were frightened. They knew that the Pharisees had agreed that anyone who acknowledged Jesus as the Christ would be put out of the synagogue. So they chose their words carefully. "We know this is our son," they said. "We know he was born blind. But how he now sees, we do not know. He is of age. Ask him."

The Pharisees called the man back a second time. "Give glory to God," they said sternly. "We know that this man Jesus is a sinner."

The man who had been blind looked at them. "Whether he is a sinner, I do not know," he said. "One thing I do know. I was blind, and now I see."

"What did he do to you?" they demanded. "How did he open your eyes?"

The man's patience was thinning. "I already told you," he said, "and you did not listen. Why do you want to hear it again? Do you want to become his disciples too?"

They reviled him. They said, "You are his disciple, but we are disciples of Moses. We know that God spoke to Moses. As for this fellow, we do not even know where he comes from."

The man shook his head. "That is remarkable," he said. "You don't know where he comes from, and yet he opened my eyes. Since the beginning of the world, no one has opened the eyes of a person born blind. If this man were not from God, he could do nothing."

They were furious. "You were born entirely in sin," they said, "and you are trying to teach us?" And they threw him out.

When Jesus heard that the man had been cast out, he found him. The man was standing alone — no longer by the roadside with his begging bowl, no longer welcome in the synagogue, no longer anyone's easy explanation.

"Do you believe in the Son of God?" Jesus asked.

The man looked at him. He was seeing Jesus' face for the first time — the face of the man whose hands had made the clay, whose voice had told him to wash, whose word had changed everything.

"Who is he, Lord," the man said, "that I may believe in him?"

Jesus said, "You have seen him. He is the one speaking with you."

Something settled in the man's expression. Not surprise. Recognition. As though the light that had entered his eyes had at last reached the deepest part of him.

"Lord, I believe," he said.

And he worshiped him.
