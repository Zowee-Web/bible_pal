The streets of Jerusalem were full of people that morning. Men and women moved between the market stalls and the gates of the temple, bearing bread, leading animals, calling one to another across the narrow lanes. The sun was already warm, and its light fell in sharp angles between the stone buildings, leaving pools of shadow along the walls.

Jesus and his disciples were passing through the city when they came upon a man sitting beside the way. He had been blind from his birth. His eyes were closed, his face turned slightly upward, as one that hearkeneth to the world about him. His hands rested upon his knees. A bowl sat in the dust beside him. He was a beggar — known to the people of the neighbourhood, seen daily in the same place. He had never beheld the temple. He had never seen the sky. He had never looked upon the face of another living soul. His world was sound and smell and the touch of hands, and it had ever been so.

His disciples asked him, saying, "Master, who did sin, this man, or his parents, that he was born blind?"

It was a common question. People supposed that such affliction must have a cause — that someone had transgressed, and the blindness was the consequence thereof.

Jesus answered, "Neither hath this man sinned, nor his parents: but that the works of God should be made manifest in him. I must work the works of him that sent me, while it is day: the night cometh, when no man can work."

He ceased walking. He kneeled in the dust beside the man. He spat upon the dry ground and mingled the earth with the moisture, working it between his fingers until it became a smooth, dark clay. Then he leaned forward and anointed the man's eyes, pressing the clay gently upon his closed lids. The man flinched not. He sat still, the clay cool and moist upon his skin.

"Go," said Jesus, "wash in the pool of Siloam."

The man arose. He stood in the road a moment, the clay drying upon his face, the sun warm upon his shoulders. Then he turned and went his way. He moved through the streets of Jerusalem guided by sound — the tap of a staff, the scraping of sandals upon stone, the voices of merchants and children. He came unto the pool of Siloam, which was hewn in the stone at the lower end of the city. The water was cool and clear. He kneeled at the edge, dipped his hands, and washed the clay from his eyes.

And he received his sight.

Light flooded in upon him. The world sharpened round about in a great rushing of colour and form — the blue of the water, the grey of the stone, the green leaves upon the trees along the wall, the brown of his own hands dripping before his face. He looked upward and beheld the sky for the first time — vast, bright, open beyond all reckoning. He looked down and saw his own face trembling upon the surface of the pool. He rose and turned about slowly, looking upon all things, his mouth open, his breath quick and shallow.

He returned through the streets, and his neighbours were first to take notice. "Is not this he that sat and begged?" said some. Others shook their heads. "Nay; he is like him." But the man said, "I am he."

"How then were thine eyes opened?"

He told them plainly all that had befallen him. "A man that is called Jesus made clay, and anointed mine eyes, and said unto me, Go to the pool of Siloam, and wash: and I went and washed, and I received sight."

"Where is he?" they asked.

"I know not," said he.

They brought him before the Pharisees, for the healing had come to pass upon the sabbath day. The Pharisees examined him straitly — how it had happened, who had wrought it, what had been spoken. The man told them as he had told the others — the clay, the washing, the sight. Some of the Pharisees said, "This man is not of God, because he keepeth not the sabbath day." Others said, "How can a man that is a sinner do such miracles?" And there was a division among them.

They turned to the man. "What sayest thou of him, that he hath opened thine eyes?"

He said, "He is a prophet."

The Pharisees would not receive this. They sent for his parents. "Is this your son?" they demanded. "Was he born blind? How doth he now see?"

His parents feared them, for the Jews had agreed already that if any man did confess that Jesus was Christ, he should be put out of the synagogue. Therefore they chose their words carefully. "We know that this is our son, and that he was born blind: but by what means he now seeth, we know not. He is of age; ask him: he shall speak for himself."

The Pharisees called the man again. "Give God the praise," they said. "We know that this man is a sinner."

He that had been blind looked upon them. "Whether he be a sinner or no, I know not," he said. "One thing I know, that, whereas I was blind, now I see."

"What did he to thee?" they pressed. "How opened he thine eyes?"

The man's patience was tried. "I have told you already," he said, "and ye did not hear: wherefore would ye hear it again? Will ye also be his disciples?"

They reviled him. They said, "Thou art his disciple; but we are Moses' disciples. We know that God spake unto Moses: as for this fellow, we know not from whence he is."

The man shook his head. "Why herein is a marvellous thing," he said, "that ye know not from whence he is, and yet he hath opened mine eyes. Since the world began was it not heard that any man opened the eyes of one that was born blind. If this man were not of God, he could do nothing."

They were wroth. "Thou wast altogether born in sins," they said, "and dost thou teach us?" And they cast him out.

When Jesus heard that they had cast him out, he found him. The man was standing alone — no longer beside the road with his beggar's bowl, no longer suffered in the synagogue, no longer any man's simple explanation.

"Dost thou believe on the Son of God?" Jesus asked.

The man looked upon him. He was beholding the face of Jesus for the first time — the face of him whose hands had made the clay, whose voice had bidden him wash, whose word had changed all things.

"Who is he, Lord," said the man, "that I might believe on him?"

Jesus saith unto him, "Thou hast both seen him, and it is he that talketh with thee."

Something settled upon the man's countenance. Not astonishment. Recognition. As though the light that had entered his eyes had at the last reached the very depth of him.

"Lord, I believe," he said.

And he worshipped him.
