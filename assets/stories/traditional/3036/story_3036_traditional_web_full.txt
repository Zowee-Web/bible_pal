Nahum's oracle turns directly toward Nineveh. One who dashes in pieces has come up, and the city hears a rapid series of commands: keep the fortress, watch the road, strengthen the waist, fortify power mightily. The commands do not promise success. The oracle grounds the approaching judgment in Yahweh's restoration of the excellency of Jacob and Israel, whose vine branches destroyers had ruined.

Shields are red; valiant men wear scarlet; chariots flash with steel; spears are brandished. Chariots rage through the streets, rushing like torches and lightning. Picked troops are summoned, yet they stumble while hurrying to the wall. The defensive covering is prepared, but river gates open and the palace dissolves.

The decree carries Nineveh away. Her servants moan like doves and beat their breasts. Though the city had long seemed like a pool full of water, its people now flee. Commands to stop them receive no backward glance. The invader is told to take silver and gold from stores that seem without end. The threefold description remains: empty, void, and waste. Hearts melt, knees knock, bodies tremble, and faces grow pale.

The oracle answers the fallen city with a taunt. Where is the lions' den, the place where lion, lioness, and cub walked without fear? The lion had torn enough prey for his cubs and filled caves and dens with the kill. Now Yahweh of Armies declares, “Behold, I am against you.” He will burn the chariots, allow the sword to consume the young lions, cut off the prey, and silence the messengers.

The next woe names Nineveh a bloody city, full of lies and robbery, never lacking prey. Sound and sight press together: whip, rattling wheels, prancing horses, bounding chariots, flashing sword, and glittering spear. A multitude lie slain, with bodies without end. Nineveh had sold nations and families through its corrupting power.

For the second time Yahweh of Armies says, “Behold, I am against you.” Nineveh's shame will be exposed before nations and kingdoms. She will be made a spectacle. Those who see her will flee and say, “Nineveh is laid waste! Who will mourn for her?” Yahweh then asks, “Where will I seek comforters for you?”

The oracle points to No-Amon. Waters surrounded that city; the sea served as rampart and wall. Cush and Egypt gave boundless strength, with Put and Libya as helpers. Yet No-Amon was carried away. Her children suffered violence, lots were cast for her honorable men, and her great men were bound. Nineveh is told that she too will be overwhelmed and will seek a refuge from the enemy.

Her fortresses are compared to fig trees holding first-ripe fruit: one shaking drops the figs into the eater's mouth. Her gates stand open and fire consumes their bars. She may draw siege water, strengthen defenses, tread mortar, and reinforce brickwork, but fire and sword still await her. She may multiply like grasshoppers and locusts; her numerous merchants will strip and fly away. Guards and officials resemble locust swarms settled in cold weather that disappear when the sun rises.

At the end, the address narrows to the king of Assyria. His shepherds slumber, his nobles lie down, and the people scatter on the mountains without anyone to gather them. Nineveh's wound has no healing; the injury is fatal. Those hearing the report clap their hands, and the oracle closes with its reason: no people had remained untouched by Nineveh's endless cruelty.
