Bethany was a small village in the hills eastward of Jerusalem, nigh enough unto the city that the smoke of the temple fires could be seen rising above the ridge on still mornings. Stone houses with flat roofs stood along the narrow paths. Olive groves and fig trees grew upon the terraced slopes, and the air in the early hours carried the smell of bread baking and the faint bleating of goats tethered near the walls.

In one of those houses dwelt a man called Lazarus and his two sisters, Mary and Martha. They were known in the village as quiet, faithful people. Jesus had been a guest in their house many times. He had sat at their table, broken bread with them, and tarried late into the evening while the oil lamp burned low and the stars appeared above the courtyard. They were dear unto him, as near as kinfolk.

When Lazarus fell sick, the illness came upon him swiftly. His skin grew hot. He could not eat. He lay upon his mat and rose not. Martha pressed cool cloths upon his brow and kept the room dark and still. Mary sat beside him, her hand resting upon his arm. The hours passed slowly, and he grew worse.

The sisters sent a message unto Jesus, wheresoever he might be. The message was brief and urgent: "Lord, behold, he whom thou lovest is sick."

When Jesus received the word, he saith to those about him, "This sickness is not unto death, but for the glory of God, that the Son of God might be glorified thereby." And though he loved Martha and her sister and Lazarus, he departed not. He abode two days still in the same place.

After two days he saith unto his disciples, "Let us go into Judaea again."

The disciples said unto him with alarm, "Master, the Jews of late sought to stone thee; and goest thou thither again?"

Jesus answered, "Are there not twelve hours in the day? If any man walk in the day, he stumbleth not, because he seeth the light of this world." Then he saith, "Our friend Lazarus sleepeth; but I go, that I may awake him out of sleep."

The disciples were relieved. "Lord, if he sleep, he shall do well," they said.

Then said Jesus unto them plainly, "Lazarus is dead. And I am glad for your sakes that I was not there, to the intent ye may believe; nevertheless let us go unto him."

Then said Thomas, which is called Didymus, unto his fellowdisciples, "Let us also go, that we may die with him." His voice was quiet but resolved.

The journey unto Bethany was not short. When Jesus came at last, he found that Lazarus had lain in the grave four days already. Bethany being nigh unto Jerusalem, about fifteen furlongs off, many of the Jews had come from the city to be with the family. The house was full of mourners. Women sat in the doorway with their veils drawn over their faces, rocking slowly. Men stood without in small companies, speaking low. The smell of spices drifted from the open door. Within, the sound of weeping passed from room to room.

Martha heard that Jesus was coming and went forth to meet him upon the road, ere he reached the village. Mary sat still in the house among the mourners.

When Martha found Jesus, she was out of breath from walking quickly. Her countenance was drawn, her eyes red with weeping. She stood before him and said, "Lord, if thou hadst been here, my brother had not died." She paused and drew her breath. "But I know that even now, whatsoever thou wilt ask of God, God will give it thee."

Jesus saith unto her with gentle steadiness, "Thy brother shall rise again."

"I know that he shall rise again in the resurrection at the last day," Martha answered.

Jesus said unto her, "I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live: and whosoever liveth and believeth in me shall never die." He paused. "Believest thou this?"

Martha looked upon his face directly. "Yea, Lord," she said. "I believe that thou art the Christ, the Son of God, which should come into the world."

She turned and went again unto the house. Inside, she found Mary amongst the mourners. She knelt beside her and whispered, "The Master is come, and calleth for thee." Mary arose quickly and went out. The mourners saw her go and followed after, supposing she went unto the grave to weep there.

When Mary came unto Jesus — for he had not yet entered the village but was still upon the road where Martha had met him — she fell down at his feet. Her body shook with weeping. "Lord," she said, "if thou hadst been here, my brother had not died."

When Jesus therefore saw her weeping, and the Jews also weeping which came with her, he groaned in the spirit and was troubled. Something stirred deep within him.

"Where have ye laid him?" saith he. His voice was low and strained.

"Lord, come and see."

Jesus wept. The tears moved down his face. He wiped them not away.

The Jews therefore said among themselves, "Behold how he loved him!" And some of them said, "Could not this man, which opened the eyes of the blind, have caused that even this man should not have died?"

Jesus therefore, again groaning in himself, cometh to the grave. It was a cave cut in the limestone of the hillside, and a stone lay upon it. The stone was rough and heavy. The ground before the entrance was bare, trodden down by the feet of mourners in the days before. The air bore the smell of dust and something heavier still.

"Take ye away the stone," Jesus said.

Martha, standing nigh unto him, spoke softly. "Lord, by this time he stinketh: for he hath been dead four days."

Jesus turned unto her. "Said I not unto thee, that, if thou wouldest believe, thou shouldest see the glory of God?"

Men came forward and set their shoulders to the stone. It scraped against the rock and moved slowly aside, revealing the dark mouth of the cave. Cool air drifted forth. The mourners drew back, some covering their faces.

Jesus lifted up his eyes unto heaven. "Father," he said, "I thank thee that thou hast heard me. I knew that thou hearest me always: but because of the people which stand by I said it, that they may believe that thou hast sent me."

Then he drew a deep breath and cried with a loud voice:

"Lazarus, come forth!"

For a moment there was nothing. The cave was dark and still. The mourners held their breath. The wind stirred the dry grass upon the hillside above the sepulchre.

Then something moved within the darkness. A form appeared in the mouth of the cave — a man, bound head to foot in linen, his face wrapped about with a napkin. He moved slowly, stiffly, his arms bound at his sides. He stepped forth from the darkness and into the bright light of the afternoon.

The crowd cried out. Some fell upon their knees. A woman covered her face with both hands. A man near the entrance of the tomb stumbled backward.

Jesus looked upon those standing nearest. His voice was calm and steady.

"Loose him," he saith, "and let him go."

Hands reached forward. The linen was unwound, strip by strip, falling in pale coils at the man's feet. The napkin was taken from his face. Lazarus stood in the sunlight — blinking, breathing, alive.
