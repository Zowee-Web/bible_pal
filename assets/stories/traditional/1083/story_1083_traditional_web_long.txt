Bethany was a small village in the hills east of Jerusalem, close enough to the city that the smoke from the temple fires could sometimes be seen rising above the ridge on still mornings. Stone houses with flat roofs lined the narrow paths. Olive groves and fig trees grew along the terraced slopes, and the air in the early part of the day carried the smell of baking bread and the faint bleating of goats tethered near the walls.

In one of those houses lived a man named Lazarus and his two sisters, Mary and Martha. They were known in the village as quiet, faithful people. Jesus had been a guest in their home many times. He had sat at their table, broken bread with them, talked late into the evening while the oil lamp flickered and the stars appeared above the courtyard. They were close to him — as close as family.

When Lazarus fell sick, the illness came on quickly. His skin grew hot. He could not eat. He lay on his mat and did not rise. Martha pressed cool cloths to his forehead and kept the room dark and still. Mary sat beside him, her hand resting on his arm. The hours passed slowly, and he grew worse.

The sisters sent a message to Jesus, wherever he was. The message was brief and urgent: "Lord, the one you love is sick."

When Jesus received the word, he said to those around him, "This sickness is not to death, but for the glory of God, that God's Son may be glorified by it." And though he loved Martha and her sister and Lazarus, he did not leave. He stayed two more days in the place where he was.

After two days, he said to his disciples, "Let us go into Judea again."

The disciples looked at him with alarm. "Rabbi," they said, "the Jews were just trying to stone you there. Are you going back?"

Jesus answered, "Are there not twelve hours of daylight? If a man walks in the day, he does not stumble, because he sees the light of this world." Then he said, "Our friend Lazarus has fallen asleep. But I am going so that I may wake him."

The disciples looked relieved. "Lord, if he has fallen asleep, he will recover."

Then Jesus said plainly, "Lazarus is dead. And I am glad for your sakes that I was not there, so that you may believe. Nevertheless, let us go to him."

Thomas, the one called Didymus, turned to the others. His voice was quiet but resolved. "Let us go also," he said, "that we may die with him."

The journey to Bethany took time. When at last Jesus arrived, he learned that Lazarus had already been in the tomb four days. Bethany was near Jerusalem — about fifteen stadia away — and many people had come from the city to be with the family. The house was full of mourners. Women sat in the doorway with their heads covered, rocking slowly back and forth. Men stood in small groups outside, speaking in low voices. The smell of incense drifted from the open door. Inside, the sound of weeping carried from room to room.

Martha heard that Jesus was on his way and went out to meet him on the road before he reached the village. Mary stayed behind, sitting in the house among the mourners.

When Martha found Jesus, she was breathing hard from walking quickly. Her face was drawn and pale, her eyes red. She stood before him and said, "Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died." She paused, drawing a breath. "But even now I know that whatever you ask of God, God will give you."

Jesus looked at her with steady kindness. "Your brother will rise again," he said.

"I know that he will rise again," Martha answered, "in the resurrection on the last day."

Jesus said to her, "I am the resurrection and the life. He who believes in me, though he die, yet shall he live. And whoever lives and believes in me shall never die." He paused. "Do you believe this?"

Martha looked directly at his face. "Yes, Lord," she said. "I believe that you are the Christ, the Son of God, who comes into the world."

She turned and went back to the house. Inside, she found her sister sitting among the mourners. She knelt beside her and whispered, "The Teacher is here. He is asking for you." Mary rose quickly and went out. The mourners saw her leave and followed, thinking she was going to the tomb to weep.

When Mary reached Jesus — he had not yet entered the village but was still on the road where Martha had met him — she fell down at his feet. Her body shook with weeping. "Lord," she said, "if you had been here, my brother would not have died."

Jesus saw her weeping. He saw the mourners behind her, weeping. The grief filled the open air between the hillside and the road. Something moved deep inside him. He groaned in his spirit. He was troubled.

"Where have you laid him?" he asked. His voice was low and tight.

"Lord, come and see," they said.

And Jesus wept. Tears moved down his face. He did not wipe them away.

The people standing nearby looked at one another. "See how much he loved him," some of them said. Others murmured, "Could not this man, who opened the eyes of the blind, have also kept this man from dying?"

Jesus, again deeply moved within himself, came to the tomb. It was a cave cut into the limestone hillside, with a great stone rolled across the opening. The stone was rough and heavy. The ground in front of the entrance was bare, trampled by the feet of mourners in the days before. Dried flowers lay near the base of the stone. The air smelled of dust and something deeper — the faint, sweet heaviness of death.

"Take away the stone," Jesus said.

Martha, standing near him, spoke softly. "Lord, by this time there will be a stench. He has been dead four days."

Jesus turned to her. "Did I not tell you," he said, "that if you believed, you would see the glory of God?"

Several men stepped forward and put their shoulders to the stone. It scraped against the rock and rolled slowly aside, revealing the dark mouth of the cave. Cool air drifted out. The mourners stepped back, some covering their faces.

Jesus lifted his eyes to heaven. "Father," he said, "I thank you that you heard me. I know that you always hear me. But because of the people standing here, I have said this — that they may believe that you sent me."

Then he drew a deep breath and cried out with a loud voice:

"Lazarus, come out!"

For a moment, nothing. The cave was dark and still. The mourners held their breath. The wind moved through the dry grass on the hillside above the tomb.

Then something stirred in the darkness. A shape appeared in the mouth of the cave — a man, wrapped head to foot in linen strips, his face covered with a burial cloth. He moved slowly, stiffly, his arms bound at his sides. He stepped out of the darkness and into the bright afternoon light.

The crowd gasped. Some fell to their knees. Others cried out. A woman covered her face with both hands. A man standing near the entrance of the tomb stumbled backward.

Jesus looked at those standing nearest. His voice was calm and steady.

"Loose him," he said, "and let him go."

Hands reached forward. The linen was unwound, strip by strip, falling in pale coils at the man's feet. The cloth was removed from his face. Lazarus stood in the sunlight, blinking, breathing, alive.
