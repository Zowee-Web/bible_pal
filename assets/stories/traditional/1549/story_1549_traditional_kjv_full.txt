The road had been climbing since the breaking of the day. It was a narrow way, of packed earth and loose stone, and it wound between ridges that rose up brown and barren against the heavens. Dust hung upon the air behind every footfall. The sun had passed its height and the shadows had begun to lean, yet they offered nothing. There was no shade upon that stretch. There had been no shade for many hours.

The pilgrims moved in a long and uneven line. Some walked two together. Some walked alone. A few donkeys bore burdens lashed down with rope, and their hooves struck upon the flat rocks that broke through the face of the road. Children kept close unto their mothers. Old men leaned upon their staffs. The column stretched back so far that the last among them were hidden by the turning of the hill behind.

A woman walked near the midst of them. Her robe was pale with the dust of the road from the hem even unto the waist. Her headcloth had slipped backward and she had not set it right. The water skin upon her hip swung with each step, nigh unto empty, the leather soft and slack. Her sandals were worn through at the ball of the left foot. She had kept her eyes upon the ground — the stones, the ruts, the cracked earth — for the greater part of the morning. Her shoulders were low and drawn forward, and her breathing was steady, yet slow.

She lifted up her head.

The hills filled all the view before her. They rose one behind another, ridge upon ridge, the colour of old bread, and dry grass clung to their sides in patches. Somewhere beyond them lay Jerusalem, though it could not be seen from that place. Only the hills. Only the road winding upward between them.

She opened her mouth. Her voice came forth rough and quiet, worn by the dust and the dry air.

"I shall lift mine eyes unto the hills. From whence doth my help come?"

A man before her turned his head. He was broad across the back, and his pack rode high between his shoulders. He did not cease walking. He listened.

"My help cometh from the Lord, who hath made the heavens and the earth."

The melody was a simple thing. It moved in a narrow compass, the manner of tune that abideth in the throat even when the voice is spent. It was a song of ascent — one of the songs the pilgrims sang upon the road going up unto Jerusalem. Every soul upon that road knew the words thereof.

The broad man's lips moved. He joined her upon the next line, his voice lower, and rougher than hers.

"He shall not suffer thy foot to be moved."

A woman farther ahead took up the song. Then a young man walking beside a donkey. And the words passed forward and backward through the column. Not all at once. Not with one voice. Souls found the song at divers moments, as men step into a stream at different points along the bank.

"He that keepeth watch over thee shall not slumber."

The road turned sharply unto the left around a rocky outcrop. The stones there were larger, pale and riven by weather, and the path grew narrow so that scarcely two could walk abreast. A child stumbled where a rock jutted forth from the packed earth. A hand reached down — the hand of a man, rough at the knuckle, the nails dark with soil — and caught the child by the arm and set the child upright. The child continued walking. The man continued walking. Neither one ceased from singing.

"He that keepeth Israel shall not slumber. He shall not close his eyes."

More voices now. They were not strong voices. They were the voices of people who had walked for days, people whose lips were cracked and whose knees ached and whose robes bore the smell of sweat and road dust and woodsmoke from the fires of the night before. Yet the song carried. The dry air held it. The hills received it and let it go.

"The Lord is thy keeper."

The woman wiped her face with the back of her hand. Her fingers left a pale streak through the dust upon her cheek. She kept her eyes lifted now, upon the hills. Her feet found the road and did not require the looking.

"The Lord is thy shade upon thy right hand."

The sun pressed down upon the column. Heat rose from the stones. A donkey shook its head and the rope upon its burden creaked. A fly circled and alighted upon a man's neck, and he did not brush it away. He was singing.

"The sun shall not smite thee by day, neither the moon by night."

The road grew level for a brief span — a flat place no longer than thirty paces — and then it climbed again. The pilgrims leaned forward into the slope. The tips of staffs scraped upon stone. Breath came harder. The singing did not cease.

"The Lord shall preserve thee from all evil. He shall keep watch over thy life."

The woman's voice was clearer now. Not louder. Clearer. As though the singing itself had opened something within her throat. She walked with her head lifted and her hands loose at her sides and the empty water skin swinging against her hip.

"The Lord shall watch over thy going out and thy coming in, from this hour forth, and forevermore."

The last word faded. For a moment there was only the sound of feet upon stone. Then someone farther back in the column began the song again from the beginning, and other voices found it. The hills rose before them, ridge behind ridge, brown and dry and vast beneath the white sky. The road wound upward between them. The pilgrims climbed.
