The road had been climbing since dawn. It was a narrow road, packed earth and loose stone, and it wound between ridges that rose brown and treeless against the sky. Dust hung in the air behind each footstep. The sun was past its midpoint and the shadows had started to lean but offered nothing. There was no shade on this stretch. There had been no shade for hours.

The pilgrims moved in a long uneven line. Some walked in pairs. Some walked alone. A few donkeys carried bundles lashed down with rope, their hooves clicking on the flat rocks that broke through the surface of the road. Children walked close to their mothers. Old men leaned on staffs. The column stretched back far enough that the last travelers were hidden by the curve of the hill behind them.

A woman walked near the middle. Her robe was pale with road dust from the hem to the waist. Her headcloth had slipped back and she had not fixed it. The water skin at her hip swung with each step, almost empty, the leather soft and slack. Her sandals were worn through at the ball of the left foot. She had been watching the ground — the stones, the ruts, the cracked earth — for most of the morning. Her shoulders were low and forward and her breathing was steady but slow.

She lifted her head.

The hills filled the view ahead. They rose one behind another, ridge after ridge, the color of old bread, dry grass clinging to their sides in patches. Somewhere beyond them lay Jerusalem, though it could not be seen from here. Only the hills. Only the road winding up between them.

She opened her mouth. Her voice came out rough and quiet, worn by the dust and the dry air.

"I will lift up my eyes to the hills. Where does my help come from?"

A man ahead of her turned his head. He was broad across the back, his pack riding high between his shoulder blades. He did not stop walking. He listened.

"My help comes from the LORD, who made heaven and earth."

The melody was simple. It moved in a narrow range, the kind of tune that stayed in the throat even when the voice was tired. It was a song of ascent — one of the songs the pilgrims sang on the road going up to Jerusalem. Everyone on this road knew the words.

The broad man's lips moved. He joined her on the next line, his voice lower, rougher.

"He will not let your foot slip."

A woman farther ahead picked it up. Then a young man walking beside a donkey. The words passed forward and backward through the column. Not all at once. Not in unison. Voices found the song at different moments, like people stepping into a stream at different points along the bank.

"He who watches over you will not sleep."

The road bent sharply to the left around a rocky outcrop. The stones here were larger, pale and cracked by weather, and the path narrowed so that two could barely walk abreast. A child stumbled where a rock jutted from the packed earth. A hand reached down — a man's hand, rough-knuckled, the nails dark with dirt — and caught the child's arm and pulled the child upright. The child kept walking. The man kept walking. Neither of them stopped singing.

"The one who watches over Israel will not sleep. He will not close his eyes."

More voices now. They were not strong voices. They were the voices of people who had been walking for days, people whose lips were cracked and whose knees ached and whose robes smelled of sweat and road dust and woodsmoke from the fires of the night before. But the song carried. The dry air held it. The hills caught it and let it go.

"The LORD is your keeper."

The woman wiped her face with the back of her hand. Her fingers left a pale streak through the dust on her cheek. She kept her eyes up now, on the hills. Her feet found the road without looking.

"The LORD is your shade at your right hand."

The sun pressed down on the column. Heat rose from the stones. A donkey shook its head and the rope on its pack creaked. A fly circled and landed on a man's neck and he did not brush it away. He was singing.

"The sun will not strike you by day, nor the moon by night."

The road leveled briefly — a flat stretch no longer than thirty paces — and then climbed again. The pilgrims leaned forward into the slope. Staff tips scraped against stone. Breath came harder. The singing did not stop.

"The LORD will keep you from all harm. He will watch over your life."

The woman's voice was clearer now. Not louder. Clearer. As if the singing itself had opened something in her throat. She walked with her head up and her hands loose at her sides and the empty water skin swinging against her hip.

"The LORD will watch over your going out and your coming in, from this time on, and forever."

The last word faded. For a moment there was only the sound of feet on stone. Then someone farther back in the column began the song again from the beginning, and other voices found it. The hills rose ahead, ridge behind ridge, brown and dry and enormous under the white sky. The road wound upward between them. The pilgrims climbed.
