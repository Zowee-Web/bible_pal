In Jerusalem, near the Sheep Gate on the north side of the city, there was a pool called Bethesda. It was surrounded by five covered porches — long colonnades of stone pillars supporting roofs of timber and clay. The shadows under the porches were cool even in the worst of the summer heat. The water in the pool was still and greenish, fed by an underground spring that sometimes stirred without warning.

Under those porches lay a great number of sick people. They were spread along the stone floor on mats and blankets — the blind, the lame, the paralyzed, the wasted. Some lay still, staring at the ceiling. Some sat propped against the pillars, their eyes fixed on the water, waiting. Others were tended by family members who sat beside them and wiped their foreheads and fanned the flies away. The smell under the porches was heavy — sweat, stale water, illness, the close air of bodies that had been lying in one place too long.

Among them was a man who had been sick for thirty-eight years. No one remembered what he had been before the illness. He had been lying near this pool for so long that he had become part of its landscape, like the stones themselves. His mat was worn through in places. His limbs were thin and stiff. His face was lined with the kind of weariness that comes not from one bad day, but from thousands of them.

Jesus came to the pool. He walked through the porches, past the rows of sick people, and his eyes moved from one to the next. He saw the man lying there. He knew — without being told — how long the man had been waiting.

He stopped beside him and asked a single question.

"Do you want to be made well?"

The man did not say yes. He did not say no. He did not even look up at first. He said, "Sir, I have no one to put me into the pool when the water is stirred up. While I am trying to get in, someone else always steps down before me."

It was not an answer to the question. It was the sound of thirty-eight years of being alone — of watching others receive what he could not reach, of having no one to help, of trying and failing so many times that trying had become its own kind of suffering.

Jesus looked at him.

"Rise," he said. "Take up your mat, and walk."

And immediately the man was made well. Strength poured into his legs. His knees bent. His feet pressed flat against the stone floor. He stood — trembling, uncertain, but standing — for the first time in thirty-eight years. He reached down, rolled up the mat he had been lying on, tucked it under his arm, and walked out of the porches and into the sunlight.

It was the Sabbath.

The Jewish leaders saw the man walking through the streets with his mat under his arm. They stopped him. "It is the Sabbath," they said. "It is not lawful for you to carry your mat."

The man looked at them. "The one who made me well told me, 'Take up your mat, and walk,'" he said.

"Who is this man who told you to take it up and walk?" they demanded. But the man did not know who it was. Jesus had slipped away into the crowd that filled the area.

Later, Jesus found him in the temple. The man was standing there — standing, on his own two feet — in the courts where he had once been carried by others. Jesus looked at him.

"See," he said, "you have been made well. Sin no more, so that nothing worse may happen to you."

The man went away and told the Jewish leaders that it was Jesus who had made him well.
