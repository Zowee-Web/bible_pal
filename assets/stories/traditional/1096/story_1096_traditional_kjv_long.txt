The pool of Bethesda lay in the northeastern quarter of Jerusalem, nigh unto the sheep gate where animals were brought in for the temple sacrifices. It was a large pool, rectangular in shape, fed by a spring beneath the ground whose waters sometimes stirred without cause. About the pool ran five porches — colonnades of rough stone pillars bearing roofs of timber and dried clay. The shadows under the porches were dark and cool, even in the heat of summer. The smell was heavy — stagnant water, unwashed bodies, the close air of sickness and long waiting.

In these lay a great multitude of impotent folk. They were everywhere — upon the stone floor, upon thin mats, upon blankets worn through from use. The blind sat with their backs against the pillars, their hands folded, their faces turned toward nothing. The lame lay with their limbs at strange angles, some covered, some bare. The withered stared at the ceiling, their eyes moving even when their bodies could not. Some were tended by kindred — a wife pressing a cloth to her husband's brow, a son holding a cup to a father's lips. Others were alone, and had been alone for years.

Among them was a certain man which had an infirmity thirty and eight years.

Thirty and eight years. The number was nigh impossible to hold in the mind. Longer than the working lives of most men. Longer than the reigns of kings. He had lain near this pool since before many that walked the city were born. His mat was worn through, the reeds soft and flat from decades of bearing the same body in the same place. His limbs were thin, the sinews wasted. His countenance was deeply lined — not with age alone, but with the particular weariness of a man who hath waited so long that waiting hath become his very nature.

None remembered what he had been before. None asked.

Jesus came unto the pool. He entered the porches and walked past the rows of sick folk, past the blind and the lame and the withered, and his eyes moved from one to the next. Some reached toward him. Others lay still.

He stopped beside the man who had been there eight and thirty years. He stood over him, looking down. He knew — without being told, without asking — how long the man had waited.

He asked a question.

"Wilt thou be made whole?"

It was not a question asked in passing. It went unto the heart of what eight and thirty years of infirmity can do to a soul — the way hope itself becometh unbearable, the way wanting a thing so long turneth to fear of receiving it, the way a man may cease to believe that change is possible and begin to order his life about the absence of it.

The man answered not yea. He looked not up. He said, "Sir, I have no man, when the water is troubled, to put me into the pool: but while I am coming, another steppeth down before me."

It was not an answer to the question put to him. It was the accumulated weight of eight and thirty years — of watching others receive what he could not reach, of having none to help, of arriving too late every time, of being always and forever alone in his need.

Jesus looked upon him. He addressed not the pool, nor the water, nor the lack of a helper. He spake directly to the man's body.

"Rise, take up thy bed, and walk."

And immediately the man was made whole. Strength poured into his legs as water into a dry channel. His knees bent. His feet pressed flat upon the stone. His back straightened. He rose — trembling, his body remembering a posture it had long forgotten — and he stood. For the first time in eight and thirty years, he was standing upon his own feet.

He looked down upon the mat beneath him. He reached down, rolled it, took it under his arm.

And he walked.

He walked forth from the porches, past the other sick folk, past the pool, into the bright light of the street. The sun fell upon his face. The noise of the city surrounded him. He was walking.

And on that day was the sabbath.

The Jews saw the man going through the streets with his mat. They stopped him. "It is the sabbath day," said they. "It is not lawful for thee to carry thy bed."

The man looked upon them. He was yet adjusting to the sensation of standing. "He that made me whole," he said, "the same said unto me, Take up thy bed, and walk."

"What man is that?" they demanded. But he that was healed wist not who it was, for he had asked no name, and Jesus had conveyed himself away, a multitude being in that place.

Afterward Jesus findeth him in the temple. The man was standing in the courts — standing, upon his own feet, in the place where he had once been borne. He was looking about the temple with the eyes of a man beholding something familiar from a height he had never known.

Jesus saith unto him, "Behold, thou art made whole: sin no more, lest a worse thing come unto thee."

The man departed and told the Jews that it was Jesus which had made him whole.
