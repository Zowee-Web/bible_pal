The pool of Bethesda lay in the northeastern quarter of Jerusalem, near the Sheep Gate where animals were brought in for the temple sacrifices. It was a large pool, rectangular, fed by an underground spring whose waters sometimes stirred without explanation. Around the pool ran five covered porches — colonnades of rough stone pillars supporting roofs of timber and dried clay. The shadows under the porches were dark and cool, even in the heat of summer. The smell was heavy — stagnant water, unwashed bodies, the sour air of illness and waiting.

Under those porches lay a great number of sick people. They were everywhere — on the stone floor, on thin mats, on blankets that had been folded and refolded until the fabric was translucent. The blind sat with their backs against the pillars, their hands folded, their faces turned toward nothing. The lame lay with their legs at odd angles, some covered by cloth, some exposed to the air. The paralyzed stared at the ceiling, their eyes moving even when their bodies could not. Some were tended by family members — a wife wiping a husband's forehead, a son holding a cup to a father's lips. Others were alone, and had been alone for years.

Among them was a man who had been sick for thirty-eight years.

Thirty-eight years. The number was almost impossible to hold in the mind. Longer than most people's working lives. Longer than the reigns of kings. He had been lying near this pool since before many of the people walking through the city were born. His mat was worn through in places, the reeds soft and flat from decades of bearing the same body in the same position. His limbs were thin, the muscles atrophied to cords. His face was deeply lined — not with age alone, but with the particular weariness of a man who has waited so long that waiting has become his identity.

No one remembered what he had been before. No one asked.

Jesus came to the pool. He entered the porches and walked past the rows of sick people, past the blind and the lame and the paralyzed, and his eyes moved from one to the next. The sick watched him pass. Some reached toward him. Others lay still.

He stopped beside the man who had been there thirty-eight years. He stood over him, looking down. He knew — without being told, without asking — how long the man had been waiting.

He asked a question.

"Do you want to be made well?"

It was not a casual question. It was not rhetorical. It went to the heart of something that thirty-eight years of illness can do to a person — the way hope itself can become unbearable, the way wanting something for so long can turn into a fear of receiving it, the way a man can stop believing that change is possible and begin to arrange his life around the absence of it.

The man did not say yes. He did not look up. He said, "Sir, I have no one to put me into the pool when the water is stirred up. While I am trying to get in, someone else always steps down before me."

It was not an answer to the question. It was the accumulated weight of thirty-eight years — thirty-eight years of watching others get what he could not reach, of having no one to help, of arriving too late every time, of being always and forever alone in his need. He had stopped answering the question Jesus actually asked and started explaining why the answer didn't matter.

Jesus looked at him. He did not argue with the explanation. He did not address the pool or the water or the lack of a helper. He spoke directly to the man's body.

"Rise," he said. "Take up your mat, and walk."

And immediately — the word is immediate, without delay, without transition — the man was made well. Strength poured into his legs like water into a dry channel. His knees bent. His feet pressed flat against the stone floor. His back straightened. He rose — trembling, uncertain, his body remembering a posture it had forgotten — and he stood. For the first time in thirty-eight years, he was standing on his own two feet.

He looked down at the mat beneath him — the mat he had lain on for decades. He reached down, rolled it up, tucked it under his arm.

And he walked.

He walked out of the porches, past the other sick people, past the pool, into the bright light of the street. The sun fell on his face. The noise of the city surrounded him. He was walking.

It was the Sabbath.

The Jewish leaders saw the man moving through the streets with his mat. They stopped him. "It is the Sabbath," they said. "It is not lawful for you to carry your mat."

The man looked at them. He was still adjusting to the sensation of standing. "The one who made me well said to me, 'Take up your mat, and walk,'" he said.

"Who is this man?" they demanded. "Who told you to take it up and walk?" But the man did not know. He had not asked for a name. And Jesus had slipped away into the crowd that filled the area.

Afterward, Jesus found the man in the temple. The man was standing in the courts — standing, on his own feet, in the place where he had once been carried. He was looking around at the temple with the eyes of a man seeing something familiar for the first time from a new height.

Jesus came to him. "See," he said, "you have been made well. Sin no more, so that nothing worse may happen to you."

The man went away. He found the Jewish leaders and told them that it was Jesus who had healed him.
