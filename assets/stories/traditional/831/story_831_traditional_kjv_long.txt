Two years had passed since Joseph last spake unto Pharaoh's chief butler. The underground prison wherein Joseph dwelt was dark and heavy with the savour of damp stone. Far above him, in the sunlit halls of Egypt's palace, Pharaoh rested restlessly, troubled by dreams that would not leave him.

And it came to pass at the end of two full years, that Pharaoh slept and dreamed. He remembered every vision clearly, as though it were burned into his mind. He stood beside the Nile, the river's surface shimmering beneath the Egyptian dawn. And, behold, there came up out of the river seven well favoured kine and fatfleshed, feeding among the reeds. Behind them, seven other kine came up after them, ill favoured and leanfleshed, and stood by the other kine. Suddenly, the seven lean kine did eat up the seven fat kine, yet remained as ill favoured as before. Pharaoh awoke. Confused, he drifted again to sleep.

He dreamed again. This time he beheld seven ears of corn upon one stalk, rank and good. After them sprang up seven thin ears blasted with the east wind. The thin ears devoured the rank and full ones. Pharaoh awoke, his spirit troubled and his breath uneasy.

Morning soon dawned. Sunlight slanted through carved windows into the great halls. Pharaoh's servants moved quietly, but the king's restlessness was unmistakable. Pharaoh sent and called for all the magicians of Egypt, and all the wise men thereof, and told them his dreams in turn. They hearkened carefully, yet there was none that could interpret them unto Pharaoh. The golden court, wont to be full of certainty and confidence, was now heavy with anxious whispers. No man understood what the dreams meant.

Then a voice broke the distressed murmurs. The chief butler, having served Pharaoh faithfully since his own release from the prison house, remembered something. A memory from two years past returned and pressed upon his conscience. Carefully, he stepped forward and spake with humility.

"I do remember my faults this day," the butler said unto Pharaoh. "Pharaoh was wroth with his servants, and put me in ward in the captain of the guard's house, both me and the chief baker. And we dreamed a dream in one night, he and I; we dreamed each man according to the interpretation of his dream. And there was there with us a young man, an Hebrew, servant to the captain of the guard. We told him, and he interpreted to us our dreams; to each man according to his dream he did interpret. And it came to pass, as he interpreted to us, so it was; me he restored unto mine office, and him he hanged."

The butler's words sent a ripple through the chamber. Pharaoh heard and acted at once. He sent for Joseph without delay.

In the lower depths of the palace, Joseph was hastily summoned from his cell. Men came unto him and bade him make ready at once. He washed, shaved, and changed his raiment. The linen was bright and clean, compared with prison rags. The light above ground was sharp in his eyes after so long in the shadow. At last, he was brought into Pharaoh's throne room — a place flooded with sunlight, gilded pillars, and the cool fragrance of myrrh.

Joseph stood before Pharaoh.

Pharaoh looked upon the young Hebrew, clothed now in foreign garments, and said unto Joseph, "I have dreamed a dream, and there is none that can interpret it: and I have heard say of thee, that thou canst understand a dream to interpret it."

Joseph answered directly, his voice steady. "It is not in me: God shall give Pharaoh an answer of peace."

Pharaoh leaned forward and recounted his dreams. "In my dream, behold, I stood upon the bank of the river. And, behold, there came up out of the river seven kine, fatfleshed and well favoured; and they fed in a meadow. And, behold, seven other kine came up after them, poor and very ill favoured and leanfleshed, such as I never saw in all the land of Egypt for badness. And the lean and the ill favoured kine did eat up the first seven fat kine. And when they had eaten them up, it could not be known that they had eaten them; but they were still ill favoured, as at the beginning. So I awoke."

He continued, "And I saw in my dream, and, behold, seven ears came up in one stalk, full and good. And, behold, seven ears, withered, thin, and blasted with the east wind, sprung up after them. And the thin ears devoured the seven good ears. And I told this unto the magicians; but there was none that could declare it to me."

The throne room was still. The gold and marble seemed colder with waiting. Joseph hearkened unto every word. Then he spake.

"The dream of Pharaoh is one: God hath shewed Pharaoh what he is about to do. The seven good kine are seven years; and the seven good ears are seven years: the dream is one."

Joseph's words moved slowly through the hall, echoed by the silent courtiers.

"And the seven thin and ill favoured kine that came up after them are seven years; and the seven empty ears blasted with the east wind shall be seven years of famine."

Joseph looked directly unto Pharaoh. "This is the thing which I have spoken unto Pharaoh: What God is about to do he sheweth unto Pharaoh. Behold, there come seven years of great plenty throughout all the land of Egypt: And there shall arise after them seven years of famine; and all the plenty shall be forgotten in the land of Egypt; and the famine shall consume the land. And the plenty shall not be known in the land by reason of that famine following; for it shall be very grievous."

Stepping forward, Joseph's words were clear and sure. "And for that the dream was doubled unto Pharaoh twice; it is because the thing is established by God, and God will shortly bring it to pass."

Now Joseph counselled Pharaoh with wisdom from above. "Now therefore let Pharaoh look out a man discreet and wise, and set him over the land of Egypt. Let Pharaoh do this, and let him appoint officers over the land, and take up the fifth part of the land of Egypt in the seven plenteous years. And let them gather all the food of those good years that come, and lay up corn under the hand of Pharaoh, and let them keep food in the cities. And that food shall be for store to the land against the seven years of famine, which shall be in the land of Egypt; that the land perish not through the famine."

The air lingered between the spoken words and the awaiting reply. The magicians and servants looked unto their king. Pharaoh took counsel with all his servants and considered the matter. The throne room, once harsh with tension, now filled with a new, cautious hope.

Pharaoh turned unto Joseph. "Can we find such a one as this is, a man in whom the Spirit of God is?"

He looked upon Joseph with favour and spake clearly that all might hear. "Forasmuch as God hath shewed thee all this, there is none so discreet and wise as thou art: Thou shalt be over my house, and according unto thy word shall all my people be ruled: only in the throne will I be greater than thou."

Pharaoh took off his signet ring, the sign of royal authority, and put it upon Joseph's hand. The ring was heavy and cold, its gold glinting in the sunlight. Servants beheld the exchange, the tokens of power passing from one hand to another.

The savour of incense drifted through the chamber. Pharaoh declared unto all that were present: "See, I have set thee over all the land of Egypt."
