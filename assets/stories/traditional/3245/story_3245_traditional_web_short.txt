On the third day, Laban learned that Jacob had fled. Taking his relatives, he pursued Jacob seven days’ journey and overtook him in the mountain of Gilead. God came to Laban the Syrian in a dream that night: “Be careful that you don’t speak to Jacob either good or bad.”

Jacob had pitched his tent in the mountain. Laban and his relatives camped there too. Laban accused Jacob of deceiving him and carrying his daughters away like captives of the sword. Why leave secretly, he demanded, when he might have sent them off with joy, songs, tambourine, and harp? Jacob had not even allowed him to kiss his sons and daughters. Laban called that foolish.

He claimed the power to hurt Jacob, but repeated the warning Jacob’s father’s God had given him the night before. Though Jacob greatly longed for his father’s house, Laban said, why had he stolen Laban’s gods?

Jacob answered that he had feared Laban would take his daughters back by force. Whoever had Laban’s gods should not live. Before their relatives, Laban could identify and take anything belonging to him. Jacob did not know Rachel had stolen the gods.

Laban entered Jacob’s tent, Leah’s tent, and the tent of the two female servants, without finding them. Leaving Leah’s tent, he entered Rachel’s. She had put the teraphim in the camel’s saddle and sat on them. Laban felt through the tent but found nothing. Rachel asked her father not to be angry that she could not rise, saying she was having her period. His search did not uncover the teraphim.

Now Jacob angrily challenged Laban. What trespass or sin had brought this hot pursuit? After searching everything, what household goods had Laban found? Let him set them before both men’s relatives to judge between them.

Jacob recounted twenty years of service. Laban’s ewes and female goats had not miscarried, he said, and he had eaten none of the flock’s rams. He had borne the loss of animals torn by beasts instead of bringing them to Laban. Laban had required payment for anything stolen, by day or night.

Drought had consumed him by day, frost by night, Jacob said, and sleep had fled his eyes. Fourteen of those twenty years were for Laban’s two daughters, six for his flock. Laban had changed his wages ten times.

Without his father’s God, the God of Abraham and the fear of Isaac, Jacob said, Laban would have sent him away empty. God had seen his affliction and the labor of his hands, and had rebuked Laban the night before.
