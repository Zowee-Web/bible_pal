The ark had been in the house of Abinadab for twenty years.

The men of Kiriath Jearim had come for it on the day after the trouble at Beth Shemesh. They had come up the road in a small, quiet group, and they had taken the ark, and they had brought it into the house on the hill. They had sanctified Eleazar his son to keep it. The wooden chest stood in the inner room with a cloth covering, and a lamp was kept burning beside it. The household moved quietly past the door. The children of Abinadab grew, and married, and brought their own children to the house, and the ark stood where it had been set.

Twenty harvests had passed under that roof. Twenty rainy seasons. The olives had been gathered twenty times in the grove behind the house. Eleazar's hair had gone gray at the temples. He kept the room swept. He kept the lamp filled. He kept silence in the threshold.

And all the house of Israel lamented after the Lord.

The lamenting went on in the villages. It went on in the fields at evening. The old men remembered the days before the ark had been taken into battle and lost. They remembered Eli, and the disaster at Aphek, and the news that had come back. The younger men only knew that the Lord seemed far away. The Philistines pressed the borders. They came up into the hill country at harvest. They took what they wished. The high places still smoked with offerings to other gods, because the people did not know how to come back. The lamps in the houses of Baal still burned. The shapes of Ashtaroth still stood in the household corners, small and familiar, fingered by women who feared the failure of the rains.

Then Samuel came among them.

He had been a child in the temple at Shiloh in the old days, and now he was a man. He spoke to all the house of Israel. The elders gathered to hear him. He stood among them with his staff in his hand, and his voice was steady, and it carried into the back of the crowd.

"If you do return to the Lord with all your heart," he said, "then put away the foreign gods and the Ashtaroth from among you, and direct your hearts to the Lord, and serve him only; and he will deliver you out of the hand of the Philistines."

He did not raise his voice. He did not need to. The words moved through them.

The people went home. They went into their houses, and they lifted down the shapes from the corners. They carried them out. They broke them on the stones of the courtyards. The Baals were gathered, and the Ashtaroth, and the small household gods that had been kept in cloth, and all of them were destroyed. The lamps that had burned to other gods were put out. The children of Israel served the Lord only.

Then Samuel said, "Gather all Israel to Mizpah, and I will pray for you to the Lord."

They came. They came up from the valleys and down from the hills, from the tribes scattered across the country. They gathered on the high place at Mizpah. The crowd spread across the open ground. And on that day they did a strange and quiet thing.

They drew water from the well. They poured it out before the Lord.

The water spread on the dust at their feet and was gone into the earth. Hand after hand poured a jar. The dust took it. The Lord received it. It was a sign with no explanation, and they made the sign together. They fasted on that day. They ate nothing. They sat in the gathered silence under the sun, and they said there, "We have sinned against the Lord." The words moved through the gathering like a single breath. Samuel judged the children of Israel in Mizpah.

While they were gathered, the lords of the Philistines heard. Word came down to the cities of the plain. The Philistines saw an army assembled at Mizpah where no army had been before, and they did not wait. They went up against Israel. The chariots came up the road. The footmen came on behind them.

The children of Israel heard it, and they were afraid of the Philistines.

The fear ran through the crowd. Men looked at one another. They had no formation. They had no plan of war. They had come to fast and to pour out water and to confess. They had not come to fight.

They came to Samuel. They said to him, "Don't cease to cry to the Lord our God for us, that he will save us out of the hand of the Philistines."

Samuel took a sucking lamb. He laid it on the altar at Mizpah. He offered it for a whole burnt offering to the Lord. The smoke went up. He cried to the Lord for Israel.

As he was offering up the burnt offering, the Philistines drew near. The line of them came on across the open ground. The dust of their advance rose against the sky. The men of Israel stood on the high place at Mizpah and watched. They had nothing in their hands but the staffs they had brought to gather. The smoke from the altar was still rising. Samuel was still on his knees.

Then the Lord thundered with a great thunder on that day upon the Philistines.

The sky cracked open above their line. The sound was not the sound of a storm. It was the voice of the Lord broken loose over them. The thunder rolled along their advance and tore through them. The horses bucked in the chariot harness. The drivers lost their reins. The footmen scattered. They were struck down before Israel and confused, and they ran.

The men of Israel saw it. They went out of Mizpah and pursued the Philistines. They smote them until they came under Beth Kar. The pursuit went on until the light began to fail, and then they came back, and the field was theirs.

When the dust had settled, Samuel walked out from the camp. He went a short way from Mizpah, to a place between Mizpah and Shen. He found a stone in the field. It was not a great stone. It was the kind of stone a man could lift with his own hands. He lifted it. He set it upright in the open ground.

Then he gave it a name.

"Ebenezer," he said. "The Lord helped us until now."

The stone stood there in the daylight. It was not a fortress. It was not a monument of war. It was a marker, set up by a man who had cried to the Lord and been heard. The word he had given it was not a boast. It was not even a triumph. It was an honest word, set in stone — that until this moment, all the way from the house of Abinadab on the hill, all the way through the twenty years of lamenting, all the way to the smoke of the burnt offering and the breaking of the Philistines, the Lord had helped.

The Philistines were subdued. They did not come any more within the border of Israel. The hand of the Lord was against the Philistines all the days of Samuel. The cities they had taken from Israel were restored, from Ekron even to Gath, and Israel recovered its border out of their hand. There was peace between Israel and the Amorites.

Samuel judged Israel all the days of his life. He went year by year in a circuit to Bethel, to Gilgal, to Mizpah. He judged the people in all those places. His return was always to Ramah, for there was his house, and there he built an altar to the Lord.

But the stone he had set between Mizpah and Shen kept standing. The grass grew up around its base. The wind moved past it. The light fell on it morning by morning. And whoever passed it knew its name.
