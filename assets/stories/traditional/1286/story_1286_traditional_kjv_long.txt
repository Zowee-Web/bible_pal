The ark had abode in the house of Abinadab twenty years.

The men of Kirjathjearim had come for it on the day after the trouble at Bethshemesh. They had come up the road in a small, quiet company, and they had fetched up the ark of the Lord, and brought it into the house in the hill. They had sanctified Eleazar his son to keep it. The wooden chest stood in the inner room with a cloth covering, and a lamp was kept burning beside it. The household moved quietly past the door. The children of Abinadab grew, and married, and brought their own children unto the house, and the ark stood where it had been set.

Twenty harvests had passed under that roof. Twenty rainy seasons. The olives had been gathered twenty times in the grove behind the house. Eleazar's hair had gone gray at the temples. He kept the room swept. He kept the lamp filled. He kept silence in the threshold.

And all the house of Israel lamented after the Lord.

The lamenting went on in the villages. It went on in the fields at evening. The old men remembered the days before the ark had been taken into battle and lost. They remembered Eli, and the disaster at Aphek, and the tidings that had come back. The younger men knew only that the Lord seemed far off. The Philistines pressed the borders. They came up into the hill country at harvest. They took what they would. The high places still smoked with offerings unto other gods, for the people knew not how to return. The lamps in the houses of Baal still burned. The shapes of Ashtaroth still stood in the household corners, small and familiar, fingered by women who feared the failure of the rains.

Then Samuel came among them.

He had been a child in the temple at Shiloh in the old days, and now he was a man. He spake unto all the house of Israel. The elders gathered to hear him. He stood among them with his staff in his hand, and his voice was steady, and it carried into the back of the throng.

"If ye do return unto the Lord with all your hearts," he said, "then put away the strange gods and Ashtaroth from among you, and prepare your hearts unto the Lord, and serve him only: and he will deliver you out of the hand of the Philistines."

He lifted not his voice. He had no need. The words moved through them.

The people went home. They went into their houses, and they took down the shapes from the corners. They carried them out. They brake them upon the stones of the courtyards. The Baalim were gathered, and the Ashtaroth, and the small household gods that had been kept in cloth, and all of them were destroyed. The lamps that had burned unto other gods were put out. The children of Israel served the Lord only.

Then Samuel said, "Gather all Israel to Mizpeh, and I will pray for you unto the Lord."

They came. They came up from the valleys and down from the hills, from the tribes scattered across the country. They gathered on the high place at Mizpeh. The throng spread across the open ground. And on that day they did a strange and quiet thing.

They drew water from the well. They poured it out before the Lord.

The water spread on the dust at their feet and was gone into the earth. Hand after hand poured a jar. The dust took it. The Lord received it. It was a sign with no explanation, and they made the sign together. They fasted on that day. They did eat nothing. They sat in the gathered silence under the sun, and they said there, "We have sinned against the Lord." The words moved through the gathering as a single breath. And Samuel judged the children of Israel in Mizpeh.

While they were gathered, the lords of the Philistines heard. Word came down to the cities of the plain. The Philistines saw a host assembled at Mizpeh where no host had been before, and they tarried not. They went up against Israel. The chariots came up the road. The footmen came on behind them.

The children of Israel heard it, and were afraid of the Philistines.

The fear ran through the throng. Men looked one upon another. They had no array. They had no plan of war. They had come to fast, and to pour out water, and to confess. They had not come to fight.

They came to Samuel. They said unto him, "Cease not to cry unto the Lord our God for us, that he will save us out of the hand of the Philistines."

Samuel took a sucking lamb. He laid it upon the altar at Mizpeh. He offered it for a burnt offering wholly unto the Lord. The smoke went up. He cried unto the Lord for Israel.

And as he was offering up the burnt offering, the Philistines drew near to battle against Israel. The line of them came on across the open ground. The dust of their advance rose against the sky. The men of Israel stood on the high place at Mizpeh and watched. They had nothing in their hands save the staves they had brought to gather. The smoke from the altar was still rising. Samuel was still upon his knees.

Then the Lord thundered with a great thunder on that day upon the Philistines.

The sky brake open above their line. The sound was not the sound of a storm. It was the voice of the Lord broken loose over them. The thunder rolled along their advance and tore through them. The horses bucked in the chariot harness. The drivers lost their reins. The footmen scattered. They were discomfited before Israel, and they fled.

The men of Israel beheld it. They went out of Mizpeh, and pursued the Philistines. They smote them, until they came under Bethcar. The pursuit went on until the light began to fail, and then they returned, and the field was theirs.

When the dust had settled, Samuel walked out from the camp. He went a short way from Mizpeh, to a place between Mizpeh and Shen. He found a stone in the field. It was not a great stone. It was the kind of stone a man could lift with his own hands. He took it up. He set it upright in the open ground.

Then he gave it a name.

"Ebenezer," he said. "Hitherto hath the Lord helped us."

The stone stood there in the daylight. It was not a fortress. It was not a monument of war. It was a marker, set up by a man who had cried unto the Lord and been heard. The word he had given it was not a boast. It was not even a triumph. It was an honest word, set in stone — that hitherto, all the way from the house of Abinadab in the hill, all the way through the twenty years of lamenting, all the way to the smoke of the burnt offering and the breaking of the Philistines, the Lord had helped.

So the Philistines were subdued. They came no more into the coast of Israel. The hand of the Lord was against the Philistines all the days of Samuel. The cities which the Philistines had taken from Israel were restored, from Ekron even unto Gath, and the coasts thereof did Israel deliver out of the hand of the Philistines. And there was peace between Israel and the Amorites.

Samuel judged Israel all the days of his life. He went from year to year in circuit to Bethel, to Gilgal, to Mizpeh. He judged the people in all those places. His return was to Ramah, for there was his house, and there he built an altar unto the Lord.

But the stone he had set between Mizpeh and Shen kept standing. The grass grew up about its base. The wind moved past it. The light fell upon it morning by morning. And whoever passed it knew its name.
