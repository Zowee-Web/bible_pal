The road wound between Samaria and Galilee, a track of pale dust pressed thin by the feet of many travelers. Stones lay scattered along its edges, worn smooth by wind and rain, and the scrub that grew in patches along the hillside was dry, its leaves curled and brittle. The heat of the day bore down upon the land, and the air hung still, carrying the faint smell of dry earth and sun-warmed rock.

Jesus walked that road, and his disciples moved with him, their sandals scraping against the hard ground. The dust rose in small clouds about their ankles. Before them, the outline of a village appeared — low walls of rough-cut stone, a few rooftops visible beyond a stand of withered trees. The road bent toward the village gate, and there the path narrowed between two low ridges of broken ground.

It was there, at the entrance to that village, that ten men stood together. They did not stand upon the road itself but kept their distance, gathered in a rough cluster upon the open ground beyond the path's edge. Their garments hung loose and torn, stained with dust and wear. Cloth wrappings covered portions of their hands, their arms, their faces — wound about the flesh where the disease had done its work. Some among them leaned upon staves of rough wood. Others stood with arms drawn close against their bodies. Their skin, where it could be seen, was marked and discolored, the texture changed, the surface broken.

They were lepers, every one of them. Ten men set apart from the village, from the road, from the common life of those who passed by. They stood where lepers stood — at a distance, beyond the reach of those who were whole.

And when they saw Jesus drawing near upon the road, they lifted up their voices. The sound carried across the open ground, rising above the stillness of the afternoon. Their cry was loud, urgent, the voices of ten men calling out together. They called him by name, and they called him Master, and they asked him for mercy. The words rang out plainly, without adornment — a petition spoken from where they stood, across the distance that the law and their affliction required them to keep.

Jesus stopped upon the road. He turned toward them. The dust settled about his feet. He looked upon them — ten men standing apart, their wrappings visible, their faces lifted toward him.

He spoke to them. His voice carried clearly across the space between. He told them to go and show themselves unto the priests.

That was all. No gesture of the hand laid upon their flesh. No command spoken over the disease itself. Only the instruction: go, and show yourselves unto the priests.

The ten men turned. They moved together at first, their staves tapping against the ground, their sandals raising dust. The road stretched out before them, and the village lay behind, and the distance between themselves and the priests was not a small thing. Yet they went. They set their feet upon the path and walked.

And it came to pass, as they went — not before they departed, not after they arrived, but as they walked, as their feet moved upon the road and the dust rose about them — that the change came upon them. The flesh beneath their wrappings grew whole. The marks upon their skin receded, the broken surfaces mending, the discoloration fading. Where the disease had eaten, the skin grew smooth and sound. Hands that had been numb regained their feeling. Fingers that had been stiff moved freely. The ten men walked, and as they walked, they were cleansed.

Nine of them continued forward. Their pace quickened. The road carried them onward toward the priests, toward the verification of what had been done, toward the restoration of their place among the living. Their footsteps grew fainter upon the path. The distance between them and the village widened.

But one of them stopped.

He stood still upon the road. He looked down at his hands. The wrappings hung loose where the swelling had gone. The skin beneath was whole, unbroken, restored. He turned. He turned his body back toward the place from which he had come, back toward the road where Jesus stood. And he began to walk — not onward toward the priests, but back. His steps were swift. The dust rose about his feet as he moved.

And as he drew near, his voice rose. He glorified God with a loud voice — not a whisper, not a murmur, but a cry that carried across the open ground, a declaration spoken aloud for any ear that might hear it. The words poured from his mouth, praising God, giving glory, his voice lifting above the stillness of the road and the dry hills and the scrub that lined the way.

He came to where Jesus stood. And there, before him, the man fell down upon his face. His body met the ground. His hands pressed flat against the dust of the road. His forehead touched the earth at the feet of Jesus. He lay prostrate, and from that posture he gave thanks. His voice, which had been loud upon the road, now spoke upward from the dust — words of gratitude, directed to the one who had spoken the command.

This man was a Samaritan.

Jesus spoke. He did not speak only to the man upon the ground. His words carried to those who stood near — the disciples, any who had gathered, any who could hear. He asked a question, and the question was plain. Were there not ten cleansed? He asked it openly, and the air held the words.

Then he asked another. Where were the nine? The road stretched empty in the direction the others had gone. No figures returned. No voices called back. The dust had settled where their feet had passed, and the road was quiet.

Jesus spoke again. He observed aloud that none had returned to give glory to God save this stranger — this one, this Samaritan, this man who was not of the house of Israel, who had come back and pressed his face into the dust and lifted his voice in praise.

The man lay still upon the ground. The dust clung to his garments, to his restored hands, to the cloth that had once covered his affliction. The sun bore down upon his back.

Then Jesus spoke to him directly. He told him to arise. The man lifted himself from the earth. Dust fell from his garments, from his knees, from his palms. He rose to his feet and stood before Jesus upon the road. His face was uncovered now, the skin whole, the features clear.

Jesus told him to go his way. And he spoke one thing more — that the man's faith had made him whole.

The man stood upon the road. The village gate was near. The hills rose dry and pale on either side. The air was still, and the dust lay quiet upon the ground where he had pressed his face. He stood whole, his flesh restored, his garments hanging loose where the wrappings had been, the voice that had cried out for mercy now silent, the voice that had glorified God still echoing against the stones.

He turned, and he went his way. The road received his footsteps. The nine were gone ahead, somewhere beyond the bend, somewhere upon the long path toward the priests. The dust settled behind them all. Jesus stood upon the road, and the village lay before him, and the afternoon light fell long across the pale ground of that place between Samaria and Galilee.
