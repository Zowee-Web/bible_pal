The road stretched between Samaria and Galilee, a dry track of packed earth worn smooth by feet and hooves and the wooden wheels of carts. Dust hung in the still air, pale and fine, catching what light came through the haze of midday. The land on either side lay brown and sparse — low scrub, a few twisted olive trees with silver-green leaves, stones half-buried in the dirt. Jesus walked along this road with his disciples, their sandals scuffing against the ground, their robes gathering dust at the hems. They were heading toward Jerusalem, and the road led them near the edge of a village.

The village sat close to the road, a cluster of flat-roofed houses built from rough stone, their walls the color of the earth around them. A few goats stood in the shade of a wall. Somewhere inside the village, a dog barked once and then fell silent. But before Jesus and his company reached the first houses, voices rose from a distance — not from the village itself, but from a place set apart, off to the side of the road where the ground sloped down toward a shallow ravine.

Ten men stood there, gathered together in a loose group. Their clothing hung ragged and stained. Strips of cloth covered parts of their faces, wrapped around hands, wound over forearms. The skin that was visible showed the marks of leprosy — patches of white and raw pink, swollen places, fingers shortened or curled. Some of them leaned on sticks. One man's feet were bound in layers of torn fabric, and he shifted his weight carefully from one foot to the other. Another had a cloth pulled across the lower half of his face so that only his eyes showed above it, red-rimmed and dry.

They stood at a distance, as the law required. The space between them and the road was perhaps fifty paces — enough that the travelers could see them clearly but could not smell the vinegar and herbs some of them used to dress their sores. The ten had gathered from different places, drawn together by their shared condition. Among them was a Samaritan, standing shoulder to shoulder with the others, distinctions of heritage set aside in this company of affliction.

When they saw Jesus approaching with his group, they lifted their voices together. The sound carried across the open ground, rough and loud, the cry of men accustomed to calling out from a distance.

"Jesus, Master, have mercy on us!"

The words came ragged and overlapping, some voices deeper, some thinner, one cracking midway through. They called again, and the sound echoed faintly off the stone walls of the nearest house. A few of the disciples slowed their pace and turned to look. Jesus stopped on the road.

He looked toward the ten men standing in that barren strip of ground. The wind stirred the dust between them. One of the lepers leaned forward on his stick, his wrapped hand gripping the wood. Another stood with both arms raised, palms open, the cloth around his wrists fluttering slightly.

Jesus called back to them, his voice clear and carrying easily across the distance.

"Go and show yourselves to the priests."

That was all he said. No gesture beyond the words. No step toward them. The ten men stood still for a moment, the instruction hanging in the dry air between them. Then they turned. One by one and then together, they began to walk. Some moved slowly — the man with the bound feet placed each step with care on the uneven ground. Others walked faster, their sticks tapping against rock and hard earth. They moved away from the road, heading in the direction a priest might be found, following the instruction they had been given.

As they walked, it happened. The change came upon them while they were in motion, while their feet were still carrying them forward along the path. The white patches on one man's arm began to close, the skin drawing together, color returning like water soaking into dry cloth. Another man stopped and pulled the wrapping from his hand — his fingers were straight, the swelling gone, the skin smooth and whole. He flexed his hand open and shut. A third man touched his own face and then pulled the cloth away from it entirely, running his fingers along his jaw, his cheek, finding nothing but clean skin.

All ten of them were made clean as they walked. The leprosy left their bodies. Sores closed. Swelling receded. Damaged skin became new. They looked at themselves, at each other, and they kept walking toward the priests, their pace quickening now, their sticks abandoned on the ground behind them.

But one of them stopped.

He stood in the middle of the path, looking down at his own hands. He turned them over slowly. The wrappings had fallen loose, and beneath them his skin was whole — brown and smooth, unbroken. He looked up in the direction the other nine were walking, their figures growing smaller as they moved away. Then he turned around.

He walked back the way he had come. His stride was different now — no limp, no careful placement of weight. His feet struck the ground firmly. As he came closer to the road where Jesus still stood with his disciples, his voice rose. He was praising God, loudly, the words tumbling out in a shout that scattered a pair of birds from a nearby bush. His voice was hoarse but strong, and it rang across the open space.

"Glory to God! Praise to God!"

He did not stop at the distance where he had stood before. He came all the way to the road, all the way to where Jesus was, and there he fell on his face at Jesus' feet. His forehead pressed against the dust of the road. His hands lay flat on the packed earth on either side of his head. His shoulders shook as he spoke, his words directed downward into the ground and upward at the same time.

"Thank you. Thank you."

He lay there at Jesus' feet, this man, this Samaritan. The dust clung to his clean skin, to his forehead, to the palms of his newly whole hands. The other travelers on the road had stopped to watch. The disciples stood nearby, some of them looking back down the path where the other nine had disappeared from view.

Jesus spoke. He did not speak only to the man on the ground. His voice was pitched so that those around him could hear, and the words came in the form of questions.

"Weren't the ten cleansed? But where are the nine?"

He paused. The road was quiet. No answer came. The wind moved through the scrub brush with a dry whisper.

"Were there none found who returned to give glory to God, except this foreigner?"

The word hung in the air — foreigner. The Samaritan remained on the ground, his face still pressed to the earth near the dust-covered leather of Jesus' sandals. The sun bore down on the road and on the man's back, warming the clean skin beneath his ragged clothing.

Then Jesus spoke to him directly.

"Get up, and go your way. Your faith has made you well."

The man lifted his head. He pushed himself up from the ground, first to his knees, then to his feet. Dust covered the front of his robe and his hands and his face. He stood before Jesus, upright, his body whole. The road stretched behind him toward the village, and ahead of him in the direction of Samaria. The other nine were long gone, somewhere on the path to the priests, already out of sight beyond the low hills.

The Samaritan stood there on the road for a moment, his cleaned hands hanging at his sides. Then he turned and walked, his footsteps steady on the dry ground, moving away under the open sky.
