Hathach came back through the palace corridors. His sandals were quiet on the polished stone. He passed the guards at the inner doors and entered the queen's chambers, where Esther waited with her attendants around her.

He stood before the queen and told her everything. Every word Mordecai had spoken at the king's gate — the exact sum of silver Haman had promised to pay into the royal treasury, the decree that had been issued, the copy of the written order for the destruction of all the Jews in every province. Mordecai had given Hathach the document itself so that Esther could see it with her own eyes. And Mordecai had charged him to urge the queen to go before the king and plead for her people.

Esther listened. The room was still. Light came through the high windows and fell across the tiled floor in long pale rectangles.

Then she gave Hathach her answer to carry back.

"Any man or woman who comes to the king in the inner court without being called — there is one law for them. Death. Unless the king holds out the golden scepter. Then that person lives." She looked at Hathach directly. "I have not been called to come to the king these thirty days."

Hathach bowed and left. He crossed the open courtyards under the wide sky and passed through the palace gate to the city square where Mordecai stood waiting in sackcloth. The ashes were still on his head. People moved around him in the street, going about their business.

Hathach delivered the queen's words. All of them.

Mordecai listened. His face did not change. The sackcloth hung rough against his arms and shoulders. He was quiet for a moment, standing in the noise of the square — merchants calling, carts rolling over stone, the sounds of Shushan going on as though nothing had happened.

Then he gave his reply. He spoke it carefully, word by word, so Hathach would carry it exactly.

"Do not think to yourself that you will escape in the king's house any more than all the other Jews. If you stay altogether silent at this time, relief and rescue will rise for the Jews from another place. But you and your father's house will be destroyed."

He paused. The square moved around him, full of dust and afternoon light.

"And who knows whether you have come to the kingdom for such a time as this?"

Hathach turned back toward the palace. He walked through the gate, past the guards who knew him by now, through the long corridors with their columns and their shadows, and into the queen's chambers once more.

He repeated Mordecai's words to Esther. Every one. The attendants stood nearby, their hands still, their faces turned toward the queen.

Esther was quiet. She stood in the center of the room. The light had shifted since Hathach had left — it came lower now through the windows and touched the far wall. No one spoke. The sound of the palace around them — distant footsteps, a door closing somewhere far off — seemed very small.

Then Esther lifted her head and gave her command.

"Go," she said. "Gather together all the Jews who are in Shushan. Fast for me. Do not eat. Do not drink. Three days. Night and day."

Her voice was steady. It did not waver.

"My young women and I will fast the same way."

She looked at Hathach. Her hands were at her sides. Her chin was level.

"And then I will go in to the king. It is not according to the law." She drew one breath. "And if I perish, I perish."

Hathach bowed and left for the last time that day. He walked through the corridors, out through the gate, across the open ground to where Mordecai still stood in his sackcloth in the city square. The shadows had grown longer. The dust hung golden in the low sun.

He delivered the queen's words. Mordecai listened. He did not ask anything else. He did not send another message.

The square was thinning out as the day faded. Merchants were pulling cloths over their goods. A dog crossed the open ground between the stalls. Somewhere a child called out and was answered.

Mordecai turned from the gate and went his way through the streets of Shushan. He did everything that Esther had commanded him.
