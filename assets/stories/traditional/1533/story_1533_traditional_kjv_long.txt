Hathach returned through the corridors of the palace. Soft were his sandals upon the polished stone. The walls rose high on either hand, graven with figures and bound about with long bands of color, and the air bore the faint savour of oil from the lamps that burned in their brackets. He passed the guards at the inner doors. They stayed him not. They knew his countenance. And he entered the chambers of the queen, where Esther waited, her handmaidens gathered about her in a loose half-circle.

He stood before the queen and told her all. Every word that Mordecai had spoken at the king's gate. The sum of silver that Haman had promised to weigh into the royal treasury. The decree that had gone forth in the king's name. The copy of the writing for the destruction of all the Jews in every province of the empire — young and old, women and children, in one day. Mordecai had put into Hathach's hand the document itself, sealed and folded, that Esther might behold it with her own eyes. And Mordecai had charged him to entreat the queen, that she go in unto the king and make supplication for her people.

Esther hearkened. The chamber was still. One handmaiden drew a slow breath. The light came through the high windows and fell upon the tiled floor in long pale measures, catching upon the coloured stones at the borders of the patterns. A bird cried somewhere in the palace garden, and was silent again.

Then gave she Hathach her answer to bear back.

"Whosoever cometh unto the king into the inner court, being not called — one law there is. Death. Save the king hold forth the golden sceptre. Then liveth that one." She looked upon Hathach steadfastly. Her hands were folded before her. "I have not been called to come unto the king these thirty days."

Hathach bowed and went forth. He crossed the open courts beneath the wide sky. The sun was past his height, and the heat lay heavy upon the stones. He went through the gate of the palace into the city square, where Mordecai stood waiting in sackcloth. The ashes were yet upon his head, gray against his hair and his temples. The people passed about him in the street, going every man unto his business. A water-seller went by with his clay vessels balanced upon a pole across his shoulders. Two women bare a basket between them. None of them stayed.

Hathach delivered the words of the queen. All of them.

Mordecai hearkened. His countenance changed not. The sackcloth hung rough against his arms and shoulders, and the hem of it was darkened where his hands had rubbed upon the cloth. He was silent a little while, standing in the noise of the square — the merchants crying their wares, the carts rolling upon stone, the bray of an ass somewhere down a side street, the sounds of Shushan continuing as though no thing had come to pass.

Then he made his answer. He spake it with care, word by word, that Hathach might carry it true.

"Think not within thine own heart that thou shalt escape in the king's house, more than all the Jews. If thou altogether holdest thy peace at this time, then shall enlargement and deliverance arise to the Jews from another place. But thou and thy father's house shall be destroyed."

He paused. The square moved about him, full of dust and the light of the afternoon. A gust of wind lifted the corner of an awning at a stall nearby, and let it fall again.

"And who knoweth whether thou art come to the kingdom for such a time as this?"

Hathach turned again toward the palace. He passed through the gate, by the guards who knew him now. They beheld him as he went by, and spake not. He went through the long corridors with their pillars and their shadows, his sandals quiet again upon the stone, and entered once more into the chambers of the queen.

He repeated the words of Mordecai unto Esther. Every one. The handmaidens stood nigh, their hands still, their faces turned toward the queen. One among them held a folded cloth, and seemed not to remember that she held it.

Esther was silent. She stood in the midst of the chamber. The light had moved since Hathach had gone forth — it came lower now through the windows, and touched the far wall, warming the painted plaster thereon. No man spake. The sound of the palace round about them — distant footsteps in some other passage, a door shutting afar off, the faint clatter of a tray set down — seemed very small.

Then Esther lifted up her head and gave her command.

"Go," said she. "Gather together all the Jews that are in Shushan. Fast ye for me. Eat not. Drink not. Three days. Night and day."

Her voice was steady. It wavered not. One of the handmaidens pressed her lips together.

"I also and my maidens will fast in like manner."

She looked upon Hathach. Her hands were at her sides. Her chin was level. She turned not away.

"And so will I go in unto the king. It is not according to the law." She drew one breath. "And if I perish, I perish."

Hathach bowed and went forth that day for the last time. He went through the corridors more slowly now, out through the gate, across the open ground to where Mordecai yet stood in his sackcloth in the city square. The shadows were grown long. The dust hung golden in the low sun, drifting where feet had passed. A cool current was beginning to move through the streets as the heat slackened.

He delivered the words of the queen. Mordecai hearkened. He asked nothing further. He sent no other message. He stood a moment with his hands at his sides, and looked once toward the high walls of the palace beyond the gate.

The square was thinning as the day waned. Merchants were drawing cloths over their wares and setting their baskets against the walls of their stalls. A dog crossed the open ground between the stalls, his nose low unto the dust. Somewhere a child cried out, and was answered. The light upon the upper stones of the buildings was gone from gold unto a softer hue, and the heaven above the rooftops began to deepen.

Mordecai turned from the gate and went his way through the streets of Shushan. And he did all that Esther had commanded him.
