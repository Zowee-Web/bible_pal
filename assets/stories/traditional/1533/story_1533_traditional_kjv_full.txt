Hathach returned by way of the palace corridors. His sandals made little sound upon the polished stone. He passed the guards who stood at the inner doors and came into the chambers of the queen, where Esther waited, and her attendants round about her.

And he stood before the queen and told her all things. Every word that Mordecai had spoken at the king's gate he set before her — the full sum of silver that Haman had pledged to weigh into the royal treasury, the decree that had gone forth, the copy of the written commandment for the destruction of all the Jews throughout every province. For Mordecai had placed the document itself into his hand, that Esther might look upon it with her own eyes. And Mordecai had charged him earnestly to bid the queen go in before the king and make supplication for her people.

Esther heard him. The chamber was still. Light fell through the high windows and lay upon the tiled floor in long pale bands.

Then she gave unto Hathach her answer, that he might bear it back.

"Whatsoever man or woman cometh unto the king in the inner court, not having been summoned — there is but one law appointed for them. It is death." She paused, and looked upon Hathach directly. "Save only if the king stretch forth the golden sceptre; then shall that one live." Her gaze did not waver. "And I have not been called to come before the king these thirty days."

Hathach bowed and departed. He crossed the open courtyards beneath the broad sky and passed through the palace gate unto the city square, where Mordecai stood waiting in sackcloth. The ashes were yet upon his head. People moved about him in the street, going each one about his business.

And Hathach delivered the words of the queen. All of them.

Mordecai heard him. His countenance did not change. The sackcloth hung coarse against his arms and his shoulders. He was silent a moment, standing amid the noise of the square — merchants crying their wares, carts rolling upon stone, the sounds of Shushan going on as though no thing had come to pass.

Then he gave his answer. He spake it with care, word by word, that Hathach might carry it back in its fulness.

"Think not within thyself that thou shalt find refuge in the king's house beyond all other Jews." His voice was low and certain. "If thou hold thy peace altogether at such a time as this, then shall deliverance and rescue arise for the Jews from some other quarter."

He let the words rest upon the air. Then he continued.

"But thou, and thy father's house, shall be brought to destruction."

He paused. The square moved about him, full of dust and the light of the afternoon.

"And who can say whether thou art not come unto the kingdom for this very hour?"

Hathach turned again toward the palace. He went in at the gate, past the guards who by now knew his face, through the long corridors with their columns and their shadows, and came once more into the chambers of the queen.

And he repeated unto Esther the words of Mordecai. Every one. The attendants stood nigh, their hands still, their faces turned toward the queen.

Esther was silent. She stood in the midst of the room. The light had shifted since Hathach had gone out from her — it came lower now through the windows and touched upon the far wall. No one spake. The sounds of the palace about them — a footfall in the distance, a door shutting somewhere far off — seemed a very small thing.

Then Esther lifted her head and gave her commandment.

"Go," she said. "Gather together all the Jews that are found in Shushan." Her voice was steady and did not falter. "Fast ye for me. Neither eat nor drink. Three days. Night and day."

She drew breath.

"I also, and my young women, will fast in like manner."

She looked upon Hathach. Her hands were at her sides. Her chin was level.

"And afterward I will go in unto the king, though it be not according to the law." She drew one breath. "And if I perish, I perish."

Hathach bowed and went out from her presence for the last time that day. He walked through the corridors, out through the gate, across the open ground to the place where Mordecai yet stood in his sackcloth in the city square. The shadows had grown long. The dust hung golden in the low sun.

And he delivered unto him the words of the queen. Mordecai heard them. He asked nothing further. He sent no other message.

The square was thinning as the day waned. Merchants drew cloths over their goods. A dog crossed the open ground between the stalls. Somewhere a child cried out and was answered.

And Mordecai turned from the gate and went his way through the streets of Shushan. And he did according to all that Esther had commanded him.
