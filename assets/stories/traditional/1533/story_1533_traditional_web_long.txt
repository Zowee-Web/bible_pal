Hathach came back through the palace corridors. His sandals were quiet on the polished stone. The walls rose high on either side of him, lined with carved figures and long bands of color, and the air carried the faint smell of oil from the lamps that burned in their brackets. He passed the guards at the inner doors. They did not stop him. They knew his face. He entered the queen's chambers, where Esther waited with her attendants gathered around her in a loose half-circle.

He stood before the queen and told her everything. Every word Mordecai had spoken at the king's gate. The exact sum of silver Haman had promised to pay into the royal treasury. The decree that had been issued in the king's name. The copy of the written order for the destruction of all the Jews in every province of the empire — young and old, women and children, on a single day. Mordecai had given Hathach the document itself, sealed and folded, so that Esther could see it with her own eyes. And Mordecai had charged him to urge the queen to go before the king and plead for her people.

Esther listened. The room was still. One of the attendants drew a slow breath. Light came through the high windows and fell across the tiled floor in long pale rectangles, catching on the colored stones at the edges of the patterns. A bird called somewhere outside in the palace garden and went silent again.

Then she gave Hathach her answer to carry back.

"Any man or woman who comes to the king in the inner court without being called — there is one law for them. Death. Unless the king holds out the golden scepter. Then that person lives." She looked at Hathach directly. Her hands were folded in front of her. "I have not been called to come to the king these thirty days."

Hathach bowed and left. He crossed the open courtyards under the wide sky. The sun was past its height now, and the heat lay heavy on the stones. He passed through the palace gate to the city square where Mordecai stood waiting in sackcloth. The ashes were still on his head, gray against his hair and his temples. People moved around him in the street, going about their business. A water-seller passed with his clay jars balanced on a pole across his shoulders. Two women carried a basket between them. None of them stopped.

Hathach delivered the queen's words. All of them.

Mordecai listened. His face did not change. The sackcloth hung rough against his arms and shoulders, and the edges of it were darkened where his hands had rubbed against the cloth. He was quiet for a moment, standing in the noise of the square — merchants calling, carts rolling over stone, the bray of a donkey somewhere down a side street, the sounds of Shushan going on as though nothing had happened.

Then he gave his reply. He spoke it carefully, word by word, so Hathach would carry it exactly.

"Do not think to yourself that you will escape in the king's house any more than all the other Jews. If you stay altogether silent at this time, relief and rescue will rise for the Jews from another place. But you and your father's house will be destroyed."

He paused. The square moved around him, full of dust and afternoon light. A gust of wind lifted the corner of an awning at a nearby stall and let it fall again.

"And who knows whether you have come to the kingdom for such a time as this?"

Hathach turned back toward the palace. He walked through the gate, past the guards who knew him by now. They watched him pass and said nothing. He went through the long corridors with their columns and their shadows, his sandals quiet again on the stone, and into the queen's chambers once more.

He repeated Mordecai's words to Esther. Every one. The attendants stood nearby, their hands still, their faces turned toward the queen. One of them held a folded cloth and did not seem to remember she was holding it.

Esther was quiet. She stood in the center of the room. The light had shifted since Hathach had left — it came lower now through the windows and touched the far wall, warming the painted plaster there. No one spoke. The sound of the palace around them — distant footsteps in another corridor, a door closing somewhere far off, the faint clatter of a tray being set down — seemed very small.

Then Esther lifted her head and gave her command.

"Go," she said. "Gather together all the Jews who are in Shushan. Fast for me. Do not eat. Do not drink. Three days. Night and day."

Her voice was steady. It did not waver. One of the attendants pressed her lips together.

"My young women and I will fast the same way."

She looked at Hathach. Her hands were at her sides. Her chin was level. She did not look away.

"And then I will go in to the king. It is not according to the law." She drew one breath. "And if I perish, I perish."

Hathach bowed and left for the last time that day. He walked through the corridors, slower now, out through the gate, across the open ground to where Mordecai still stood in his sackcloth in the city square. The shadows had grown longer. The dust hung golden in the low sun, drifting where feet had passed. A cool current was beginning to move through the streets as the heat eased.

He delivered the queen's words. Mordecai listened. He did not ask anything else. He did not send another message. He stood for a moment with his hands at his sides and looked once toward the high walls of the palace beyond the gate.

The square was thinning out as the day faded. Merchants were pulling cloths over their goods and stacking baskets against the walls of their stalls. A dog crossed the open ground between the stalls, nose low to the dust. Somewhere a child called out and was answered. The light on the upper stones of the buildings had gone from gold to a softer color, and the sky above the rooftops was beginning to deepen.

Mordecai turned from the gate and went his way through the streets of Shushan. He did everything that Esther had commanded him.
