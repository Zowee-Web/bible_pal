The servant spake of what had been given unto him, and the manner in which he spake of it was very quiet. "The Lord GOD hath given me," he said, "the tongue of the learned, that I should know how to speak a word in season to him that is weary."

He paused upon that word — weary — and then he went on.

"He wakeneth my ear morning by morning," the servant said. "He wakeneth it to hear as the learned. The Lord GOD hath opened mine ear. And I was not rebellious. Neither turned I away back."

He told what he had given in exchange for the opened ear. "I gave my back to the smiters, and my cheeks to them that plucked off the hair. I hid not my face from shame and spitting."

He named that which held him steady through it. "For the Lord GOD will help me. Therefore shall I not be confounded. Therefore have I set my face like a flint, and I know that I shall not be ashamed. He is near that justifieth me. Who will contend with me? Let us stand together. Who is mine adversary? Let him come near."

The servant fell silent again. The morning was coming on round about him, the same morning wherein his ear had been opened that day, the manner in which it had been opened on every other day since the first. The light came across the floor of the chamber wherein he had been speaking. The doves without the high window had begun to call.

The servant sat where he had been sitting. The day should have its weight, and it should press upon him as the days had pressed. The tongue that had been given him was for the weary, and there should be weary ones in the country or ever the sun was very high.

He waited for them in the small light at the table.
