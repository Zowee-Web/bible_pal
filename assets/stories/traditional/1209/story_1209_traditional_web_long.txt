When the days of Jacob drew near to die, he called for his sons.

The room was small and warm. A single oil lamp burned on a low stand near the head of the bed, its flame steady, its light reaching no farther than the close walls. The walls were of pale stone, smoothed by the hands of those who had built the house long before. The bed was a low pallet laid against the floor, layered with woven coverings — wool dyed soft brown, linen worn pale with use, a heavier blanket drawn up to the old man's chest. Jacob sat propped against a folded cloth. His white hair fell loose to his shoulders. His beard had thinned. His hands rested flat upon his thighs, the veins standing dark beneath the skin.

His twelve sons came in.

They came one by one through the low doorway. Grown men, weathered by sun and travel and years of tending flocks in open country. The eldest were gray at the temples and bearded heavy. The youngest still carried the leanness of a younger frame. They gathered around the bed. Some stood. Some knelt close on the floor. The lamplight fell on their faces and on the woven coverings of the bed.

The room was silent.

Jacob lifted his eyes.

"Gather yourselves together," he said, "that I may tell you that which will happen to you in the days to come. Assemble yourselves, and hear, you sons of Jacob. Listen to Israel your father."

Then he began.

He named them in the order of their birth. He spoke each name aloud into the small room, and the room held still around each one.

Reuben.

The eldest. The firstborn. Jacob's voice steadied on the name, and there was tenderness in it, and there was sorrow. "Reuben, you are my firstborn, my might, and the beginning of my strength, excelling in dignity, and excelling in power." Reuben stood at the side of the bed. His face was lifted, his eyes wet. Then the blessing turned. "Boiling over like water, you shall not excel." Reuben's chin lowered. He did not speak. The lamp burned on.

Simeon and Levi.

Jacob named them together. His hands tightened on his thighs. His voice did not rise but it grew firmer. He spoke of swords as weapons of violence. He spoke of anger fierce and wrath cruel. "I will divide them in Jacob, and scatter them in Israel." The two brothers stood close together at the foot of the bed. They said nothing. Their faces did not move.

Then Jacob said the name of Judah.

Judah was the fourth son. The blessing landed on him like a hand laid upon the head. Jacob's voice rose, steadier now, carrying the cadence of a man who saw what he was saying as he said it. "Judah, your brothers will praise you. Your hand will be on the neck of your enemies. Your father's sons will bow down before you." Judah stood at the foot of the bed. His shoulders straightened. His eyes did not leave his father's face.

"Judah is a lion's cub. From the prey, my son, you have gone up. He stooped down, he crouched as a lion, as a lioness. Who will rouse him up?"

The image filled the small room. The lion at rest after the kill. The prey already taken. The shoulders folded down low to the earth, the great body holding still, the eyes open and watching. The other sons heard it. None moved.

"The scepter will not depart from Judah," Jacob said, "nor the ruler's staff from between his feet, until he comes to whom it belongs. The obedience of the peoples will be to him."

Jacob's hand lifted slightly from his thigh. His fingers spread, then settled again.

"Binding his foal to the vine, his donkey's colt to the choice vine, he has washed his garments in wine, his robes in the blood of grapes. His eyes will be red with wine, his teeth white with milk."

The lamp guttered once and steadied. Judah stood where he stood, unmoving, the prophecy resting on him like a weight that had been waiting a long time to find its place.

Jacob spoke on.

Zebulun. Brief. He would dwell at the haven of the sea, a haven for ships, his border on Sidon. Issachar. A strong donkey lying down between the saddlebags. He had seen the land and found it pleasant, and he would bow his shoulder to the burden. Each name a brief shape in the lamplight. Each blessing carried on the same patient rhythm of the old man's breathing.

Then Dan.

"Dan will judge his people, as one of the tribes of Israel." Jacob paused. He drew a slow breath, and when he spoke again his voice came lower, with the curl of the image inside it. "Dan will be a serpent on the trail, an adder in the path, that bites the horse's heels, so that his rider falls backward."

The serpent moved in the room. The thin shape on the trail, the bite, the horseman tipping back from the saddle.

Then Jacob paused longer. His eyes closed. His head turned slightly toward the lamp. When he spoke again the words came almost to himself, quiet, half-prayer.

"I have waited for your salvation, Yahweh."

The sons heard it. They did not move.

Then Jacob opened his eyes and went on.

Gad. A troop would press on him, but he would press on their heel. Asher. His food would be rich. He would produce royal dainties. Naphtali. A doe set free, who bears beautiful fawns. Each son standing where he stood. Each name spoken into the lamplit room.

Then Joseph.

Jacob's eyes found him at the side of the bed. Joseph knelt close, his head bowed, his hands resting on the edge of the covering. The lamp lit one side of his face.

Jacob's voice softened and warmed.

"Joseph is a fruitful vine, a fruitful vine by a spring. His branches run over the wall."

The picture lifted in the small room. Green leaves spilling over a stone boundary. Fruit hanging heavy on the running branches. Water at the root.

"The archers have severely grieved him, shot at him, and persecuted him. But his bow remained strong. The arms of his hands were made strong, by the hands of the Mighty One of Jacob — from there is the shepherd, the stone of Israel — even by the God of your father, who will help you, by the Almighty, who will bless you, with blessings of heaven above, blessings of the deep that lies below, blessings of the breasts, and of the womb."

Joseph's head bowed lower. His shoulders moved with his breathing.

"The blessings of your father," Jacob said, "have prevailed above the blessings of my ancestors, above the boundaries of the ancient hills. They will be on the head of Joseph, on the crown of the head of him who is separated from his brothers."

The room was still. The lamp burned.

Last came Benjamin.

The youngest. Jacob's voice gathered itself for one final image. "Benjamin is a ravenous wolf. In the morning he will devour the prey. At evening he will divide the plunder."

The blessings ended.

The twelve sons stood around the bed. The lamp burned at the head of the pallet, the flame holding steady, the oil pooling clear at the base of the wick. The old man looked at each of his sons in turn. His eyes moved slowly across their faces.

Then he gave them one more word.

He told them where he was to be buried — in the cave of Machpelah, in the field Abraham had bought from Ephron the Hittite. Where Abraham and Sarah lay. Where Isaac and Rebekah lay. Where he had buried Leah. His voice was low now, almost spent. He named each name, slowly, the buried ones, the field, the cave, the land.

When he had finished charging his sons, he gathered up his feet into the bed.

He breathed his last.

He was gathered to his people.

The lamp burned on in the still room. The twelve sons stood around their father, and the words he had spoken stayed with them — son by son, blessing by blessing — long after the breath had gone from his lips and the small flame burned alone.
