When the days of Jacob drew nigh that he must die, he called for his sons.

The room was small. A single lamp burned on a low stand, its flame steady, the oil pooling clear at the base of the wick. The walls were close. The bed was a low pallet near the floor, layered with woven cloths and a heavier covering drawn up unto Jacob's breast. The old man sat propped against a folded blanket, his white hair loose against his shoulders, his hands resting flat upon his thighs.

The sons came in. Twelve men, grown and weathered, the eldest already gray at the temples, the youngest still carrying the leanness of a younger frame. They gathered about the bed, some standing, some kneeling close. The lamplight fell upon their faces. They were silent.

Jacob lifted up his eyes.

"Gather yourselves together," he said, "that I may tell you that which shall befall you in the last days. Gather yourselves together, and hear, ye sons of Jacob; and hearken unto Israel your father."

Then he began to speak. He named them one by one, in the order of their birth, and the room held still around each name.

Reuben.

The eldest. Jacob's voice steadied on the word, gentle and grieved at once. "Reuben, thou art my firstborn, my might, and the beginning of my strength, the excellency of dignity, and the excellency of power. Unstable as water, thou shalt not excel." Reuben lowered his eyes. The lamp burned on.

Simeon and Levi. Jacob's hands tightened upon his thighs as he named them together. He spake of swords and of anger fierce and cruel. "Instruments of cruelty are in their habitations. I will divide them in Jacob, and scatter them in Israel." The two brothers stood close to each other and said nothing.

Then Jacob said the name of Judah.

Judah was the fourth son. The blessing landed upon him like a hand upon the head. Jacob's voice rose, steadier now, with the cadence of prophecy. "Judah, thou art he whom thy brethren shall praise. Thy hand shall be in the neck of thine enemies. Thy father's children shall bow down before thee." Judah stood at the foot of the bed. His shoulders straightened.

"Judah is a lion's whelp. From the prey, my son, thou art gone up. He stooped down, he couched as a lion, and as an old lion. Who shall rouse him up?" The image filled the small room. The lion at rest, the prey already taken, the gathered strength holding still. "The sceptre shall not depart from Judah, nor a lawgiver from between his feet, until Shiloh come; and unto him shall the gathering of the people be." Jacob's hand lifted slightly from his thigh. "His eyes shall be red with wine, and his teeth white with milk."

The lamp guttered once and steadied. Judah did not move.

Jacob spake on. Zebulun by the sea. Issachar lying down between two burdens. Each name a brief shape, each blessing carried upon the same patient rhythm.

Then Dan.

"Dan shall be a serpent by the way, an adder in the path, that biteth the horse heels, so that his rider shall fall backward." Jacob paused. He drew a slow breath. Then his voice went quieter, almost to himself. "I have waited for thy salvation, O LORD."

Gad. Asher. Naphtali. The hind let loose. Each name spoken, each son standing where he stood.

Then Joseph.

Jacob's eyes found him at the side of the bed. Joseph knelt close. Jacob's voice softened and warmed. "Joseph is a fruitful bough, even a fruitful bough by a well; whose branches run over the wall." The picture lifted in the room — green leaves spilling over stone, fruit hanging heavy. "The archers have sorely grieved him, and shot at him, and hated him. But his bow abode in strength, and the arms of his hands were made strong by the hands of the mighty God of Jacob; from thence is the shepherd, the stone of Israel." Joseph's head lowered.

"The blessings of thy father have prevailed above the blessings of my progenitors unto the utmost bound of the everlasting hills. They shall be on the head of Joseph, and on the crown of the head of him that was separate from his brethren."

Last came Benjamin. The youngest. Jacob's voice gathered for one final image. "Benjamin shall ravin as a wolf: in the morning he shall devour the prey, and at night he shall divide the spoil."

The blessings ended. The lamp burned. The twelve sons stood about the bed in the small lamplit room. Jacob looked upon each of them in turn.

Then he gave them one more word. He told them where he was to be buried — in the cave of Machpelah, in the field that Abraham bought of Ephron the Hittite. Where Abraham and Sarah lay. Where Isaac and Rebekah lay. Where he had buried Leah.

When he had finished, he gathered up his feet into the bed. He yielded up the ghost. He was gathered unto his people.

The lamp burned on in the still room. The twelve sons stood about their father, and the words he had spoken stayed with them — son by son, blessing by blessing — long after the breath had gone from his lips and the small flame burned alone.
