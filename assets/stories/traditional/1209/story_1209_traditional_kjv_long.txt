When the days of Jacob drew nigh that he must die, he called for his sons.

The room was small and warm. A single oil lamp burned upon a low stand near the head of the bed, its flame steady, its light reaching no farther than the close walls. The walls were of pale stone, smoothed by the hands of those who had built the house long before. The bed was a low pallet laid against the floor, layered with woven coverings — wool dyed soft brown, linen worn pale with use, a heavier blanket drawn up unto the old man's breast. Jacob sat propped against a folded cloth. His white hair fell loose to his shoulders. His beard had thinned. His hands rested flat upon his thighs, the veins standing dark beneath the skin.

His twelve sons came in.

They came one by one through the low doorway. Grown men, weathered by sun and travel and years of tending flocks in open country. The eldest were gray at the temples and bearded heavy. The youngest still carried the leanness of a younger frame. They gathered about the bed. Some stood. Some knelt close upon the floor. The lamplight fell upon their faces and upon the woven coverings of the bed.

The room was silent.

Jacob lifted up his eyes.

"Gather yourselves together," he said, "that I may tell you that which shall befall you in the last days. Gather yourselves together, and hear, ye sons of Jacob; and hearken unto Israel your father."

Then he began.

He named them in the order of their birth. He spake each name aloud into the small room, and the room held still around each one.

Reuben.

The eldest. The firstborn. Jacob's voice steadied upon the name, and there was tenderness in it, and there was sorrow. "Reuben, thou art my firstborn, my might, and the beginning of my strength, the excellency of dignity, and the excellency of power. Unstable as water, thou shalt not excel." Reuben stood at the side of the bed. His face was lifted, his eyes wet. Then the blessing turned. The old man did not soften it. Reuben's chin lowered. He did not speak. The lamp burned on.

Simeon and Levi.

Jacob named them together. His hands tightened upon his thighs. His voice did not rise but it grew firmer. "Instruments of cruelty are in their habitations." He spake of anger fierce and wrath cruel. "O my soul, come not thou into their secret. I will divide them in Jacob, and scatter them in Israel." The two brothers stood close together at the foot of the bed. They said nothing. Their faces did not move.

Then Jacob said the name of Judah.

Judah was the fourth son. The blessing landed upon him like a hand laid upon the head. Jacob's voice rose, steadier now, carrying the cadence of a man who saw what he was saying as he said it. "Judah, thou art he whom thy brethren shall praise. Thy hand shall be in the neck of thine enemies. Thy father's children shall bow down before thee." Judah stood at the foot of the bed. His shoulders straightened. His eyes did not leave his father's face.

"Judah is a lion's whelp. From the prey, my son, thou art gone up. He stooped down, he couched as a lion, and as an old lion. Who shall rouse him up?"

The image filled the small room. The lion at rest after the kill. The prey already taken. The shoulders folded down low to the earth, the great body holding still, the eyes open and watching. The other sons heard it. None moved.

"The sceptre shall not depart from Judah," Jacob said, "nor a lawgiver from between his feet, until Shiloh come; and unto him shall the gathering of the people be."

Jacob's hand lifted slightly from his thigh. His fingers spread, then settled again.

"Binding his foal unto the vine, and his ass's colt unto the choice vine; he washed his garments in wine, and his clothes in the blood of grapes. His eyes shall be red with wine, and his teeth white with milk."

The lamp guttered once and steadied. Judah stood where he stood, unmoving, the prophecy resting upon him like a weight that had been waiting a long time to find its place.

Jacob spake on.

Zebulun. Brief. He should dwell at the haven of the sea, a haven for ships, and his border unto Zidon. Issachar. A strong ass couching down between two burdens. He had seen the land and found it pleasant, and he would bow his shoulder to bear. Each name a brief shape in the lamplight. Each blessing carried upon the same patient rhythm of the old man's breathing.

Then Dan.

"Dan shall judge his people, as one of the tribes of Israel." Jacob paused. He drew a slow breath, and when he spake again his voice came lower, with the curl of the image inside it. "Dan shall be a serpent by the way, an adder in the path, that biteth the horse heels, so that his rider shall fall backward."

The serpent moved in the room. The thin shape upon the trail, the bite, the horseman tipping back from the saddle.

Then Jacob paused longer. His eyes closed. His head turned slightly toward the lamp. When he spake again the words came almost to himself, quiet, half-prayer.

"I have waited for thy salvation, O LORD."

The sons heard it. They did not move.

Then Jacob opened his eyes and went on.

Gad. A troop should overcome him, but he should overcome at the last. Asher. His bread should be fat, and he should yield royal dainties. Naphtali. A hind let loose, that giveth goodly words. Each son standing where he stood. Each name spoken into the lamplit room.

Then Joseph.

Jacob's eyes found him at the side of the bed. Joseph knelt close, his head bowed, his hands resting upon the edge of the covering. The lamp lit one side of his face.

Jacob's voice softened and warmed.

"Joseph is a fruitful bough, even a fruitful bough by a well; whose branches run over the wall."

The picture lifted in the small room. Green leaves spilling over a stone boundary. Fruit hanging heavy on the running branches. Water at the root.

"The archers have sorely grieved him, and shot at him, and hated him. But his bow abode in strength, and the arms of his hands were made strong by the hands of the mighty God of Jacob; from thence is the shepherd, the stone of Israel. Even by the God of thy father, who shall help thee, and by the Almighty, who shall bless thee with blessings of heaven above, blessings of the deep that lieth under, blessings of the breasts, and of the womb."

Joseph's head bowed lower. His shoulders moved with his breathing.

"The blessings of thy father," Jacob said, "have prevailed above the blessings of my progenitors unto the utmost bound of the everlasting hills. They shall be on the head of Joseph, and on the crown of the head of him that was separate from his brethren."

The room was still. The lamp burned.

Last came Benjamin.

The youngest. Jacob's voice gathered itself for one final image. "Benjamin shall ravin as a wolf: in the morning he shall devour the prey, and at night he shall divide the spoil."

The blessings ended.

The twelve sons stood about the bed. The lamp burned at the head of the pallet, the flame holding steady, the oil pooling clear at the base of the wick. The old man looked upon each of his sons in turn. His eyes moved slowly across their faces.

Then he gave them one more word.

He told them where he was to be buried — in the cave of Machpelah, in the field that Abraham bought of Ephron the Hittite. Where Abraham and Sarah lay. Where Isaac and Rebekah lay. Where he had buried Leah. His voice was low now, almost spent. He named each name, slowly, the buried ones, the field, the cave, the land.

When he had made an end of charging his sons, he gathered up his feet into the bed.

He yielded up the ghost.

He was gathered unto his people.

The lamp burned on in the still room. The twelve sons stood about their father, and the words he had spoken stayed with them — son by son, blessing by blessing — long after the breath had gone from his lips and the small flame burned alone.
