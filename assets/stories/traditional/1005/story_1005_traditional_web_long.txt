The road into Jericho was packed with bodies. Merchants had abandoned their stalls. Women carried children on their hips, pressing forward through the crowd. Older men leaned on walking sticks, craning their necks above the shoulders of those in front of them. The noise was constant — sandals scraping against packed earth, voices calling out names, arguments over position along the roadside. Dust rose from the street in thin clouds, stirred by hundreds of shuffling feet, and it settled on tunics, on bare arms, on the leaves of the low shrubs that lined the path.

Jesus was coming through Jericho.

Word had moved faster than any runner could carry it. From the outskirts of the city to its center, people gathered and waited. Some climbed onto low walls. Others stood on the steps of buildings. A group of young men had positioned themselves at the edge of a rooftop, their legs dangling over the side, watching the road below.

In this city there lived a man named Zacchaeus. He was a chief tax collector, and he was rich. His house sat in one of the better quarters of Jericho, built with thick walls of pale stone, its courtyard shaded by a fig tree. The coins he collected passed through his hands daily — Roman denarii, temple shekels, copper lepta — and a portion stayed with him each time. He employed other tax collectors beneath him, and their collections added to his own wealth. The people of Jericho spoke his name with a particular edge. When he walked through the market, conversations dropped to murmurs. Vendors watched him pass without greeting.

Now Zacchaeus was trying to see who Jesus was. He moved along the street, weaving between clusters of people who stood shoulder to shoulder. But the crowd was thick, and Zacchaeus was short in stature. He stepped to one side, then the other, rising on his toes. Ahead of him, a wall of backs and heads blocked the road completely. He tried pressing between two men, but they shifted and closed the gap. He moved further along, searching for an opening, but the mass of people only grew denser as the road narrowed.

He stopped. Ahead, along the route where Jesus would pass, a sycamore-fig tree stood beside the road. Its trunk was wide and rough-barked, its lower branches thick enough to hold a man's weight. The branches spread outward in broad, uneven arms, heavy with dark green leaves. The tree was taller than the surrounding walls, and from its upper limbs, a person could see far down the road in both directions.

Zacchaeus ran ahead of the crowd. His robes gathered around his legs as he moved. He reached the base of the sycamore-fig and placed both hands against its bark. The surface was coarse under his palms, ridged and cracked with age. He pulled himself up, finding footholds in the gnarled trunk, his sandals scraping against the wood. One branch, then another. He climbed until he was well above the heads of the crowd, settling himself on a broad limb where the leaves partially screened him from below. From there, the road stretched out clearly. He could see the tops of heads, the colors of cloaks, the slow movement of the crowd parting as someone approached.

The noise shifted. Voices at the far end of the road grew louder, then quieted in a wave that moved toward him. People pressed back against the walls and against each other. Children were lifted higher. A group came into view — several men walking together, their garments dusty from the road, their faces tanned from days of travel. And among them, at the center, walked Jesus.

He moved at an unhurried pace. The crowd called out to him from both sides. Some reached toward him. Others simply stood and watched. His disciples walked close beside him, and the whole procession moved steadily along the packed-earth road toward the place where the sycamore-fig tree stood.

Jesus came to the tree and stopped.

He looked up.

The leaves rustled faintly in the warm air. Zacchaeus sat on the branch above, his hands gripping the bark, his short legs braced against the limb. The crowd around the base of the tree went quiet. Faces turned upward, following Jesus' gaze.

Jesus spoke directly to him. "Zacchaeus, hurry and come down, for today I must stay at your house."

The words carried clearly in the stillness. People standing nearby turned to one another. Murmurs rippled outward through the crowd. Zacchaeus moved immediately. He swung his legs from the branch and lowered himself down, his hands gripping the rough bark as he descended. His sandals hit the ground, and he straightened, brushing fragments of bark and leaf from his robes. He received Jesus joyfully.

Around them, the murmuring grew louder. Voices overlapped. People standing close enough to hear repeated the words to those behind them. The complaint passed through the crowd like a stone dropped in water, spreading in every direction. They said it openly, not troubling to lower their voices: "He has gone in to lodge with a man who is a sinner."

The words were audible — spoken by merchants, by neighbors, by men and women who had watched Zacchaeus collect taxes at the city gate for years. Some shook their heads. Others crossed their arms and stood rigid, watching as Zacchaeus turned toward the road that led to his home.

Zacchaeus stood still. He turned to face the Lord. The street around them was crowded, the air warm and heavy with dust. His voice rose above the murmur of the crowd, steady and clear.

"Behold, Lord, half of my goods I give to the poor," he said. "If I have wrongfully exacted anything of anyone, I restore four times as much."

The crowd pressed closer. Some leaned in to hear. Others stepped back, watching from a distance. Zacchaeus stood with his hands open at his sides, his short frame upright before Jesus in the middle of the road.

Jesus answered him. His voice was audible to those standing near, and the words passed outward through the gathered people.

"Today, salvation has come to this house, because he also is a son of Abraham."

The crowd shifted. Some stood motionless, listening. Others whispered to one another. The dust continued to drift in the warm air. A child tugged at a mother's sleeve. An old man leaned forward on his stick, his mouth slightly open.

Jesus spoke again, and these words, too, carried through the stillness of the street.

"For the Son of Man came to seek and to save that which was lost."

The sycamore-fig tree stood behind them, its broad branches casting a pattern of shade and light across the packed earth. The bark still bore the scuff marks where Zacchaeus had climbed. Fragments of leaf and bark lay scattered at the base of the trunk. The crowd remained gathered along the road — merchants, mothers, children, old men, tax collectors, laborers — all standing in the dust and the warm air of Jericho, watching as Jesus and Zacchaeus turned together toward the house with the pale stone walls and the shaded courtyard, and walked away from the tree, and moved down the road, and the crowd slowly parted to let them pass.
