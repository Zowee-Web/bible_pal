The road into Jericho stretched long and pale beneath the afternoon sun, and the dust upon it had been ground fine by the feet of many travelers. The stones along the edges were smooth and sun-warmed. Thin grass grew in patches where the road widened near the city gate, and the walls of Jericho rose beyond, their surfaces bright with the reflected heat of the day. A dry wind moved through the date palms that lined the approach, stirring their fronds with a low, papery sound.

Word had passed through the city that Jesus of Nazareth was coming along the road, and the people of Jericho had come out in great number to see him. Men and women pressed together along both sides of the way, standing shoulder to shoulder, three and four deep in places. Vendors had left their stalls in the marketplace with goods still laid upon the tables. Women carried young children on their hips. Boys climbed onto low stone walls and crouched there, gripping the rough edges with their hands. Old men leaned upon staffs near the roadside, their mantles drawn about their shoulders despite the warmth. The noise of voices rose and mingled — questions, shouts, the high calling of children — until the sound filled the street like the noise of a market at its busiest hour.

Among that throng moved a man of small stature, shorter by a head than most who stood around him. His name was Zacchaeus, and he was chief among the publicans of that region, a man of exceeding wealth. His garments were of fine cloth, and his sandals were well-made, but no one stepped aside for him. He pressed forward through the crowd, turning his shoulders to pass between two men who stood with arms folded, but the bodies before him stood close together and gave no room. He could see only the backs of heads, the raised arms of those who pointed down the road, the shifting press of shoulders and cloaks.

He moved to the left, stepping over a basket that a woman had set upon the ground, and tried to reach the edge of the road where the view might open. A taller man stepped into the gap before him. Zacchaeus rose upon his toes, stretching upward, but still the crowd blocked his sight entirely. Voices around him called out that Jesus was drawing near, and the press of bodies grew tighter still as people pushed forward.

So Zacchaeus turned and ran. He broke free of the crowd and moved quickly along the outer edge of the throng, his sandals striking the packed earth with short, rapid steps. He passed the line of onlookers — merchants in their working clothes, beggars seated against the base of a wall, women standing together in clusters with their heads covered. He ran ahead of them all, his breath quickening, the dust rising in small puffs behind his feet.

Ahead, set back from the road by a few paces, a sycamore tree grew broad and heavy. Its trunk was thick and deeply furrowed, its lower branches wide and spreading, and its leaves broad enough to cast a wide circle of shade upon the ground beneath. The tree stood where the road curved slightly, and from its upper branches the whole approach would be visible.

Zacchaeus reached the tree and took hold of the lowest branch with both hands. The bark was rough beneath his palms. He pulled himself upward, setting one foot against a knot in the trunk, and swung his leg over the first branch. He climbed higher, gripping the limbs one after another, his fine garments catching against the bark. A strip of dust marked the front of his robe where he pressed against the trunk. He settled himself upon a broad branch perhaps twice the height of a man above the road, his legs braced against the trunk on either side, his hands resting upon the limb before him. The leaves moved around him in the dry wind. From that place, the road lay open beneath him, stretching back toward the approaching crowd.

The noise grew. The crowd shifted and parted as Jesus came walking through their midst. He moved at a steady pace, unhurried, his garments plain, his sandals dusty from the road. Men and women on either side reached toward him, calling out. Some pressed close. Others stood back and watched. Dust rose from the footsteps of those who walked with him, catching the light where the sun struck it between the buildings. The shadow of the crowd moved along the ground like a single dark shape.

Jesus drew nearer to the sycamore tree. The crowd thinned slightly where the road curved, and his steps became visible from above — the fall of his sandals upon the pale earth, the hem of his garment brushing the dust. Then he stopped. He stood directly beneath the tree, and the shade of its branches fell across his shoulders.

He lifted his face upward, looking into the branches where Zacchaeus sat among the broad leaves.

He spoke directly to the man above him. He called him by name — Zacchaeus — and his voice carried clearly in the open air. He bade him come down with haste. He told him that he must abide at his house that day.

The crowd around the base of the tree grew still. Faces turned upward. People near the back pressed forward to see.

Zacchaeus moved at once. He gripped the branch beneath him and swung his legs free, lowering himself to the limb below. His hands scraped along the bark as he descended, one branch and then another, his sandals finding footholds against the furrowed trunk. A piece of bark broke loose and fell to the ground. His feet touched the earth, and he straightened, standing before Jesus in the open road. Dust clung to the front of his garments and to the palms of his hands. He received Jesus with visible gladness, his face turned fully toward him, his posture upright and open.

The murmuring began almost at once. Voices passed from mouth to mouth through the gathered crowd — sharp, distinct, carrying through the warm air. They said that Jesus had gone to be guest with a man who was a sinner. Men shook their heads. Women leaned toward one another and spoke behind their hands. The words moved backward through the throng like a ripple passing through standing water, and the sound of it was low and constant.

Then Zacchaeus stood before them all and spoke aloud. His voice was clear and carried past the nearest onlookers into the crowd beyond. He declared unto the Lord that the half of his goods he would give to the poor. He paused. The crowd quieted. He spoke again, saying that if he had taken anything from any man by false accusation, he would restore it fourfold.

The street was still. The wind moved through the sycamore leaves overhead, and the shadow of the tree shifted slightly upon the ground.

Jesus answered him there, standing in the road, in the hearing of the people gathered on every side. He declared that salvation had come to that house on this day, forasmuch as Zacchaeus also was a son of Abraham. He spoke plainly, his voice carrying without strain, and said that the Son of man was come to seek and to save that which was lost.

No one in the crowd answered. The murmuring had ceased. The road stretched out behind them toward the open country, pale and bright under the sun. The sycamore tree cast its broad shadow across the dust where they stood. Zacchaeus remained before Jesus in the full light of the afternoon, his hands at his sides, the dust still upon his garments. The crowd stood along the road in silence, and the dry wind passed once more through the fronds of the date palms beyond the gate.
