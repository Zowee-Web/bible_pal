The road into Jericho lay thick with dust, and the press of bodies along its edges stirred that dust into a low haze that clung to sandals and hems alike. Merchants had drawn their carts to the side. Women stood upon the raised thresholds of doorways, craning forward. Children climbed the low mud-brick walls that bordered the road, their bare feet balanced on the uneven edges. Word had passed through the city that Jesus of Nazareth drew near, and the crowds had gathered accordingly — shoulder pressed against shoulder, voices overlapping, hands shading eyes against the afternoon glare.

Among them moved a man called Zacchaeus, chief among the publicans of that region, and wealthy. He pressed between the bodies of taller men, turning sideways to push through gaps that closed almost as quickly as they opened. His stature was small, and the wall of backs and shoulders before him yielded nothing. He could hear the sound of the approaching company — the shuffle of many feet, the murmur growing louder — but he could see nothing of the one who came.

He ran ahead. His robes gathered in his hands, he moved quickly past the edge of the crowd, his sandals slapping against the packed earth. A sycamore tree stood along the road ahead, its broad trunk leaning slightly, its thick branches spreading low and wide. Zacchaeus set his hands upon the bark, pulled himself upward, and climbed into the limbs. He settled among the branches, his legs braced against the wood, and from that height the road below opened clearly to his sight.

The procession came. Jesus walked at the center of a loose company of men, and the crowd parted and reformed around them like water around stones in a stream. Dust rose from beneath their feet. Voices called out from the edges — names, questions, pleas. Some reached forward to touch the hem of his garment. Others simply stood and watched him pass.

Then Jesus came beneath the sycamore tree and stopped. He lifted his face upward, and his gaze found the man sitting among the branches. The crowd nearby slowed, and those closest turned to see where the teacher looked.

He called him by name. Zacchaeus, he said, and bade him come down with haste, for he must abide at his house that day.

The branches rustled. Zacchaeus descended quickly, his hands gripping the bark, his feet finding the lower limbs until he dropped to the ground. He stood before Jesus, and he received him with visible gladness — his posture open, his step quick as he turned to lead the way.

Around them the murmuring began at once. Voices carried sharp and low through the gathered people. They spoke one to another, and their words were plain: he had gone to be guest with a man that was a sinner. The word passed backward through the crowd like a stone cast into still water — publican, sinner, his house. Faces turned. Hands gestured. Some shook their heads. The displeasure was audible, spoken openly and without restraint.

But Zacchaeus stood before the Lord, and he spoke. His voice carried above the murmur. He declared that the half of his goods he would give to the poor. He paused. The crowd nearby grew quieter, and those who had been speaking among themselves turned to listen. Then he spoke again: if he had taken anything from any man by false accusation, he would restore it fourfold.

The words hung in the open air. No one answered him. The dust settled slowly around their feet.

Then Jesus spoke, and those standing near drew closer to hear. He declared that salvation had come to that house on that day. He named Zacchaeus a son of Abraham. The crowd stood still. Some leaned forward. Others shifted where they stood but did not move away.

Jesus continued. He spoke of the Son of man — that he had come to seek and to save that which was lost. His voice carried clearly in the street, past the sycamore tree, past the edges of the crowd that had gathered to watch and had remained to listen.

The road stretched onward through Jericho. The dust still hung faintly in the air. The merchants remained beside their carts, and the children still perched upon the walls. Zacchaeus stood near the doorway of his own house, and Jesus entered in. The crowd lingered in the street outside, their voices lower now, their faces turned toward the open door through which the teacher had passed. The afternoon light fell across the threshold, and the sounds of the city — the carts, the animals, the distant calls of vendors — continued around them, unchanged, while within that house the guest sat down with the publican, and the door remained open to the street.
