Moses had been on the mountain forty days.

The cloud sat on the summit. The fire moved in the cloud. The people had watched the mountain from below for as long as they could stand to watch it, and then they had stopped watching. They had eaten the manna. They had drawn the water. The days had grown long. They had forgotten how long forty days felt, even though they had walked enough roads to know.

It had been weeks since they had heard his voice. It had been months since they had stood at the foot of the mountain and heard the words of the law roll down across the camp. Now the camp moved as it had always moved — the smoke of the cooking fires in the mornings, the sound of children in the lanes, the work of the tents, the gathering at the wells. But something in the rhythm had begun to fray.

Aaron had been left in charge. He had not been given clear instructions. He had simply been told that Moses would be on the mountain a while. He had grown more uncertain as the days passed. He was not Moses. He had not been the one who stood at the bush. He had been the one who carried the words. He had been good at carrying the words. He was less good at choosing them.

The people came together to Aaron at last, and the request was put to him by enough of them at once that he could not easily refuse it.

Come, they said, make us gods, which shall go before us. As for this Moses, the man who brought us up out of the land of Egypt, we don't know what has become of him.

It was not a small thing they asked. Aaron knew it was not a small thing. He had heard the law. He had seen the fire on the mountain. He had watched Moses stand at the door of the tent of meeting and speak with the One whose name he would not say. He had been there for all of it. He knew what he was being asked to make.

But the camp was large, and the pressure was real, and he was alone. And he had been waiting for forty days without knowing if his brother would come back.

He said: take off the golden rings which are in the ears of your wives, of your sons, and of your daughters, and bring them to me.

Perhaps he hoped, when he asked it, that they would refuse. That the women would not give up the gold. That the children would clutch at their ears. That the request would prove too much, and the people would think better of it, and the moment would pass.

They did not refuse. They brought the rings. They brought them quickly. They brought them in piles.

He received what they handed him, and fashioned it with an engraving tool, and made it a molten calf.

They said: these are your gods, Israel, which brought you up out of the land of Egypt.

The word in that sentence — gods — was the same word the Egyptians had used for theirs, on the river, in the temples that Moses had walked past as a child of Pharaoh's court. The people who had walked out of Egypt had walked out with the language of Egypt still in their mouths. The calf was familiar. The Apis bull was familiar. It was easier to worship what they could see.

Aaron built an altar before it.

He made proclamation, and said: tomorrow shall be a feast to Yahweh.

He tried to keep the name. He tried to fold what they had done into something familiar. As though if he just said the right word over the calf, the calf would be all right. As though the name could be put on anything. He was not the first leader to try this, and he would not be the last.

The people rose up early the next day, and offered burnt offerings, and brought peace offerings. They sat down to eat and to drink, and they rose up to play.

The noise of it carried.

Yahweh spoke to Moses on the mountain.

Go down, he said, for your people, whom you brought up out of the land of Egypt, have corrupted themselves.

The phrase carried weight. They were not, in that sentence, his people anymore. They were Moses' people. The pronoun had shifted. Moses did not miss it.

They have turned aside quickly out of the way which I commanded them. They have made themselves a molten calf and have worshiped it. They have sacrificed to it. They have said: these are your gods, Israel, which brought you up out of the land of Egypt.

I have seen these people. They are a stiff-necked people. Now therefore leave me alone, that my wrath may burn hot against them, and that I may consume them. And I will make of you a great nation.

It was an offer. It was the same offer that had been made to Noah, and to Abraham. The promise had been carried in a single family before. It could be carried in a single family again. Yahweh was telling Moses he could begin over with him. He could be the patriarch of a new line. The covenant would be honored. The promise would go on. Only this generation — this stubborn, stiff-necked, calf-making generation — would be erased.

Moses did not leave him alone.

He stood on the mountain, with the fire in the cloud, and he begged Yahweh his God.

Why does your wrath burn hot against your people — and now the pronoun was Moses', the pronoun was your, the pronoun was given back to its proper owner — that you have brought out of the land of Egypt with great power and with a mighty hand?

Why should the Egyptians speak, saying, he brought them out for evil, to kill them in the mountains, and to consume them from the surface of the earth?

Turn from your fierce wrath. Turn away from this evil against your people.

Remember Abraham, Isaac, and Israel, your servants, to whom you swore by your own self, and said, I will multiply your offspring as the stars of the sky, and all this land that I have spoken of I will give to your offspring, and they shall inherit it forever.

He did not ask to be made a great nation. He did not accept the offer. He did not even mention the offer. He stood between God and the people he was responsible for, and reminded God of who they were — your people, the ones you swore to, the ones the world is watching, the ones the patriarchs prayed for.

He would say, a few verses on, that he was willing to be blotted out of the book that had been written if Yahweh would not forgive their sin. He had not yet said that. He was on his way to it.

He did not yet know what would be done with the offer. He did not yet know what the people had done in detail. He had not yet come down. He had not yet seen the calf, or heard the noise of the camp, or had to break the tablets at the foot of the mountain. He stood there and pleaded.

And Yahweh repented of the evil which he had said he would do to his people.

The mountain held its quiet. The cloud sat on it. The fire moved in the cloud. The pleading had been heard. The judgment had been turned. The long mercy had been turned along with it. There would be consequences. There would be a long road still ahead. The covenant had not been canceled — but neither had it been re-cut into the shape Moses had asked for. It had been kept whole.

And Moses began to come down — the tablets in his hands, the people in his throat, the long descent ahead of him before he would see what waited at the bottom.

And so it was on the day Moses stood between God and Israel, on Mount Sinai, in the last hour of the forty days.
