Moses had been upon the mountain forty days.

The cloud sat upon the summit. The fire moved in the cloud. The people had watched the mountain from below as long as they could endure to watch it, and then they had ceased to watch. They had eaten the manna. They had drawn the water. The days had grown long. They had forgotten how long forty days felt, though they had walked enough roads to know.

It had been weeks since they had heard his voice. It had been months since they had stood at the foot of the mountain and heard the words of the law roll down across the camp. Now the camp moved as it had always moved — the smoke of the cooking fires in the mornings, the sound of children in the lanes, the work of the tents, the gathering at the wells. But something in the rhythm had begun to fray.

Aaron had been left in charge. He had not been given clear instructions. He had simply been told that Moses should be on the mountain a while. He had grown more uncertain as the days passed. He was not Moses. He had not been the one who stood at the bush. He had been the one who bore the words. He had been good at bearing the words. He was less good at choosing them.

The people gathered themselves together unto Aaron at last, and the request was put unto him by enough of them at once that he could not easily refuse it.

Up, they said, make us gods, which shall go before us; for as for this Moses, the man that brought us up out of the land of Egypt, we wot not what is become of him.

It was no small thing they asked. Aaron knew it was no small thing. He had heard the law. He had seen the fire upon the mountain. He had watched Moses stand at the door of the tent of meeting and speak with the One whose name he would not say. He had been there for all of it. He knew what he was being asked to make.

But the camp was large, and the pressure was real, and he was alone. And he had been waiting for forty days without knowing if his brother should come back.

He said: break off the golden earrings, which are in the ears of your wives, of your sons, and of your daughters, and bring them unto me.

Perhaps he hoped, when he asked it, that they should refuse. That the women should not give up the gold. That the children should clutch at their ears. That the request should prove too much, and the people should think better of it, and the moment should pass.

They refused not. They brake them off and brought them. They brought them quickly. They brought them in piles.

He received them at their hand, and fashioned it with a graving tool, after he had made it a molten calf.

And they said: these be thy gods, O Israel, which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt.

The word in that sentence — gods — was the same word the Egyptians had used for theirs, upon the river, in the temples that Moses had walked past as a child of Pharaoh's court. The people who had walked out of Egypt had walked out with the language of Egypt yet in their mouths. The calf was familiar. The Apis bull was familiar. It was easier to worship what they could see.

Aaron built an altar before it.

He made proclamation, and said: tomorrow is a feast to the Lord.

He sought to keep the name. He sought to fold what they had done into something familiar. As though if he but said the right word over the calf, the calf should be all right. As though the name could be put on anything. He was not the first leader to try this, and he should not be the last.

The people rose up early on the morrow, and offered burnt offerings, and brought peace offerings. They sat down to eat and to drink, and rose up to play.

The noise of it carried.

And the Lord said unto Moses upon the mountain:

Go, get thee down; for thy people, which thou broughtest out of the land of Egypt, have corrupted themselves.

The phrase carried weight. They were not, in that sentence, his people anymore. They were Moses' people. The pronoun had shifted. Moses missed it not.

They have turned aside quickly out of the way which I commanded them: they have made them a molten calf, and have worshipped it, and have sacrificed thereunto, and said, these be thy gods, O Israel, which have brought thee up out of the land of Egypt.

I have seen this people, and, behold, it is a stiffnecked people. Now therefore let me alone, that my wrath may wax hot against them, and that I may consume them: and I will make of thee a great nation.

It was an offer. It was the same offer that had been made unto Noah, and unto Abraham. The promise had been borne in a single family before. It could be borne in a single family again. The Lord was telling Moses he could begin over with him. He could be the patriarch of a new line. The covenant should be honoured. The promise should go on. Only this generation — this stubborn, stiffnecked, calf-making generation — should be erased.

Moses let him not alone.

He stood upon the mountain, with the fire in the cloud, and he besought the Lord his God.

Lord, why doth thy wrath wax hot against thy people — and now the pronoun was Moses', the pronoun was thy, the pronoun was given back to its proper owner — which thou hast brought forth out of the land of Egypt with great power, and with a mighty hand?

Wherefore should the Egyptians speak, and say, for mischief did he bring them out, to slay them in the mountains, and to consume them from the face of the earth?

Turn from thy fierce wrath. Repent of this evil against thy people.

Remember Abraham, Isaac, and Israel, thy servants, to whom thou swarest by thine own self, and saidst unto them, I will multiply your seed as the stars of heaven, and all this land that I have spoken of will I give unto your seed, and they shall inherit it for ever.

He asked not to be made a great nation. He accepted not the offer. He named not the offer at all. He stood between God and the people he was set over, and reminded God of who they were — thy people, the ones thou hast sworn unto, the ones the world watcheth, the ones the patriarchs prayed for.

He should say, a few verses on, that he was willing to be blotted out of the book that had been written if the Lord should not forgive their sin. He had not yet said it. He was on his way to it.

He knew not yet what should be done with the offer. He knew not yet what the people had done in detail. He had not yet come down. He had not yet seen the calf, or heard the noise of the camp, or had to break the tables at the foot of the mountain. He stood there and besought.

And the Lord repented of the evil which he thought to do unto his people.

The mountain held its quiet. The cloud sat upon it. The fire moved in the cloud. The pleading had been heard. The judgment had been turned. The long mercy had been turned along with it. There should be consequences. There should be a long road yet ahead. The covenant had not been cancelled — but neither had it been re-cut into the shape Moses had asked for. It had been kept whole.

And Moses began to come down — the tables in his hands, the people in his throat, the long descent ahead of him before he should see what waited at the bottom.

And so it was on the day Moses stood between God and Israel, on Mount Sinai, in the last hour of the forty days.
