In the days when the judges judged, there was a famine in the land. The barley did not stand in the fields outside Bethlehem. The wells of the city dropped low. Men measured what was left at the bottom of their grain jars and measured again. Children grew thinner. Wives mended the same cloak for the third winter.

A certain man named Elimelech, an Ephrathite of Bethlehem Judah, gathered his household and turned his face east. He took his wife Naomi. He took his two sons, Mahlon and Chilion. They left the city gates with what could be carried. They crossed the Jordan in the dry place where the cattle waded. They climbed the road up into the country of Moab. There the rains had not failed, and there they came to live.

They lived there. They built a fire under another sky. The bread on their table was Moabite bread. The neighbors spoke a tongue not their own.

Elimelech died in that foreign land. Naomi was left with her two sons. Mahlon and Chilion grew into men, and they took for themselves wives of the women of Moab. The name of the one was Orpah. The name of the other was Ruth. They lived there about ten years. Then Mahlon died. Then Chilion died. The woman was bereaved of her two children and of her husband. Three graves stood on a Moabite hillside, and Naomi sat in a house that had once held a household.

Word came at last from across the river. A trader at the marketplace, a man passing through, spoke of Bethlehem. The Lord had visited his people. He had given them bread again. The fields were rising. The harvest was near.

Naomi arose. Her two daughters-in-law arose with her. The three women went out of the place where they had been, and they went on the way to return to the land of Judah.

The road climbed out of the valley. The Moabite hills fell behind them. The wind off the highlands pulled at their cloaks. Naomi walked ahead. Her hands were empty at her sides. Then, at a turning of the road, she stopped. She turned to face the two younger women.

"Go, return each of you to her mother's house. May the Lord deal kindly with you, as you have dealt with the dead and with me. May the Lord grant you that you may find rest, each of you in the house of her husband."

She kissed them on the forehead, first one and then the other. They lifted up their voices and wept. The sound carried back along the empty road.

"No, but we will return with you to your people."

Naomi shook her head. The hood fell back from her hair. "Go back, my daughters. Why would you go with me? Do I still have sons in my womb, that they may be your husbands? Go back, my daughters. Go your way. I am too old to have a husband. If I should say tonight, 'I have hope,' and even bore sons, would you wait until they were grown? Would you refrain from having husbands? No, my daughters. It grieves me seriously for your sakes, for the Lord's hand has gone out against me."

The wind moved through the brush at the road's edge. The women lifted up their voices and wept again.

Then Orpah leaned close. She kissed her mother-in-law on the cheek. She held the older woman a long moment. Then she stepped back. She turned. She began to walk the way they had come. Her form grew smaller against the brown hills. She did not look behind her.

Naomi watched her go. Then she turned to the other one.

Ruth had not moved. Her hand was on Naomi's sleeve. The cloth of the older woman's garment was bunched between her fingers. She would not let go.

"Behold, your sister-in-law has gone back to her people and to her god. Follow your sister-in-law."

Ruth's grip tightened. Her body did not move forward toward Bethlehem and did not move back toward Moab. She stood between, with one hand fastened to the woman she would not leave. Her voice when it came was low and very steady.

"Do not urge me to leave you, and to return from following you. For where you go, I will go. Where you stay, I will stay. Your people shall be my people, and your God my God. Where you die, I will die, and there I will be buried. May the Lord do so to me, and more also, if anything but death parts you and me."

The words settled in the air between them. Naomi looked at her a long time. She looked at the foreign daughter who had buried a son of hers in Moabite ground and would not turn back to the house of her mother. She saw that Ruth was determined to go with her. She stopped urging her. She turned her face again toward Judah.

Ruth turned with her. The two women walked on together.

The road dropped down out of the hills and crossed the Jordan in the low place. The fields on the far side were green. The sun was westering by the time the road climbed up again toward Bethlehem. The walls of the city showed pale against the slope.

Word ran ahead of them through the gate. Word travels faster than two tired women on a road. The neighbors of the old days came out from their doorways. Men paused with their water jars at the well. Women set down what they were carrying. They looked at the bowed figure walking up the path with a younger woman beside her, a Moabite by her head covering and the cut of her cloak, and they began to speak to one another, and the speaking grew, and at last the whole city was stirred.

"Is this Naomi?"

The name reached her. She lifted her head. The face the neighbors saw was not the face that had left Bethlehem in the famine years. The years had worked on her. The losses had worked on her. Her eyes had a weight in them that had not been there before.

She did not soften the answer. She did not put her grief away for the comfort of the gathering. She stood on the road that had carried her out and brought her back, and she spoke plainly into the watching faces.

"Do not call me Naomi. Call me Mara, for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me. I went out full, and the Lord has brought me home again empty. Why do you call me Naomi, since the Lord has testified against me, and the Almighty has afflicted me?"

The neighbors fell quiet around her. No one corrected her. No one answered. The two women crossed into the city in the slanting afternoon light. Ruth walked a half step behind, the road dust still on her hem.

So Naomi returned, and Ruth the Moabitess, her daughter-in-law, with her, who returned out of the country of Moab. They came to Bethlehem in the beginning of barley harvest. Beyond the walls, the fields lay golden in the long evening, and the reapers were sharpening their sickles for the morning.
