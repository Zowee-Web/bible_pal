She rises while it is yet night.

The fire in the hearth has burned down to coals. She kneels on the packed earth of the kitchen and feeds the coals from the wood she set aside the evening before, and the small flame catches, and the room begins to warm. The children are still asleep in the inner room. Her husband turns under the blanket but does not wake. The lamp she lights from the new fire will not go out again until the work of the day is done.

By the time the first gray light comes over the wall of the courtyard, she has given food to her household. She has given portions to her servant girls. The bread is on the table. The children come out one by one, rubbing their eyes, and sit down to eat. She sees to it that each one has what they need before she sits down herself.

The day's labor begins.

She works wool and flax with her own hands. She seeks out what she needs and works eagerly with what she has. She is like the merchant ships — she brings her bread from afar. She considers a field, and weighs the cost, and buys it. With the fruit of her hands she plants a vineyard. She arms her waist with strength. She makes her arms strong. She perceives that her merchandise is good. Her lamp does not go out by night.

She lays her hands to the distaff. Her hands hold the spindle. The thread grows long across the morning.

When a beggar comes to the door, she does not turn him away. She stretches out her hand to the poor. She reaches out her hands to the needy. She does not weigh whether they deserve what she gives. She gives. She is not afraid of the snow for her household, because she has clothed them all in scarlet against the cold to come. She makes coverings of tapestry for the beds. Her own clothing is fine linen and purple.

Her husband is respected in the gates, where he sits among the elders of the land. They know him because of who comes home to him at the end of the day, and what waits for him when he gets there. She makes linen garments and sells them in the market. She delivers sashes to the merchant. The household lives by her hands.

Strength and dignity are her clothing. She laughs at the time to come.

She opens her mouth with wisdom, and the teaching of kindness is on her tongue. She does not raise her voice with the children. She does not scold the servant girls before others. She looks well to the ways of her household. She does not eat the bread of idleness.

The lamp burns late.

When the sons of the house grow up, they remember her at night more than they remember her in the day. They remember the lamp burning when they came in late from the field. They remember the bread waiting on the table when there was no reason to expect it. They rise up and call her blessed. Her husband, when he speaks of her in the gates, praises her. "Many women have done worthily," he says, "but you excel them all."

Charm is deceitful, the writing says. Beauty is vain. But a woman who fears the Lord — she shall be praised.

Give her of the fruit of her hands. Let her own works praise her in the gates.

And so it was set down in the writing of the wise, in the days when the king's mother spoke to her son, that he might know, when he saw such a woman in his life, what he was looking at.
