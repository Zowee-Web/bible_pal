She rises while it is yet night.

The house is quiet. The fire in the hearth has burned down to coals, and the air in the kitchen is cool. She kneels on the packed earth of the floor and feeds the coals from the wood she set aside the evening before, and the small flame catches. She holds her hands over it for a moment, watching it find its way into the dry sticks. The room begins to warm.

The children are still asleep in the inner room. Her husband turns under the blanket but does not wake. She does not wake him. There is no need. The work of the morning is hers.

The lamp she lights from the new fire will not go out again until the work of the day is done. The oil is enough. She has seen to that.

She moves through the house with the quiet of someone who knows every place a foot might catch on a loose stone. She brings down the meal. She kneads the dough. She sets the loaves where the heat of the hearth will rise to them. The bread will be ready when the children come out.

By the time the first gray light comes over the wall of the courtyard, she has given food to her household. She has given portions to the servant girls who sleep in the side room. The bread is on the table. The children come out one by one, rubbing their eyes, and sit down to eat. She sees to it that each one has what they need before she sits down herself.

The day's labor begins.

She works wool and flax with her own hands. She seeks out what she needs and works eagerly with what she has. She is like the merchant ships — she brings her bread from afar. The cloth she trades for in the market town two valleys over will be worth twice what she gave for it by the time the wool of her own sheep is spun and woven. She has thought all of this through.

She considers a field. She weighs the soil between her fingers. She walks the boundaries. She buys it. With the fruit of her hands she plants a vineyard along the southern slope. She arms her waist with strength. She makes her arms strong. She perceives that her merchandise is good. Her lamp does not go out by night.

She lays her hands to the distaff. Her hands hold the spindle. The thread grows long across the morning. She does not stop because no one is watching.

When a beggar comes to the door at midday, she does not turn him away. She stretches out her hand to the poor. She reaches out her hands to the needy. She does not weigh whether they deserve what she gives. She gives. She has decided that long ago, and the decision does not need to be made again at the door.

She is not afraid of the snow for her household, because she has clothed them all in scarlet against the cold to come. The wool has been carded, dyed, spun, and woven. The garments hang in the chest where they will be needed. She makes coverings of tapestry for the beds. Her own clothing is fine linen and purple, but she does not think much about it.

Her husband is respected in the gates, where he sits among the elders of the land. They know him in part because of who comes home to him at the end of the day, and what waits for him when he gets there. She makes linen garments and sells them in the market. She delivers sashes to the merchant. The household lives by her hands, and no one outside the household sees how much of the holding is hers.

Strength and dignity are her clothing. She laughs at the time to come. She is not afraid of what is ahead, because she has prepared for it as much as can be prepared for.

She opens her mouth with wisdom, and the teaching of kindness is on her tongue. She does not raise her voice with the children. She does not scold the servant girls in front of others. When a thing must be said hard, she says it hard in private. When a thing can be said softly, she says it softly. The household runs on this.

She looks well to the ways of her household. She does not eat the bread of idleness. The fire does not go out. The lamp burns late.

When the sons of the house grow up, they remember her at night more than they remember her in the day. They remember the lamp burning when they came in late from the field. They remember the bread waiting on the table when there was no reason to expect it. They remember the sound of her at work in the next room before anyone else was awake. They rise up and call her blessed.

Her husband, when he speaks of her in the gates, praises her. "Many women have done worthily," he says, "but you excel them all."

Charm is deceitful, the writing says. Beauty is vain. But a woman who fears the Lord — she shall be praised.

Give her of the fruit of her hands. Let her own works praise her in the gates.

And so it was set down in the writing of the wise, in the days when the king's mother spoke to her son, that he might know, when he saw such a woman in his life, what he was looking at.
