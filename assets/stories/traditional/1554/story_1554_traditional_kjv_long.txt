She riseth while it is yet night.

The house is quiet. The fire in the hearth hath burned down to coals, and the air in the kitchen is cool. She kneeleth upon the packed earth of the floor and feedeth the coals from the wood she had laid aside the evening before, and the small flame catcheth. She holdeth her hands over it for a moment, watching it find its way into the dry sticks. The room beginneth to warm.

The children are yet asleep in the inner room. Her husband turneth under the blanket but waketh not. She waketh him not. There is no cause. The work of the morning is hers.

The candle she lighteth from the new fire shall not go out again until the work of the day is done. The oil is enough. She hath seen to that.

She moveth through the house with the quiet of one who knoweth every place where a foot might catch upon a loose stone. She bringeth down the meal. She kneadeth the dough. She setteth the loaves where the heat of the hearth shall rise to them. The bread shall be ready when the children come out.

By the time the first gray light cometh over the wall of the courtyard, she hath given meat to her household. She hath given a portion to the maidens that sleep in the side room. The bread is upon the table. The children come out one by one, rubbing their eyes, and sit down to eat. She seeth to it that each one hath what they need before she sitteth down herself.

The day's labour beginneth.

She seeketh wool, and flax, and worketh willingly with her hands. She is like the merchants' ships — she bringeth her food from afar. The cloth she trades for in the market town two valleys over shall be worth twice what she gave for it by the time the wool of her own sheep is spun and woven. She hath thought all of this through.

She considereth a field. She weigheth the soil between her fingers. She walketh the boundaries. She buyeth it. With the fruit of her hands she planteth a vineyard along the southern slope. She girdeth her loins with strength. She strengtheneth her arms. She perceiveth that her merchandise is good. Her candle goeth not out by night.

She layeth her hands to the spindle. Her hands hold the distaff. The thread groweth long across the morning. She stoppeth not because no one is watching.

When a beggar cometh to the door at midday, she turneth him not away. She stretcheth out her hand to the poor. She reacheth forth her hands to the needy. She weigheth not whether they have deserved what she giveth. She giveth. She decided that long ago, and the decision must not be made again at the door.

She is not afraid of the snow for her household, for she hath clothed them all with scarlet against the cold to come. The wool hath been carded, dyed, spun, and woven. The garments hang in the chest where they shall be needed. She maketh herself coverings of tapestry for the beds. Her own clothing is silk and purple, but she thinketh not much upon it.

Her husband is known in the gates, when he sitteth among the elders of the land. They know him in part because of who cometh home to him at the end of the day, and what waiteth for him when he getteth there. She maketh fine linen and selleth it in the market. She delivereth girdles unto the merchant. The household liveth by her hands, and no man without seeth how much of the holding is hers.

Strength and honour are her clothing. She shall rejoice in time to come. She is not afraid of what is ahead, for she hath prepared for it as much as can be prepared for.

She openeth her mouth with wisdom, and in her tongue is the law of kindness. She raiseth not her voice with the children. She rebuketh not the maidens before others. When a thing must be spoken hard, she speaketh it hard in private. When a thing may be spoken softly, she speaketh it softly. The household runneth upon this.

She looketh well to the ways of her household. She eateth not the bread of idleness. The fire goeth not out. The candle burneth late.

When the sons of the house grow up, they remember her at night more than they remember her in the day. They remember the candle burning when they came in late from the field. They remember the bread waiting upon the table when there was no cause to expect it. They remember the sound of her at work in the next room before any other was awake. They arise up and call her blessed.

Her husband, when he speaketh of her in the gates, praiseth her. "Many daughters have done virtuously," he saith, "but thou excellest them all."

Favour is deceitful, the writing saith. Beauty is vain. But a woman that feareth the Lord — she shall be praised.

Give her of the fruit of her hands. Let her own works praise her in the gates.

And so it was set down in the writing of the wise, in the days when the king's mother spake unto her son, that he might know, when he saw such a woman in his life, what he was looking upon.
