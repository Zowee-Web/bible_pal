Moses was a hundred and twenty years old. His eye was not dim, nor his natural force abated, but the day of his death was nigh enough that he had ceased to write songs and was now speaking in the plain prose of a man who knew the end. He had climbed Mount Nebo only once already, in his thoughts. He would climb it once more in his body before this week was out. The people stood gathered before him on the plains of Moab, on the near side of the Jordan, in sight of the hills of the land they had been told they should enter and that he had been told he should not.

He had spoken many days. He had recounted the way the Lord had brought them out of Egypt — the plagues, the sea, the song of Miriam beside the bones of the chariots. He had recounted the giving of the law at Horeb, the trembling mountain and the fire upon its top. He had recounted the wandering in the wilderness, the manna, the water from the rock, the forty years that had buried a generation and grown a new one. He had warned them of the gods of the nations whose land they were about to take — the high places, the graven images, the names that would come whispering through the doorways of their houses. He had named the curses that should come if they turned away. He had named the blessings that should come if they obeyed.

The people had listened. The elders had nodded slowly, remembering. The children stared up at him with the open faces of those who had not been there for any of it, and who should be there for everything that came after.

And now, on the last of those days, he came to the part that mattered most to him. The part he had circled around for forty chapters and was finally ready to say plain.

"This commandment which I command thee this day is not hidden from thee, neither is it far off. It is not in heaven, that thou shouldest say, Who shall go up for us to heaven, and bring it unto us, that we may hear it, and do it? Neither is it beyond the sea, that thou shouldest say, Who shall go over the sea for us, and bring it unto us? But the word is very nigh unto thee, in thy mouth, and in thy heart, that thou mayest do it.

"See, I have set before thee this day life and good, and death and evil. In that I command thee this day to love the Lord thy God, to walk in his ways, and to keep his commandments, then thou shalt live and multiply, and the Lord thy God shall bless thee in the land whither thou goest to possess it. But if thine heart turn away, and thou wilt not hear, but shalt be drawn away and worship other gods, ye shall surely perish, and ye shall not prolong your days.

"I call heaven and earth to record this day against you, that I have set before you life and death, blessing and cursing. Therefore choose life, that both thou and thy seed may live. Love the Lord thy God, obey his voice, and cleave unto him. For he is thy life, and the length of thy days, that thou mayest dwell in the land which the Lord sware unto thy fathers, to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob, to give them."

And the old man finished what he had to say. He looked out at them and they looked back at him. Joshua stood somewhere in the assembly, the man who should lead them across the Jordan in the morning, his arms folded, his countenance composed. The elders looked at one another. No one spake. There was nothing more to add.

The plains stretched out around them. The Jordan ran behind him. The hills of the land rose in front of them in the late afternoon light. He had said it as plainly as he knew how. The choice was theirs now to make. And so it was on the day Moses set life and death before the people of Israel, in the last year of his pilgrimage, on the plains of Moab.
