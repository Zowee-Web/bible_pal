Moses was a hundred and twenty years old. His eye was not dim, nor his natural force abated, but the day of his death was nigh enough that he had ceased to write songs and was now speaking in the plain prose of a man who knew the end. He had climbed Mount Nebo only once already, in his thoughts. He would climb it once more in his body before this week was out, and from its height he should see the land laid out below him like a promise that was now for younger feet. He knew this. He had been told this. He had accepted this. But before he climbed, he had one last thing to do.

The people had gathered. They had come tribe by tribe, the elders at the front, the families gathered behind, the children moving among the legs of the grown men and being hushed by mothers who would hear what the old man had to say. They stood upon the plains of Moab, on the near side of the Jordan. The river ran behind the speaker. The hills of the land they were about to enter rose in front of them, blue and quiet in the morning haze. From where they stood they could see Jericho, far off, and the green line of the river marking the edge of forty years of waiting.

Moses had been speaking many days. He had stood under the awning his people had made for him, and he had recounted the way the Lord had brought them out of Egypt — the plagues that had broken Pharaoh, the sea that had opened and closed, the song of Miriam beside the bones of the chariots. He had recounted the giving of the law at Horeb, the trembling mountain and the fire upon its top. He had recounted the wandering in the wilderness, the manna, the water from the rock, the forty years that had buried a generation and grown a new one. He had warned them of the gods of the nations whose land they were about to take — the high places, the graven images, the names that would come whispering through the doorways of their houses once they had settled and built and forgotten the hunger of the wilderness. He had named the curses that should come if they turned away. He had named the blessings that should come if they obeyed.

The people had listened. The elders had nodded slowly, remembering. Some of the older women had wept silently into the corners of their veils for husbands buried in the wilderness, for parents they had left behind in Egypt, for the long road that was ending here. The children stared up at him with the open faces of those who had not been there for any of it, and who should be there for everything that came after. They did not yet know what they were inheriting. He knew this too.

And now, on the last of those days, Moses came to the part that mattered most to him. The part he had circled around for forty chapters and was finally ready to say plain. He lifted up his hand for silence. The silence came.

"This commandment which I command thee this day is not hidden from thee, neither is it far off. It is not in heaven, that thou shouldest say, Who shall go up for us to heaven, and bring it unto us, that we may hear it, and do it? Neither is it beyond the sea, that thou shouldest say, Who shall go over the sea for us, and bring it unto us, that we may hear it, and do it? But the word is very nigh unto thee, in thy mouth, and in thy heart, that thou mayest do it.

"See, I have set before thee this day life and good, and death and evil. In that I command thee this day to love the Lord thy God, to walk in his ways, and to keep his commandments and his statutes and his judgments, then thou shalt live and multiply, and the Lord thy God shall bless thee in the land whither thou goest to possess it. But if thine heart turn away, and thou wilt not hear, but shalt be drawn away and worship other gods and serve them, I denounce unto you this day that ye shall surely perish. Ye shall not prolong your days upon the land whither thou passest over Jordan.

"I call heaven and earth to record this day against you, that I have set before you life and death, blessing and cursing. Therefore choose life, that both thou and thy seed may live. Love the Lord thy God, obey his voice, and cleave unto him. For he is thy life, and the length of thy days, that thou mayest dwell in the land which the Lord sware unto thy fathers, to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob, to give them."

And the old man finished what he had to say.

There was a long silence. The wind moved across the plains, and the awning above him stirred. Joshua, who should lead them across the Jordan in the morning, stood somewhere in the assembly with his arms folded and his countenance composed. The elders looked at one another. The children, who had grown restless, were hushed by their mothers' hands. No one spake. There was nothing more to add. He had said it as plainly as he knew how.

After a while, Moses sat down upon the stone that had been brought for him, and the people began to depart in small companies. The tribes moved away one by one, returning to their tents, bearing the words with them. Joshua came up to the awning and stood beside him for a long time without speaking. When he finally did, it was only to lay his hand on the old man's shoulder. Moses nodded.

The plains stretched out around them. The Jordan ran behind. The hills of the land rose in front of them in the late afternoon light. The choice was theirs now to make. And so it was on the day Moses set life and death before the people of Israel, in the last year of his pilgrimage, on the plains of Moab.
