Moses was a hundred and twenty years old. His eye had not grown dim and his natural force had not abated, but the day of his death was close enough that he had stopped writing songs and was now speaking in the plain prose of a man who knew the end. He had climbed Mount Nebo only once already, in his thoughts. He would climb it once more in his body before this week was out, and from its height he would see the land laid out below him like a promise that was now for younger feet. He knew this. He had been told this. He had accepted this. But before he climbed, he had one last thing to do.

The people had gathered. They had come tribe by tribe, the elders at the front, the families gathered behind, the children moving among the legs of the adults and being shushed by mothers who wanted to hear what the old man had to say. They stood on the plains of Moab, on the near side of the Jordan. The river ran behind the speaker. The hills of the land they were about to enter rose in front of them, blue and quiet in the morning haze. From where they stood they could see Jericho, far off, and the green line of the river marking the edge of forty years of waiting.

Moses had been speaking for days. He had stood under the awning his people had made for him, and he had recounted the way Yahweh had brought them out of Egypt — the plagues that had broken Pharaoh, the sea that had opened and closed, the song of Miriam beside the bones of the chariots. He had recounted the giving of the law at Horeb, the trembling mountain and the fire on its top. He had recounted the wandering in the wilderness, the manna, the water from the rock, the forty years that had buried a generation and grown a new one. He had warned them of the gods of the nations whose land they were about to take — the high places, the carved images, the names that would come whispering through the doorways of their houses once they had settled and built and forgotten the hunger of the wilderness. He had named the curses that would come if they turned away. He had named the blessings that would come if they obeyed.

The people had listened. The elders had nodded slowly, remembering. Some of the older women had wept silently into the corners of their veils for husbands buried in the wilderness, for parents they had left behind in Egypt, for the long road that was ending here. The children stared up at him with the open faces of those who had not been there for any of it, and who would be there for everything that came after. They did not yet know what they were inheriting. He knew this too.

And now, on the last of those days, Moses came to the part that mattered most to him. The part he had circled around for forty chapters and was finally ready to say plain. He raised his hand for silence. The silence came.

"This commandment which I command you today is not too hard for you, neither is it far off. It is not in heaven, that you should say, who shall go up for us to heaven, and bring it down to us, that we may hear it, and do it? Nor is it beyond the sea, that you should say, who shall go over the sea for us, and bring it to us? The word is very near you, in your mouth and in your heart, that you may do it.

"See, I have set before you today life and good, and death and evil. If you obey the commandments of Yahweh your God which I command you today, to love Yahweh your God, to walk in his ways and to keep his commandments, then you will live and multiply, and Yahweh your God will bless you in the land where you are going in to possess it. But if your heart turns away, and you will not hear, but are drawn away and worship other gods, you will surely perish. You will not prolong your days upon the land where you are passing over the Jordan.

"I call heaven and earth to witness against you today, that I have set before you life and death, blessing and curse. Therefore choose life, that you and your descendants may live. Love Yahweh your God, obey his voice, and cling to him. For he is your life, and the length of your days, that you may dwell in the land which Yahweh swore to your fathers, to Abraham, and to Isaac, and to Jacob, to give them."

And the old man finished what he had to say.

There was a long silence. The wind moved across the plains, and the awning above him stirred. Joshua, who would lead them across the Jordan in the morning, stood somewhere in the assembly with his arms crossed and his face composed. The elders looked at one another. The children, who had grown restless, were quieted by their mothers' hands. No one spoke. There was nothing more to add. He had said it as plainly as he knew how.

After a while, Moses sat down on the stone that had been brought for him, and the people began to disperse in small groups. The tribes moved away one by one, returning to their tents, carrying the words with them. Joshua came up to the awning and stood beside him for a long time without speaking. When he finally did, it was only to lay his hand on the old man's shoulder. Moses nodded.

The plains stretched out around them. The Jordan ran behind. The hills of the land rose in front of them in the late afternoon light. The choice was theirs now to make. And so it was on the day Moses set life and death before the people of Israel, in the last year of his pilgrimage, on the plains of Moab.
