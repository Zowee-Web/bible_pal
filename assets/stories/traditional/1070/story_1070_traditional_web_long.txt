Far to the east, in a land beyond the Jordan, beyond the desert, wise men studied the night sky. They knew the movements of stars the way a shepherd knows his flock — each one familiar, each one counted, each one fixed in its course across the heavens. They had spent years watching, charting, recording. The sky held no surprises for them.

Then a new star appeared.

It rose where no star had been. It was bright and steady against the dark, fixed in a place that had been empty the night before. The wise men saw it and marked its position. They watched it the following night. It was still there — unchanged, unmoved, burning with a clear light.

The wise men gathered and spoke to one another about what they had seen. They agreed. They would follow this star westward to the land of Judea. They began making preparations.

Camels were loaded with provisions — sacks of grain, dried fruit, skins of water for the long stretches between wells. Tents were rolled and tied. And treasures were brought out from locked rooms and wrapped carefully in cloth and placed in wooden chests strapped to the animals' backs. The wise men checked each bundle, each rope, each clasp. Then they set out.

The journey was long. They crossed open desert where the wind blew sand against their faces and into the folds of their robes. The camels plodded steadily, their wide feet pressing into the ground. At midday the heat shimmered on the horizon, and the men pulled their head coverings lower. At night the air turned cold, and they sat near small fires and watched the sky.

They passed through settlements where they refilled their water skins. They crossed dry riverbeds and climbed into rocky hill country where the paths were narrow and the camels swayed on the uneven ground. Day after day. Mile after mile.

They arrived in Jerusalem.

The city rose before them — high walls, crowded gates, the great temple gleaming on its hill. Roman soldiers stood at posts along the roads. Merchants called from stalls in the marketplaces. Donkeys and carts crowded the narrow streets. The smell of cooking fires and animal dung filled the air.

The wise men moved through the city and began asking a single question. They asked it of merchants in the market. They asked it of men gathered near the city well. They asked it of anyone who would listen.

"Where is he who has been born king of the Jews?" they said. "We saw his star when it rose, and we have come to worship him."

People stopped what they were doing. They turned and looked at the foreigners — their unfamiliar robes, their loaded camels, their earnest faces. The question spread. It passed through the marketplaces and the courtyards and the halls of the temple. It reached the palace.

When King Herod heard this, he was troubled. He sat in his chamber and turned the words over. Born king of the Jews. Foreign visitors had crossed the desert because they had seen a star and believed a king had been born. All Jerusalem was troubled with him. The unease spread through the city. People spoke in low voices near doorways and glanced toward the palace.

Herod summoned the chief priests and the scribes of the people. They came to the palace and stood before him in the great hall. The stone floor was polished. The lamps burned in their brackets. Herod looked at the assembled men and asked his question.

"Where is the Christ to be born?"

They consulted among themselves briefly. Then they answered.

"In Bethlehem of Judea," they said. They cited the words of the prophet Micah: "And you, Bethlehem, in the land of Judah, are by no means least among the rulers of Judah; for from you shall come a ruler who will shepherd my people Israel."

Herod dismissed them. He watched them file out of the hall, their sandals scraping the stone floor. Then he sent for the wise men — secretly. No one else was present when they entered the room.

Herod sat forward. His hands rested on the carved arms of his chair. He studied the men before him and spoke.

"When exactly did the star appear?" he asked.

The wise men told him. They described the night they first saw it, the position in the sky, how long ago it had been. Herod listened carefully, noting every detail.

Then he leaned back and spoke again.

"Go and search diligently for the child," he said. His voice was measured, his words chosen. "And when you have found him, bring me word, that I too may come and worship him."

The wise men bowed and left the palace. They passed through the gate of Jerusalem and took the road south toward Bethlehem. The road was short — only a few miles through the rolling Judean hills. Olive trees grew along the way, their silver-green leaves catching the late afternoon light. Small stone walls lined the terraced fields. The air was cool and dry.

Evening came. The sky darkened above them.

And then the star appeared again. The same star they had seen when it rose in the east now went before them. It moved ahead of them through the sky, low and steady, as if leading them down the road. The wise men watched it. They followed in silence, the camels' hooves soft on the packed dirt.

The star stopped. It stood still over one place in the town of Bethlehem. It did not move. It held its position above the rooftops, bright against the night.

When they saw that the star had stopped, the wise men rejoiced with exceedingly great joy. They cried out. They spoke to one another, their voices rising in the quiet street. They pointed upward at the light that burned without flickering above them. After the long journey — the desert crossings, the hills, the crowded streets of Jerusalem, the meeting with Herod — they had arrived.

They dismounted. They tied the camels and unstrapped the wooden chests from the animals' backs. They carried the chests by their leather handles and walked toward the house.

The doorway was low, set in a plain stone wall. They ducked their heads and entered. The room inside was simple. Clay walls. A packed dirt floor. A small oil lamp burned on a ledge, casting uneven light. The air smelled of the lamp oil and of wool.

They saw the young child with Mary his mother.

The wise men knelt. One by one, they went down to their knees on the hard floor. Then they fell down and worshiped him. Their foreheads touched the ground. Their robes spread around them on the dirt. The room was quiet except for the sound of their breathing and the faint hiss of the lamp.

They rose. They turned to the wooden chests they had carried from the east and opened them.

The first chest held gold. It gleamed in the lamplight — dense, heavy, catching the yellow flame and throwing it back. They lifted it out and placed it on the floor before the child.

The second chest held frankincense. They unwrapped it, and the sharp, clean scent rose immediately in the small space, filling the room. The white resin lay pale against the dark cloth.

The third chest held myrrh. It was dark — deep reddish-brown, wrapped carefully in layers of linen. They set it beside the gold and the frankincense. Its rich, bitter smell joined the sweetness of the frankincense in the close air.

The three gifts lay spread on the dirt floor before the child and his mother. The lamplight moved across them.

That night the wise men found a place to sleep. They rolled out their mats and lay down. And God warned them in a dream not to return to Herod. The warning was clear and direct. They did not question it. They did not debate.

In the morning, the wise men rose early. They packed their belongings. They checked the camels — tightening the cinches on the saddles, adjusting the ropes, filling the water skins from a well in the town. The animals stood patiently, chewing, their breath visible in the cold morning air.

The wise men did not take the road north back through Jerusalem. They turned east and departed for their own country by another way. The camels moved in a line along the unfamiliar road. Dust rose behind them and settled slowly. The hills of Bethlehem grew small behind them, and the road stretched ahead into the open country.
