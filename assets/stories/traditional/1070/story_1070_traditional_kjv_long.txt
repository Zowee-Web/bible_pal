Far to the east, in a land beyond the Jordan, beyond the desert, wise men studied the night sky. They knew the movements of the stars as a shepherd knoweth his flock — each one familiar, each one numbered, each one fixed in its course across the heavens. They had spent years watching, charting, recording. The sky held no surprises for them.

Then a new star appeared.

It rose where no star had been. It was bright and steady against the darkness, fixed in a place that had been empty the night before. The wise men saw it and marked its position. They watched it the following night. It was still there — unchanged, unmoved, burning with a clear light.

The wise men gathered and spake one to another concerning that which they had seen. They were agreed. They would follow this star westward unto the land of Judæa. They began to make ready.

Camels were laden with provisions — sacks of grain, dried fruit, skins of water for the long stretches between wells. Tents were rolled and bound. And treasures were brought forth from locked chambers and wrapped carefully in cloth and set in wooden chests strapped upon the backs of the beasts. The wise men examined each bundle, each rope, each clasp. Then they set forth.

The journey was long. They crossed open desert where the wind drave sand against their faces and into the folds of their robes. The camels plodded steadily, their wide feet pressing into the ground. At midday the heat shimmered upon the horizon, and the men drew their head coverings lower. At night the air turned cold, and they sat near small fires and watched the sky.

They passed through settlements where they filled again their water skins. They crossed dry riverbeds and climbed into rocky hill country where the paths were narrow and the camels swayed upon the uneven ground. Day after day. Mile after mile.

They arrived in Jerusalem.

The city rose before them — high walls, crowded gates, the great temple gleaming upon its hill. Roman soldiers stood at posts along the roads. Merchants called out from stalls in the marketplaces. Asses and carts crowded the narrow streets. The smell of cooking fires and the dung of beasts filled the air.

The wise men passed through the city and began asking a single question. They asked it of merchants in the market. They asked it of men gathered near the well of the city. They asked it of any who would hear them.

"Where is he that is born King of the Jews?" they said. "For we have seen his star in the east, and are come to worship him."

People stopped what they were doing. They turned and looked upon the strangers — their unfamiliar robes, their laden camels, their earnest faces. The question spread. It passed through the marketplaces and the courtyards and the halls of the temple. It reached the palace.

When Herod the king had heard these things, he was troubled. He sat in his chamber and turned the words over in his mind. Born King of the Jews. Foreign visitors had crossed the desert because they had seen a star and believed a king had been born. And all Jerusalem was troubled with him. The unease spread through the city. People spake in low voices near doorways and glanced toward the palace.

Herod gathered together all the chief priests and scribes of the people. They came unto the palace and stood before him in the great hall. The stone floor was polished. The lamps burned in their brackets. Herod looked upon the assembled men and asked his question.

"Where should Christ be born?"

They consulted among themselves briefly. Then they answered.

"In Bethlehem of Judæa," they said. They cited the words of Micah the prophet: "And thou Bethlehem, in the land of Juda, art not the least among the princes of Juda: for out of thee shall come a Governor, that shall rule my people Israel."

Herod dismissed them. He watched them file out of the hall, their sandals scraping upon the stone floor. Then he sent for the wise men — privily. No other was present when they entered the room.

Herod sat forward. His hands rested upon the carved arms of his seat. He studied the men before him and spake.

"What time did the star appear?" he asked.

The wise men told him. They described the night they first beheld it, the position in the sky, how long ago it had been. Herod hearkened carefully, noting every detail.

Then he leaned back and spake again.

"Go and search diligently for the young child," he said. His voice was measured, his words chosen. "And when ye have found him, bring me word again, that I may come and worship him also."

The wise men bowed and departed from the palace. They passed through the gate of Jerusalem and took the road southward toward Bethlehem. The road was short — only a few miles through the rolling Judæan hills. Olive trees grew along the way, their silver-green leaves catching the late afternoon light. Small stone walls lined the terraced fields. The air was cool and dry.

Evening came. The sky darkened above them.

And then the star appeared again. The same star which they had seen when it rose in the east now went before them. It moved ahead of them through the sky, low and steady, as though it led them down the road. The wise men watched it. They followed in silence, the hooves of the camels soft upon the packed earth.

The star stood still. It came and stood over where the young child was, in the town of Bethlehem. It moved not. It held its place above the rooftops, bright against the night.

When they saw that the star had stood still, the wise men rejoiced with exceeding great joy. They cried out. They spake one to another, their voices rising in the quiet street. They pointed upward at the light that burned without flickering above them. After the long journey — the desert crossings, the hills, the crowded streets of Jerusalem, the meeting with Herod — they had arrived.

They dismounted. They tied the camels and unstrapped the wooden chests from the backs of the beasts. They carried the chests by their leather handles and walked toward the house.

The doorway was low, set in a plain stone wall. They bowed their heads and entered. The room within was simple. Clay walls. A packed dirt floor. A small oil lamp burned upon a ledge, casting uneven light. The air smelled of the lamp oil and of wool.

They saw the young child with Mary his mother.

The wise men knelt. One by one, they went down upon their knees on the hard floor. Then they fell down, and worshipped him. Their foreheads touched the ground. Their robes spread about them upon the dirt. The room was quiet save for the sound of their breathing and the faint hiss of the lamp.

They rose. They turned to the wooden chests which they had carried from the east and opened them.

The first chest held gold. It gleamed in the lamplight — dense, heavy, catching the yellow flame and casting it back. They lifted it out and set it upon the floor before the child.

The second chest held frankincense. They unwrapped it, and the sharp, clean scent rose straightway in the small space, filling the room. The white resin lay pale against the dark cloth.

The third chest held myrrh. It was dark — deep reddish-brown, wrapped carefully in layers of linen. They set it beside the gold and the frankincense. Its rich, bitter smell joined the sweetness of the frankincense in the close air.

The three gifts lay spread upon the dirt floor before the child and his mother. The lamplight moved across them.

That night the wise men found a place to sleep. They unrolled their mats and lay down. And being warned of God in a dream that they should not return to Herod, the warning was clear and direct. They questioned it not. They debated not.

In the morning, the wise men rose early. They gathered their belongings. They examined the camels — tightening the cinches upon the saddles, adjusting the ropes, filling the water skins from a well in the town. The beasts stood patiently, chewing, their breath visible in the cold morning air.

The wise men took not the road north again through Jerusalem. They turned eastward and departed into their own country another way. The camels moved in a line along the unfamiliar road. Dust rose behind them and settled slowly. The hills of Bethlehem grew small behind them, and the road stretched ahead into the open country.
