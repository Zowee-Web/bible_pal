By night upon her bed, the woman sought the one her soul loved, but she did not find him. She resolved to rise and go through the city, searching the streets and squares. Still he was not there. The watchmen who went about the city found her, and she asked them whether they had seen the one her soul loved.

She had scarcely passed the watchmen when she found him. She held him and would not let him go until she had brought him into her mother's house, into the room of the woman who had conceived her. Once more came the solemn charge to the daughters of Jerusalem, sworn by the does and deer of the field: love was not to be stirred or awakened before it desired.

Then a figure rose from the wilderness like pillars of smoke, perfumed with myrrh and frankincense and every spice of the merchant. Solomon's carriage came into view. Sixty of Israel's mighty men surrounded it. All were skilled with the sword, and each wore his weapon at his thigh against the fear of night.

King Solomon had made his carriage from Lebanon wood. Its pillars were silver, its base gold, and its seat purple; its midst was paved with love from the daughters of Jerusalem. The daughters of Zion were summoned to go out and behold the king wearing the crown his mother had given him on the day of his wedding, the day of his heart's gladness.

The beloved looked upon the woman and called her beautiful. Her eyes behind her veil were like doves. Her hair appeared like a flock descending Mount Gilead; her teeth like newly shorn sheep rising from the washing, paired without one missing. Her lips were scarlet thread, her mouth lovely, and her temples behind the veil like a slice of pomegranate. Her neck was like David's tower built as an armory, hung with a thousand shields. Her breasts were like twin fawns feeding among lilies.

Until the day cooled and the shadows fled, he would go to the mountain of myrrh and the hill of frankincense. He called her altogether beautiful, without spot, and invited his bride to come with him from Lebanon, away from the heights of Amana, Senir, and Hermon, from the lions' dens and mountains of leopards.

Her glance and one chain at her neck had captured his heart. Her love was better than wine, and the fragrance of her perfumes surpassed every spice. Her lips dripped like honeycomb; honey and milk were under her tongue, and her garments carried the fragrance of Lebanon.

To him, his bride was a garden enclosed, a spring shut up, a fountain sealed. Her growth was an orchard of pomegranates and precious fruits, filled with henna and spikenard, saffron, calamus, cinnamon, incense trees, myrrh, aloes, and the finest spices. She was a fountain of gardens, a well of living water, and streams flowing from Lebanon.

The woman called to the north wind to awake and to the south wind to come. Let them blow upon her garden so that its spices might flow. She invited her beloved to come into his garden and taste its precious fruit.

He answered that he had come into his garden, his sister and bride. He had gathered myrrh with spice, eaten honeycomb with honey, and drunk wine with milk. The friends were welcomed to eat, to drink, and to drink abundantly, beloved ones.
