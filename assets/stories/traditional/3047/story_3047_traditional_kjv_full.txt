By night upon her bed, the woman sought him whom her soul loved, but she found him not. She resolved to rise and go through the city, seeking in the streets and broad ways. Still he was not there. The watchmen who went about the city found her, and she asked whether they had seen him whom her soul loved.

It was but a little after she passed the watchmen that she found him. She held him and would not let him go until she had brought him into her mother's house, into the chamber of her that conceived her. Again came the solemn charge unto the daughters of Jerusalem, sworn by the roes and hinds of the field: love was not to be stirred or awakened till it pleased.

Then one came out of the wilderness like pillars of smoke, perfumed with myrrh and frankincense and all the merchant's powders. Solomon's bed came into view. Threescore valiant men of Israel stood about it. All held swords and were expert in war, and every man wore his weapon upon his thigh because of fear in the night.

King Solomon had made himself a chariot of Lebanon wood. Its pillars were silver, its bottom gold, and its covering purple; its midst was paved with love for the daughters of Jerusalem. The daughters of Zion were summoned to go forth and behold the king wearing the crown his mother had given him in the day of his espousals, the day of the gladness of his heart.

The beloved looked upon the woman and called her fair. Her eyes within her locks were like doves. Her hair appeared like a flock descending from Mount Gilead; her teeth like newly shorn sheep rising from the washing, every one bearing twins and none barren. Her lips were a thread of scarlet, her speech comely, and her temples within her locks like a piece of pomegranate. Her neck was like David's tower built for an armoury, hung with a thousand shields. Her breasts were like twin young roes feeding among lilies.

Until the day broke and the shadows fled, he would go to the mountain of myrrh and the hill of frankincense. He called her all fair, with no spot in her, and invited his spouse to come with him from Lebanon, away from the heights of Amana, Shenir, and Hermon, from the lions' dens and mountains of leopards.

Her glance and one chain of her neck had ravished his heart. Her love was better than wine, and the smell of her ointments surpassed all spices. Her lips dropped as the honeycomb; honey and milk were under her tongue, and her garments bore the smell of Lebanon.

To him, his spouse was a garden inclosed, a spring shut up, a fountain sealed. Her plants were an orchard of pomegranates and pleasant fruits, filled with camphire and spikenard, saffron, calamus, cinnamon, frankincense trees, myrrh, aloes, and all the chief spices. She was a fountain of gardens, a well of living waters, and streams from Lebanon.

The woman called to the north wind to awake and to the south wind to come. Let them blow upon her garden, that its spices might flow. She invited her beloved to come into his garden and eat its pleasant fruit.

He answered that he had come into his garden, his sister and spouse. He had gathered myrrh with spice, eaten honeycomb with honey, and drunk wine with milk. The friends were welcomed to eat, to drink, and to drink abundantly, O beloved.
