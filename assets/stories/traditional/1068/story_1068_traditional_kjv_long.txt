Then Job answered the Lord.

He was upon his knees. His robe was rent from the collar downward, the edges frayed and grey with ash. Dust covered his forearms and the backs of his hands. His skin, which had been covered with sore boils, was rough and scarred. He pressed his palms flat against the dry ground and spake.

"I know that thou canst do all things," he said. His voice was low, rough from weeks of argument and grief. "No purpose of thine can be stayed."

The wind moved across the open land, stirring the dust in small circles near his knees.

"Thou didst ask, 'Who is this that hideth counsel without knowledge?'" Job said. He paused. His breath came slow. "Therefore have I uttered that which I understood not. Things too wonderful for me, which I knew not."

He had spoken for days. He had demanded answers. He had cried out unto the heavens and received silence, and then the silence had broken open with a voice from the whirlwind — a voice that asked questions Job could not answer. Where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the earth? Hast thou commanded the morning? Have the gates of death been revealed unto thee?

Now Job repeated the words back. "Thou saidst, 'Hear, I beseech thee, and I will speak. I will demand of thee, and declare thou unto me.'"

His forehead was near unto the ground. Ash and dirt clung to his beard.

"I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear," he said. His voice brake slightly. "But now mine eye seeth thee."

He was still for a long moment. The wind dropped. The air was dry and warm.

"Wherefore I abhor myself," Job said, "and repent in dust and ashes."

He did not rise.

The three friends stood nearby, just beyond the ash heap. Eliphaz the Temanite, the eldest, stood with his arms folded within his cloak. His face was drawn, his eyes weary. Beside him stood Bildad the Shuhite, shorter, his sandals caked with the dust of the road. And Zophar the Naamathite, who had spoken the fewest words among them but spoken them sharply. They had come weeks ago. They had sat with Job seven days and seven nights in silence before any of them opened their mouths. Then they had spoken at length — of guilt, of punishment, of the ways of God with the wicked. They had insisted. They had reasoned. They had accused.

Now they were quiet.

The Lord spake unto Eliphaz the Temanite. "My wrath is kindled against thee, and against thy two friends."

Eliphaz stiffened. His hands tightened within his cloak.

"Ye have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job hath," the Lord said.

Bildad shifted his weight. Zophar looked upon the ground.

"Therefore take unto you now seven bullocks and seven rams, and go to my servant Job, and offer up for yourselves a burnt offering. And my servant Job shall pray for you, for him will I accept, lest I deal with you after your folly. For ye have not spoken of me the thing which is right, as my servant Job hath."

The three men departed. They travelled to find the animals. It took time — seven bullocks, heavy-shouldered, dark-eyed, pulling against their leads. Seven rams, thick with wool, their hooves clacking upon the stony path. Eliphaz and Bildad and Zophar brought them back along the road that wound toward Job's place.

They stacked the wood. The logs were dry, pale, cleft down the centre. They arranged the stones for the altar. The bullocks stood roped to stakes driven into the hard ground, stamping and breathing. The rams huddled together, their wool dusty. The fire was struck. It caught slowly at the first, then climbed through the kindling and took hold of the heavier wood. Smoke rose — thick, white, curling against the blue sky, carrying the sharp smell of burning fat and cedar.

Job stood near the altar. His hands were raised, his palms open. He prayed for his three friends — Eliphaz, Bildad, Zophar — the men who had sat with him in his suffering and then spoken wrongly. The fire cracked and spat. Sparks drifted upward. The smoke leaned in the wind and straightened again.

And the Lord accepted the prayer of Job.

After Job prayed for his friends, the Lord turned the captivity of Job. The Lord gave Job twice as much as he had before.

The change came not all at once, but it came. Flocks appeared in the fields. Herds filled the pastures. Servants returned. The house, which had stood hollow and quiet, began to fill with sounds again — footsteps, the clatter of pots, the creak of doors opening and closing.

Then came his brethren.

They came along the road from the east, some on foot, some riding upon asses. His sisters came from the south, their children walking beside them. All that had known him before — neighbours, distant kin, old friends — they travelled unto his house. Some had not come during his suffering. Some had turned away. Now they passed through the gate, one by one and in small companies, and they sat with him.

They did eat bread with him at his table. The bread was warm, broken by hand, passed from one unto the next. Oil lamps burned upon the table, casting uneven light across the faces gathered there. They bemoaned him and comforted him over all the evil that the Lord had brought upon him.

Every one gave him a piece of silver. Every one gave him a ring of gold.

The pieces of silver were stacked in a wooden bowl near the door. The rings of gold — some plain, some graven with fine lines — collected in a dish of clay upon the table. They caught the lamplight as more were added, ring upon ring, each one laid down with a steady hand.

So the Lord blessed the latter end of Job more than his beginning.

Where he had once possessed seven thousand sheep, he now had fourteen thousand. The hills round about his land were white with them. Their bleating carried across the morning air, a constant sound rising and falling with the wind. Where he had once possessed three thousand camels, he now had six thousand. They moved in long lines across the open ground, their wide feet silent upon the sand, their burdens swaying. A thousand yoke of oxen stood in his fields — two thousand beasts, broad and patient, their dark hides glossy in the sun. A thousand she asses grazed his pastureland, their foals running alongside them in the tall grass.

Servants moved among the animals. Hired men mended the fences. The land was alive with work and noise and increase.

Job had seven sons. They grew strong. They built houses of their own upon the land nearby. They feasted together, each one hosting the others in his turn, the sound of their gatherings carrying across the evening air.

He had three daughters.

He called the name of the first Jemima. He called the name of the second Kezia. He called the name of the third Keren-happuch. In all the land were no women found so fair as the daughters of Job. And their father gave them inheritance among their brethren — a portion of the land, a portion of the flocks, an inheritance equal and full.

The years passed. The household grew. Children were born unto Job's sons and daughters. Then children were born unto those children. Then children again. Four generations gathered under one roof at the time of the feast — infants held upon laps, small children chasing one another between the legs of the tables, young men disputing over livestock, old women laughing near the fire.

Job lived an hundred and forty years after his restoration.

He saw his children and his children's children. He watched them come through the gate — dusty from the road, carrying bundles, leading animals, calling one to another across the yard. He sat in the doorway of his house in the late afternoon light, the noise of the household about him, the hills thick with sheep, the air smelling of bread and woodsmoke.

Then Job died, being old and full of days. The lamps in the house burned low. The flocks moved slowly across the darkening hills. The last light rested upon the rooftop, and then it was gone.
