Then Job answered the Lord.

He knelt in the dust, ashes still clinging to his forearms, the torn edges of his robe hanging loose around his shoulders. His voice was rough and low.

"I know that you can do all things," he said. "No purpose of yours can be stopped."

The wind moved across the open ground. Job's hands were flat against the earth.

"You asked, 'Who is this that hides counsel without knowledge?'" he said. "I spoke of things I did not understand. Things too wonderful for me, which I did not know."

He lifted his face slightly, then pressed it down again. Dust clung to his beard.

"You said, 'Hear, and I will speak. I will question you, and you make it known to me.'" Job's breath came slow and heavy. "I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear. But now my eye sees you."

His shoulders shook. "I despise myself," he said, "and repent in dust and ashes."

The three friends stood nearby. Eliphaz the Temanite. Bildad the Shuhite. Zophar the Naamathite. They had sat with Job seven days and seven nights. They had spoken many words to him. Now they were silent, their robes dusty, their sandals worn.

The Lord spoke to Eliphaz the Temanite. "My anger burns against you and against your two friends. You have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has."

Eliphaz did not move. Bildad's jaw tightened. Zophar looked at the ground.

"Take seven bulls and seven rams," the Lord said, "and go to my servant Job. Offer up a burnt offering for yourselves. My servant Job will pray for you. I will accept his prayer and not deal with you according to your folly. For you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has."

The three men went. They gathered the animals — seven bulls with broad, dark shoulders, seven rams thick with wool. They led them to Job's place. They stacked the wood. They built the fire. The smoke rose white and heavy, climbing straight in the still air. The smell of burning fat drifted across the open land.

Job stood near the altar and prayed for his friends. His hands were raised, palms open. The fire cracked and popped. And the Lord accepted Job's prayer.

After Job prayed for his friends, the Lord restored his fortunes. The Lord gave Job twice as much as he had before.

Then his brothers came. His sisters came. All who had known him before traveled to his house. They passed through the gate and sat with him. They ate bread with him at his table. They showed him sympathy and comforted him for all the trouble the Lord had brought upon him. Each one gave him a piece of silver. Each one gave him a gold ring. The rings clinked together in a clay bowl near his hand, glinting in the lamplight.

The Lord blessed the latter days of Job more than his beginning.

Where he had once owned seven thousand sheep, he now had fourteen thousand. Where he had once owned three thousand camels, he now had six thousand. A thousand yoke of oxen stood in his fields. A thousand female donkeys grazed across his pastureland. The hills around his land were thick with animals, their sounds carrying on the morning air — bleating, lowing, the heavy shuffle of hooves on dry ground.

He had seven sons. He had three daughters. He named the first daughter Jemimah. He named the second Keziah. He named the third Keren-happuch. In all the land there were no women so beautiful as Job's daughters. Their father gave them an inheritance among their brothers.

The household grew. Children were born to his children, and then children to them. Job lived a hundred and forty years after his restoration. He saw his sons and his daughters, and their sons and their daughters — four generations gathered under his roof, filling the rooms with noise and movement.

Then Job died, old and full of days. The house was quiet. The lamps burned low. Outside, the flocks moved slowly across the hills in the fading light.
