Then Job answered the Lord.

He knelt in the dust, ashes yet clinging to his forearms, the rent edges of his robe hanging loose about his shoulders. His voice was rough and low.

"I know that thou canst do all things," he said. "No purpose of thine can be restrained."

The wind moved across the open ground. Job's hands were flat against the earth.

"Thou didst ask, 'Who is this that hideth counsel without knowledge?'" he said. "I have uttered that which I understood not. Things too wonderful for me, which I knew not."

He lifted his face slightly, then pressed it down again. Dust clung to his beard.

"Thou saidst, 'Hear, and I will speak. I will demand of thee, and declare thou unto me.'" Job's breath came slow and heavy. "I had heard of thee by the hearing of the ear. But now mine eye seeth thee."

His shoulders shook. "I abhor myself," he said, "and repent in dust and ashes."

The three friends stood nigh. Eliphaz the Temanite. Bildad the Shuhite. Zophar the Naamathite. They had sat with Job seven days and seven nights. They had spoken many words unto him. Now they were silent, their robes dusty, their sandals worn.

The Lord spake unto Eliphaz the Temanite. "My wrath is kindled against thee, and against thy two friends. For ye have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job hath."

Eliphaz did not move. Bildad's jaw tightened. Zophar looked upon the ground.

"Take unto you now seven bullocks and seven rams," the Lord said, "and go to my servant Job. Offer up for yourselves a burnt offering. And my servant Job shall pray for you. For him will I accept, lest I deal with you after your folly. For ye have not spoken of me the thing which is right, as my servant Job hath."

The three men went. They gathered the animals — seven bullocks with broad, dark shoulders, seven rams thick with wool. They led them unto Job's place. They stacked the wood. They built the fire. The smoke rose white and heavy, climbing straight in the still air. The smell of burning fat drifted across the open land.

Job stood nigh the altar and prayed for his friends. His hands were raised, palms open. The fire cracked and popped. And the Lord accepted the face of Job.

After Job prayed for his friends, the Lord turned the captivity of Job. The Lord gave Job twice as much as he had before.

Then came there unto him all his brethren. His sisters came. All they that had been of his acquaintance before journeyed unto his house. They passed through the gate and sat with him. They did eat bread with him at his table. They bemoaned him, and comforted him over all the evil that the Lord had brought upon him. Every one also gave him a piece of money. Every one gave him a ring of gold. The rings clinked together in an earthen bowl near his hand, glinting in the lamplight.

The Lord blessed the latter end of Job more than his beginning.

Where he had once possessed seven thousand sheep, he now had fourteen thousand. Where he had once possessed three thousand camels, he now had six thousand. A thousand yoke of oxen stood in his fields. A thousand she asses grazed across his pastureland. The hills round about his land were thick with animals, their sounds carrying upon the morning air — bleating, lowing, the heavy shuffle of hooves upon dry ground.

He had also seven sons and three daughters. And he called the name of the first, Jemima. And the name of the second, Kezia. And the name of the third, Keren-happuch. In all the land were no women found so fair as the daughters of Job. And their father gave them inheritance among their brethren.

The household grew. Children were born unto his children, and then children unto them. After this lived Job an hundred and forty years, and saw his sons, and his sons' sons, even four generations gathered under his roof, filling the rooms with noise and movement.

So Job died, being old and full of days. The house was quiet. The lamps burned low. Without, the flocks moved slowly across the hills in the fading light.
