Then Job answered the Lord.

He was kneeling. His robe was torn from the collar down, the edges frayed and gray with ash. Dust covered his forearms and the backs of his hands. His skin, which had been covered with sores, was rough and scarred. He pressed his palms flat against the dry ground and spoke.

"I know that you can do all things," he said. His voice was low, rough from weeks of argument and grief. "No purpose of yours can be stopped."

The wind moved across the open land, stirring the dust in small circles near his knees.

"You asked, 'Who is this that hides counsel without knowledge?'" Job said. He paused. His breath came slow. "Therefore I have uttered what I did not understand. Things too wonderful for me, which I did not know."

He had spoken for days. He had demanded answers. He had called out to the heavens and received silence, and then the silence had broken open with a voice from the whirlwind — a voice that asked questions Job could not answer. Where were you when I laid the foundation of the earth? Have you commanded the morning? Have the gates of death been revealed to you?

Now Job repeated the words back. "You said, 'Hear, and I will speak. I will question you, and you make it known to me.'"

His forehead was near the ground. Ash and dirt clung to his beard.

"I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear," he said. His voice broke slightly. "But now my eye sees you."

He was still for a long moment. The wind dropped. The air was dry and warm.

"I despise myself," Job said, "and repent in dust and ashes."

He did not rise.

The three friends stood nearby, just beyond the ash heap. Eliphaz the Temanite, the eldest, stood with his arms folded inside his cloak. His face was drawn, his eyes tired. Beside him stood Bildad the Shuhite, shorter, his sandals caked with the dust of the road. And Zophar the Naamathite, who had spoken the fewest words among them but spoken them sharply. They had come weeks ago. They had sat with Job seven days and seven nights in silence before any of them opened their mouths. Then they had spoken at length — about guilt, about punishment, about the ways of God with the wicked. They had insisted. They had reasoned. They had accused.

Now they were quiet.

The Lord spoke to Eliphaz the Temanite. "My anger burns against you and against your two friends."

Eliphaz stiffened. His hands tightened inside his cloak.

"You have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has," the Lord said.

Bildad shifted his weight. Zophar looked at the ground.

"Now therefore take seven bulls and seven rams, and go to my servant Job, and offer up a burnt offering for yourselves. And my servant Job will pray for you, for I will accept his prayer not to deal with you according to your folly. For you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has."

The three men left. They traveled to find the animals. It took time — seven bulls, heavy-shouldered, dark-eyed, pulling against their leads. Seven rams, thick with wool, their hooves clacking on the stony path. Eliphaz and Bildad and Zophar brought them back along the road that wound toward Job's place.

They stacked the wood. The logs were dry, pale, split down the center. They arranged the stones for the altar. The bulls stood roped to stakes driven into the hard ground, stamping and breathing. The rams huddled together, their wool dusty. The fire was struck. It caught slowly at first, then climbed through the kindling and took hold of the heavier wood. Smoke rose — thick, white, curling against the blue sky, carrying the sharp smell of burning fat and cedar.

Job stood near the altar. His hands were raised, palms open. He prayed for his three friends — Eliphaz, Bildad, Zophar — the men who had sat with him in his suffering and then spoken wrongly. The fire cracked and popped. Sparks drifted upward. The smoke leaned in the wind and straightened again.

And the Lord accepted Job's prayer.

After Job prayed for his friends, the Lord restored his fortunes. The Lord gave Job twice as much as he had before.

The change did not come all at once, but it came. Flocks appeared in the fields. Herds filled the pastures. Servants returned. The house, which had stood hollow and quiet, began to fill with sounds again — footsteps, the clatter of pots, the creak of doors opening and closing.

Then his brothers came.

They came along the road from the east, some on foot, some riding donkeys. His sisters came from the south, their children walking beside them. All who had known him before — neighbors, distant kin, old friends — they traveled to his house. Some had not come during his suffering. Some had turned away. Now they passed through the gate, one by one and in small groups, and they sat with him.

They ate bread with him at his table. The bread was warm, torn by hand, passed from one person to the next. Oil lamps burned on the table, casting uneven light across the faces gathered there. They showed Job sympathy. They comforted him for all the trouble the Lord had brought upon him.

Each one gave him a piece of silver. Each one gave him a gold ring.

The silver pieces were stacked in a wooden bowl near the door. The gold rings — some plain, some etched with fine lines — collected in a clay dish on the table. They caught the lamplight as more were added, ring upon ring, each one placed with a steady hand.

The Lord blessed the latter days of Job more than his beginning.

Where he had once owned seven thousand sheep, he now had fourteen thousand. The hills around his land were white with them. Their bleating carried across the morning air, a constant sound rising and falling with the wind. Where he had once owned three thousand camels, he now had six thousand. They moved in long lines across the open ground, their wide feet silent on the sand, their loads swaying. A thousand yoke of oxen stood in his fields — two thousand animals, broad and patient, their dark hides glossy in the sun. A thousand female donkeys grazed his pastureland, their foals running alongside them in the tall grass.

Servants moved among the animals. Hired men mended fences. The land was alive with work and noise and growth.

Job had seven sons. They grew strong. They built houses of their own on the land nearby. They feasted together, each one hosting the others in turn, the sound of their gatherings carrying across the evening air.

He had three daughters.

He named the first Jemimah. He named the second Keziah. He named the third Keren-happuch. In all the land there were no women so beautiful as Job's daughters. Their father gave them an inheritance among their brothers — a share of the land, a share of the flocks, a portion equal and full.

The years passed. The household grew. Children were born to Job's sons and daughters. Then children were born to those children. Then children again. Four generations gathered under one roof at festival time — infants held on laps, small children chasing each other between the legs of the tables, young men arguing about livestock, old women laughing near the fire.

Job lived a hundred and forty years after his restoration.

He saw his children and his children's children. He watched them come through the gate — dusty from the road, carrying bundles, leading animals, calling to each other across the yard. He sat in the doorway of his house in the late afternoon light, the noise of the household around him, the hills thick with sheep, the air smelling of bread and woodsmoke.

Then Job died, old and full of days. The lamps in the house burned low. The flocks moved slowly across the darkening hills. The last light rested on the rooftop, and then it was gone.
