The word of the Lord came unto Jonah the second time. The same God that had spoken before — the God from whose presence Jonah had fled unto Tarshish, the God that had sent the tempest and the fish and the three days of darkness — spake again. The same command. The same city. As though none of it had come to pass.

"Arise, go unto Nineveh, that great city, and preach unto it the preaching that I bid thee."

Jonah fled not this time.

He rose and went eastward. The journey was long — many weeks of walking through country he knew not, across open plains and through river valleys, past villages where men spake tongues he understood not. The road was dusty. The sun was fierce. He bore little — a skin of water, a staff, the clothes upon his back. He slept upon the ground at night and walked when the sun rose. He hurried not, but neither did he stay.

Behind him lay the sea, the storm, the belly of the fish. Before him lay Nineveh.

Nineveh was the greatest city in the world. Its walls were enormous — thick enough for chariots to drive upon the top, high enough to block the sun at certain hours. Guard towers stood at every interval. The gates were fitted with iron. Within the walls, the city spread in every direction — houses and temples and markets and barracks, streets thronging with soldiers and merchants and slaves and families. It was the capital of Assyria, the empire that had terrorized every nation within its reach, and its scale was made to overawe.

It was a city of three days' journey from one side to the other. Jonah entered through one of the western gates and walked a day's journey into the heart of the city. He passed market squares where vendors sold spice and cloth and wrought metal. He passed temples to strange gods, with smoke rising from their altars. He passed soldiers in bronze and women bearing water and children playing in the dirt between the houses.

Then he stopped. In the midst of a street, surrounded by people that knew him not, he opened his mouth and cried aloud.

"Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown!"

Eight words. No credential. No preamble. No theology. A deadline and a judgment, spoken by a stranger in a foreign city.

And the people of Nineveh believed God.

It began with the few that heard him. They ceased what they were doing. They looked one upon another. They went home and told their households. The households told the neighbours. The neighbours told the merchants. The merchants told the soldiers. The word moved through Nineveh as fire through a field of dry grass, street by street, house by house, from the outer markets to the inner courts.

The people proclaimed a fast. From the greatest unto the least — from the wealthiest ruler to the poorest beggar — they put on sackcloth. They covered themselves in ashes. They ceased eating. They ceased drinking. They ceased working. The busy, roaring city fell still. The markets closed. The streets were emptied. Nineveh, which had never in its history bowed to any power, bowed.

When the word came unto the king, he did what none looked for. He rose from his throne. He put off his royal robes — the purple, the gold, the heavy layers of silk and authority — and he put on sackcloth. He sat down in ashes. The most powerful man in the most powerful city upon the earth sat in the dust, wearing the garment of mourning.

Then he caused it to be proclaimed and published through Nineveh by the decree of the king and his nobles: "Let neither man nor beast, herd nor flock, taste any thing: let them not feed, nor drink water: but let man and beast be covered with sackcloth, and cry mightily unto God: yea, let them turn every one from his evil way, and from the violence that is in their hands."

He paused. Then he added the words that contained the sole hope of any of them.

"Who can tell if God will turn and repent, and turn away from his fierce anger, that we perish not?"

The city obeyed. They turned from their wickedness. They stayed the violence. They sat in ashes and cried out unto a God they had never worshipped, hoping he might hear them.

God saw what they did. Not their words — their works. He saw that they turned from their evil way.

And God repented of the evil that he had said he would do unto them; and he did it not.
