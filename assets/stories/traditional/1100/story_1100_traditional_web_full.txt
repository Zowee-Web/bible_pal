The word of the Lord came to Jonah a second time. The same God. The same command. The same city.

"Arise," God said. "Go to Nineveh, that great city, and proclaim to it the message that I tell you."

Jonah had run from this call once before. He had boarded a ship, sailed for Tarshish, been thrown into the sea, and spent three days in the belly of a great fish. He had prayed from the darkness. He had been vomited onto dry land. And now the word came again, unchanged, as though none of it had happened.

This time, Jonah went.

He rose and traveled east. The journey took weeks — through open country, across rivers, through towns where no one knew his name or his God. The road was long and dusty. The days were hot. But Jonah kept walking, one foot in front of the other, moving toward the city he had once fled from at the cost of everything.

Nineveh was enormous. The city walls were high and thick, built of brick and stone, stretching far in every direction. Inside the walls, the streets were crowded with people — merchants calling from their stalls, soldiers moving in formation, families carrying water from the wells. It took three days to walk across the city from one gate to the other. The noise pressed in from every side. This was the capital of Assyria, the empire that had terrorized the nations, and its scale was staggering.

Jonah entered the city. He walked for one full day into the heart of it, past market squares and temple gates and houses stacked close together along narrow streets. He did not seek permission. He did not introduce himself.

He stopped in the middle of the city and began to speak.

"In forty days," he cried, "Nineveh will be overthrown!"

That was the message. Eight words. No theology. No context. No invitation to negotiate. Just a deadline and a consequence.

And the people of Nineveh believed God.

It started in the streets where Jonah stood — a few people who stopped and listened, then more, then more. Word spread from block to block, from the markets to the houses to the palace. It moved through the city the way fire moves through dry grass — quickly, unstoppably, touching everything.

The people proclaimed a fast. From the greatest to the least, they put on sackcloth — rough garments of mourning and repentance. They sat in ashes. They stopped eating. They stopped working. The busy, noisy city grew still. The markets closed. The streets emptied.

When the message reached the king of Nineveh, he rose from his throne. He took off his royal robes — the purple, the gold, the finery — and put on sackcloth. He sat down in ashes. Then he issued a decree throughout the city: "Let neither man nor beast, herd nor flock, taste anything. Let them not eat or drink. Let every person cry out mightily to God. Let everyone turn from his evil way and from the violence that is in his hands. Who knows? God may turn and relent from his fierce anger, so that we do not perish."

The whole city obeyed. A hundred and twenty thousand people, the scripture would later say, who could not tell their right hand from their left. They turned from their evil ways. They stopped.

God saw what they did — not their words, but their actions. He saw that they turned from their evil.

And God relented. He did not bring upon them the destruction he had threatened.
