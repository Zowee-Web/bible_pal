In the grand palace of Susa, gilded with shimmering gold and draped in heavy curtains, Esther dwelled as queen beside King Xerxes. The sounds of palace life—whispers, footsteps, and the distant music of the court—often reached her chambers, yet her heart carried quiet concern. Beyond the ornate walls, her uncle Mordecai moved among the city’s gates, ever watchful, ever faithful. Word came to him of a grave threat: a decree, sealed by the king and Haman, calling for the destruction of the Jewish people throughout the Persian Empire.

When Mordecai learned of this calamity, he tore his garments, clothed himself with sackcloth and ashes, and mourned in the public square. Grief and fear spread among the Jews in every province, each weeping and fasting over the coming peril. News of Mordecai’s distress quickly reached Esther through her servants. Troubled, she sent garments to cover him, but he would not accept them. Through Hathach, a trusted messenger, she learned the full truth—the copy of the decree, the promise of silver Haman had offered to the royal treasury, and Mordecai’s plea that she approach the king to beg for mercy and plead for her people.

Esther's heart trembled at this request. She reminded Mordecai that anyone who approached the king without being summoned faced death unless he extended his golden scepter; it had been thirty days since the king had called for her. Yet Mordecai's answer was unwavering: "Don't think that you will escape in the king's house more than all the Jews. For if you remain silent, relief and deliverance will arise from another place, but you and your father's house will perish. Who knows whether you have come to the kingdom for such a time as this?"

Esther took his words to heart. She sent one last message to Mordecai saying, “Go, gather together all the Jews who are in Susa, and fast for me. Don’t eat or drink for three days, night or day. My maidens and I also will fast. Afterward, I will go to the king, even though it is against the law. If I perish, I perish.” So Mordecai departed, and did as Esther had commanded, and in every part of the city, there was quiet resolve and hope hidden behind closed doors and whispering prayers.

When three days had passed, Esther put on her royal robes. The morning light glinted off gold threads as she walked toward the inner court. The way was hushed; her every step echoed under high ceilings and past silent guards. Beyond the heavy curtains, the king sat on his royal throne, facing the entrance. Around him, courtiers hovered, but all eyes turned as Esther appeared in the doorway. The moment hung, breathless and still in the air, as the law of Persia forbade such approach without summons.

But King Xerxes saw Esther and was pleased. He extended his golden scepter toward her. Moving forward, Esther touched its tip. The tension broke; she had found favor. The king asked, “What is it, Queen Esther? What is your request? It shall be given you, even to half the kingdom.” Esther, with measured words, invited the king and Haman to a banquet she had prepared. There, over eating and wine, the king again asked her request. Esther only invited them to a second banquet the next day.

Meanwhile, Haman left the palace filled with pride. Yet his heart burned with anger when he saw Mordecai at the king’s gate, refusing to bow. At home, boasting to his friends and wife of his honors, Haman built a gallows seventy-five feet high, planning to ask the king for Mordecai’s life the next morning.

That night, the king could not sleep. The chronicles were read to him, and he discovered that Mordecai had once saved his life from a plot. Summoning Haman early the next day, the king asked, “What should be done to the man whom the king delights to honor?” Haman, certain it was himself, suggested elaborate honor—royal robes, a parade on the king’s horse, and public praise. The king ordered Haman to do all this for Mordecai. Humbled, Haman obeyed, leading Mordecai through the streets in honor.

When the second banquet came, the heavy silence returned. The king and Haman reclined, and Xerxes asked again for Esther’s petition. This time, Esther spoke: “If I have found favor in your sight, O king, and if it pleases the king, let my life be given me at my petition, and my people at my request. For we have been sold, I and my people, to be destroyed and slain. If it had only been slavery, I would have kept silent.” The king, astonished, demanded, “Who is he, and where is he, who has dared to do this?”

Esther revealed, "The adversary and enemy is this wicked Haman." The king, enraged, commanded that Haman be hanged on the very gallows he had built for Mordecai, and so it was done. Esther and Mordecai stood in the light of deliverance, having found courage for such a time as this.
