In the great palace at Shushan, gilded with shimmering gold and draped in heavy curtains, Esther dwelt as queen beside king Ahasuerus. The sounds of palace life — whispers, footsteps, and the distant music of the court — often reached her chambers, yet her heart bore quiet concern. Beyond the ornate walls, her uncle Mordecai moved among the gates of the city, ever watchful, ever faithful. Word came unto him of a grave threat: a decree, sealed by the king and Haman, calling for the destruction of the Jewish people throughout the Persian empire.

When Mordecai perceived this calamity, he rent his garments, clothed himself with sackcloth and ashes, and mourned in the public square. Grief and fear spread among the Jews in every province, every one weeping and fasting over the coming peril. Tidings of Mordecai's distress quickly reached Esther by her handmaidens. Sore troubled, she sent raiment to clothe him, but he received it not. Through Hatach, a trusted messenger, she learned the whole truth. She learned of the copy of the decree. She learned of the promise of silver Haman had offered unto the royal treasuries. And she learned of Mordecai's plea — that she draw near unto the king, to make supplication for mercy, and intreat for her people.

Esther's heart trembled at this request. She reminded Mordecai that whosoever drew near unto the king without being called faced death, except the king should hold out the golden sceptre; it had been thirty days since the king had called for her. Yet Mordecai's answer was unwavering: "Think not with thyself that thou shalt escape in the king's house, more than all the Jews. For if thou altogether holdest thy peace at this time, then shall there enlargement and deliverance arise to the Jews from another place; but thou and thy father's house shall be destroyed: and who knoweth whether thou art come to the kingdom for such a time as this?"

Esther took his words unto her heart. She sent one last message unto Mordecai, saying, "Go, gather together all the Jews that are present in Shushan, and fast ye for me, and neither eat nor drink three days, night or day. I also and my maidens will fast likewise. And so will I go in unto the king, which is not according to the law. And if I perish, I perish." So Mordecai departed, and did according to all that Esther had commanded him, and in every part of the city there was quiet resolve and hope hidden behind closed doors and whispering prayers.

And it came to pass on the third day, that Esther put on her royal apparel. The morning light glinted upon threads of gold as she walked toward the inner court. The way was hushed; every step echoed beneath high ceilings and past silent guards. Beyond the heavy curtains, the king sat upon his royal throne, facing the entrance. Round about him courtiers hovered, but all eyes turned as Esther appeared in the doorway. The moment hung, breathless and still in the air, for the law of Persia forbade such approach without summons.

But when the king beheld Esther, she obtained favour in his sight. He held out the golden sceptre unto her. Moving forward, Esther drew near, and touched the top of the sceptre. The tension brake; she had found grace. The king asked, "What wilt thou, queen Esther? and what is thy request? it shall be even given thee to the half of the kingdom." Esther, with measured words, invited the king and Haman unto a banquet which she had prepared. There, over meat and wine, the king again asked her request. Esther only bade them come unto a second banquet on the morrow.

Meanwhile, Haman went forth out of the palace filled with pride. Yet his heart burned with anger when he beheld Mordecai at the king's gate, refusing to bow. At home, boasting before his friends and his wife of his honours, Haman built a gallows fifty cubits high, purposing to ask the king for Mordecai's life on the morrow.

On that night the king could not sleep. The book of the chronicles was read before him, and he discovered that Mordecai had once delivered him from a plot upon his life. Calling Haman early on the morrow, the king asked, "What shall be done unto the man whom the king delighteth to honour?" Haman, certain that it was himself, suggested elaborate honour — royal robes, a parade upon the king's horse, and public proclamation. The king commanded Haman to do all this for Mordecai. Humbled, Haman obeyed, leading Mordecai through the streets in honour.

When the second banquet was come, the heavy silence returned. The king and Haman reclined, and Ahasuerus asked yet again concerning Esther's petition. This time, Esther spake: "If I have found favour in thy sight, O king, and if it please the king, let my life be given me at my petition, and my people at my request. For we are sold, I and my people, to be destroyed, to be slain, and to perish. But if we had been sold for bondmen and bondwomen, I had held my tongue." The king, astonished, demanded, "Who is he, and where is he, that durst presume in his heart to do so?"

Esther revealed, "The adversary and enemy is this wicked Haman." The king, in his wrath, commanded that Haman should be hanged upon the very gallows which he had prepared for Mordecai, and so it was done. Esther and Mordecai stood in the light of deliverance, having found courage for such a time as this.
