In the royal palace of Susa, gold gleamed on every column and rose in intricate patterns across the lofty ceilings. Thick, heavy curtains hung between arched doorways, muffling sounds and cloaking the marble halls in a gentle hush. The Persian empire’s heart beat here, in a place where power rested behind ornate doors and every whisper could ripple outward to distant provinces. Queen Esther lived here, cloaked now in royal robes but born from a people who served, not ruled. Her cousin Mordecai, who had once raised her as his own daughter after her parents died, kept faithful watch at the palace gate.

Silent anxiety lingered in the air. A new decree, stamped with the signet of King Xerxes, had gone out. It was Haman, the king’s highest official, who had proposed it, driven by anger that Mordecai would not bow before him. Now, throughout all the provinces of Persia, Esther’s people—the Jews—were marked for destruction. The news pressed in upon the palace like a gathering storm. Mordecai tore his clothes and put on sackcloth, sitting before the king’s gate in signs of grief. He could not enter the palace clothed this way, but his sorrow could not be hidden.

Esther learned of Mordecai’s distress from her servants. She sent fresh garments to him, but he would not accept them. Then she commanded her servant Hathach to go and speak with Mordecai, to learn the cause of his mourning. Through Hathach, Mordecai explained the terrible decree, and he sent Esther a copy of the edict. He pleaded with her to go before the king and beg for mercy for her people. Esther’s heart was troubled. Everyone knew that anyone who approached the king in the inner court without being summoned would be put to death, unless the king extended his golden scepter. It had been thirty days since the king had called for Esther.

The palace’s golden corridors seemed colder now, shadows gathered in corners as Esther weighed the danger. She sent word back to Mordecai: “All the king’s servants know that whoever enters the inner court uncalled will die, except for the one to whom the king holds out the golden scepter. I have not been called these thirty days.” Hathach carried her words and returned with Mordecai’s answer. Mordecai said, “Do not think, because you are in the king’s house, you alone will escape. If you remain silent now, relief and deliverance will arise for the Jews from another place, but you and your father’s house will perish. Who knows whether you have come to the kingdom for such a time as this?”

Esther felt the weight of those words. She replied to Mordecai, “Go, gather all the Jews in Susa and fast for me. Do not eat or drink for three days, night or day. My servants and I will fast as well. Then I will go to the king, though it is against the law, and if I perish, I perish.” Mordecai went and did all that Esther had commanded.

Three days passed, and quiet hope mingled with fear. Esther put on her royal robes, their colors bright and fine, her heart steady with resolve. She walked through silent, empty corridors, every step measured until she reached the inner courtyard. Light from lofty windows cast golden hues upon the marble tiles. Facing her, beyond the heavy curtains, sat King Xerxes on his majestic throne of gold. Courtiers and servants seemed to hold their breath; all turned to watch. Esther stood in the inner court, waiting. In that stillness, the king lifted his gaze and saw her. She waited for his judgment.

The king’s face softened. Reaching out his hand, he extended the golden scepter toward Esther. Soundless relief rippled through the room. Esther approached and touched the tip of the scepter. The king spoke gently, “What is your request, Queen Esther? It shall be given you, even to half of the kingdom.” Esther answered, “If it pleases the king, let the king and Haman come today to a banquet I have prepared.” The king agreed at once, and hurried to call Haman.

At the banquet, wine flowed and conversation lingered in the sweet perfumes of the palace. The king again asked, “What is your petition? It shall be granted.” Esther replied, “If I have found favor, let the king and Haman come again tomorrow to the banquet I prepare. Then I will answer.” Haman left that day joyful for the honor but, seeing Mordecai at the king’s gate, he burned with anger when Mordecai did not bow. Haman’s wife and friends advised him to build a gallows for Mordecai. Pleased, Haman ordered it to be constructed, planning Mordecai’s death the next morning.

That night, sleeplessness troubled the king. He commanded the chronicles of his reign to be read aloud. There, he learned of a plot once uncovered by Mordecai, who had saved his life. The king asked, “What honor have we given Mordecai for this?” The answer returned, “Nothing has been done.” In the morning, Haman arrived to request Mordecai’s execution. Before he could speak, the king asked, “What should be done for the man whom the king delights to honor?” Haman, thinking the honor meant for himself, described royal robes, a crown, and a grand procession. The king declared, “Do this for Mordecai. Do not leave out anything you have mentioned.” Haman, humiliated, obeyed.

Later that day, Haman went to Esther’s second banquet. Rich aromas filled the room, and the king and Haman reclined at the table. Again the king asked, “Queen Esther, what is your petition?” Esther replied, her voice steady before the king, “If I have found favor, let my life be given me at my petition, and my people at my request. For we have been sold to be destroyed, slain, and wiped out. If only we had been sold as slaves, I would have remained silent, but our suffering is too great.”

King Xerxes asked, “Who is he, and where is he who dared do this?” Esther said, “The adversary and enemy is this wicked Haman.” Haman’s face turned pale with terror before the king and queen. The king rose angrily and walked into the palace garden as Haman begged Esther for his life, falling upon her couch. When the king returned, he saw Haman at Esther’s feet and exclaimed, “Will he even assault the queen in my own house?” At the king’s command, Haman’s face was covered. Harbona, one of the eunuchs, said, “Behold, the gallows Haman made for Mordecai stands at his house.” The king ordered, “Hang him upon it.” So they hanged Haman on the gallows he had prepared for Mordecai, and the king’s anger was assuaged.

That same day, King Xerxes gave Queen Esther Haman’s house. Mordecai came before the king, for Esther had revealed his relationship to her. The king took off his signet ring, which he had taken from Haman, and gave it to Mordecai. Esther set Mordecai over Haman’s estate. The gold throne room, once heavy with silent fear, stood now as a place of deliverance. Esther’s courage had preserved her people in “such a time as this,” and the decree threatening the Jews began to be overturned. The quiet terror faded from the palace, replaced by hope and a future restored, just as Mordecai and Esther had sought before the king.
