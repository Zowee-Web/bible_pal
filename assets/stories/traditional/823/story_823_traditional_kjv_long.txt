In the royal palace at Shushan, gold gleamed upon every pillar and rose in intricate patterns across the lofty ceilings. Thick, heavy curtains hung between arched doorways, muffling sounds and cloaking the marble halls in a gentle hush. The heart of the Persian empire beat here, in a place where power rested behind ornate doors and every whisper could ripple outward unto distant provinces. Queen Esther dwelt here, cloaked now in royal apparel, but born of a people who served, and ruled not. Her cousin Mordecai, who had taken her for his own daughter after the death of her parents, kept faithful watch at the king's gate.

A silent anxiety lingered in the air. A new decree, sealed with the signet of king Ahasuerus, was gone forth. It was Haman, the king's highest officer, who had proposed it, driven by anger that Mordecai would not bow before him. Now, throughout all the provinces of Persia, Esther's people — the Jews — were marked for destruction. The tidings pressed in upon the palace like a gathering storm. Mordecai rent his clothes and put on sackcloth, sitting before the king's gate in signs of grief. He could not enter the palace clothed thus, but his sorrow could not be hid.

Esther learned of Mordecai's distress from her handmaidens. She sent fresh raiment unto him, but he received it not. Then she commanded Hatach her servant to go forth and speak with Mordecai, to learn the cause of his mourning. Through Hatach, Mordecai declared unto her the terrible decree, and he sent Esther a copy of the writing. He charged her that she should go in unto the king and make supplication for her people. Esther's heart was troubled. All knew that whosoever drew near unto the king in the inner court without being called should be put to death, except such to whom the king should hold out the golden sceptre. It had been thirty days since the king had called for Esther.

The golden corridors of the palace seemed colder now, and shadows gathered in the corners as Esther weighed the danger. She sent word again unto Mordecai: "All the king's servants do know, that whosoever, whether man or woman, shall come unto the king into the inner court, who is not called, there is one law of his to put him to death, except such to whom the king shall hold out the golden sceptre, that he may live: but I have not been called to come in unto the king these thirty days." Hatach bare her words, and returned with Mordecai's answer. Mordecai said, "Think not with thyself that thou shalt escape in the king's house, more than all the Jews. For if thou altogether holdest thy peace at this time, then shall there enlargement and deliverance arise to the Jews from another place; but thou and thy father's house shall be destroyed: and who knoweth whether thou art come to the kingdom for such a time as this?"

Esther felt the weight of those words. She answered Mordecai, "Go, gather together all the Jews that are present in Shushan, and fast ye for me, and neither eat nor drink three days, night or day: I also and my maidens will fast likewise; and so will I go in unto the king, which is not according to the law: and if I perish, I perish." So Mordecai went his way, and did according to all that Esther had commanded him.

Three days passed, and quiet hope mingled with fear. Esther put on her royal apparel, the colours bright and fine, her heart steady with resolve. She walked through silent, empty corridors, every step measured until she came unto the inner court. Light from lofty windows cast golden hues upon the tiles of marble. Before her, beyond the heavy curtains, sat king Ahasuerus upon his royal throne of gold. Courtiers and servants seemed to hold their breath; all turned to behold her. Esther stood in the inner court, waiting. In that stillness, the king lifted up his gaze, and beheld her. She waited upon his judgment.

The king's countenance softened. Reaching forth his hand, he held out the golden sceptre toward Esther. Soundless relief rippled through the room. Esther drew near and touched the top of the sceptre. The king spake gently, "What wilt thou, queen Esther? and what is thy request? it shall be even given thee to the half of the kingdom." Esther answered, "If it seem good unto the king, let the king and Haman come this day unto the banquet that I have prepared for him." The king agreed straightway, and hasted to call Haman.

At the banquet, wine flowed and conversation lingered in the sweet perfumes of the palace. The king again asked, "What is thy petition? it shall be granted thee." Esther replied, "If I have found favour in the sight of the king, let the king and Haman come tomorrow unto the banquet that I shall prepare for them; and I will do tomorrow as the king hath said." Haman went forth that day joyful for the honour, but, beholding Mordecai at the king's gate, he burned with anger when Mordecai bowed not. Haman's wife and his friends counselled him to build a gallows for Mordecai. Pleased, Haman commanded that it should be made, purposing Mordecai's death on the morrow.

On that night the king could not sleep. He commanded the book of the chronicles of his reign to be read before him. There, he learned of a plot once uncovered by Mordecai, who had saved his life. The king asked, "What honour and dignity hath been done to Mordecai for this?" The answer came, "There is nothing done for him." In the morning, Haman came to request Mordecai's execution. Or ever he could speak, the king asked, "What shall be done unto the man whom the king delighteth to honour?" Haman, supposing that the honour was meant for himself, described royal robes, a crown, and a grand procession. The king commanded, "Make haste, and take the apparel and the horse, as thou hast said, and do even so to Mordecai. Let nothing fail of all that thou hast spoken." Haman, humbled, obeyed.

Later that day, Haman went unto Esther's second banquet. Rich savours filled the chamber, and the king and Haman reclined at the table. Again the king asked, "What is thy petition, queen Esther?" Esther answered, her voice steady before the king, "If I have found favour in thy sight, O king, and if it please the king, let my life be given me at my petition, and my people at my request. For we are sold, I and my people, to be destroyed, to be slain, and to perish. But if we had been sold for bondmen and bondwomen, I had held my tongue, although the enemy could not countervail the king's damage."

King Ahasuerus asked, "Who is he, and where is he, that durst presume in his heart to do so?" Esther said, "The adversary and enemy is this wicked Haman." Haman's countenance waxed pale with terror before the king and the queen. The king arose in his wrath and went into the palace garden, as Haman besought Esther for his life, falling upon her couch. When the king returned, he beheld Haman at the feet of Esther and cried out, "Will he force the queen also before me in the house?" At the king's commandment, Haman's face was covered. Harbonah, one of the chamberlains, said, "Behold also, the gallows fifty cubits high, which Haman had made for Mordecai, standeth in the house of Haman." The king commanded, "Hang him thereon." So they hanged Haman on the gallows that he had prepared for Mordecai. Then was the king's wrath pacified.

On that same day did the king Ahasuerus give the house of Haman unto Esther the queen. Mordecai came before the king, for Esther had told what he was unto her. The king took off his ring, which he had taken from Haman, and gave it unto Mordecai. And Esther set Mordecai over the house of Haman. The golden throne room, once heavy with silent fear, stood now as a place of deliverance. Esther's courage had preserved her people in such a time as this, and the decree threatening the Jews began to be overturned. The quiet terror faded from the palace, replaced by hope and a future restored, even as Mordecai and Esther had sought before the king.
