King Ahab sent messengers throughout all Israel and gathered the people together at Mount Carmel. He gathered the four hundred and fifty prophets of Baal as well, summoning them from their high places and their groves. They came up the mountain in a long line — robed figures moving through the scrub and dry grass along the ridge. Behind them came the people of Israel, hundreds of them, climbing the rocky slopes as the morning light spread across the coastal plain far below.

Mount Carmel rose above the land like a broad shoulder of limestone. The wind moved across its summit without obstruction. The sky was wide and pale. The people spread out along the ridge and among the rocks, finding places to stand and watch.

Elijah stood before them all.

One man. Across from him, four hundred and fifty.

He looked out over the crowd. "How long will you go limping between two opinions?" he said. His voice was hard and clear in the open air. "If the Lord is God, follow him. But if Baal, then follow him."

The people did not answer him a word. The wind moved through the silence.

Elijah spoke again. "I, even I only, am left a prophet of the Lord." He gestured toward the mass of prophets opposite him. "But Baal's prophets are four hundred and fifty men."

He let the number hang in the air. Then he laid out the terms.

"Let two bulls be given to us. Let them choose one bull for themselves and cut it in pieces and lay it on the wood, but put no fire to it. I will prepare the other bull and lay it on the wood and put no fire to it. Then you call upon the name of your god, and I will call upon the name of the Lord. And the god who answers by fire — he is God."

All the people answered together. "It is well spoken."

Two bulls were brought forward, led up from the lower slopes on ropes. Their hooves scraped on the rocks. Elijah turned to the prophets of Baal. "Choose for yourselves one bull and prepare it first, for you are many. Then call upon the name of your god, but put no fire to it."

The prophets of Baal chose their bull. They slaughtered it there on the ridge. They built an altar of stones and arranged wood upon it and laid the pieces of the bull on the wood. The blood ran down the stones and into the dust. They positioned themselves around their altar — a great ring of men, shoulder to shoulder.

From morning they began to call.

"O Baal, answer us!"

Their voices rose together across the summit. Some raised their hands toward the sky. Some pressed their faces toward the altar. They called again. "O Baal, answer us!" They moved around the altar, limping and swaying in a rhythmic circle, their robes dragging over the rocky ground.

The morning passed.

No voice answered. No one responded. The meat sat on the wood. The wood sat on the stones. Nothing burned.

The sun climbed to its highest point. The heat pressed down on the mountain. The prophets of Baal kept calling, their voices growing hoarse. The people of Israel stood on the slopes and watched.

At noon, Elijah mocked them.

"Cry aloud," he said, "for he is a god." His voice carried across the ridge, sharp with scorn. "Either he is musing, or he is relieving himself, or he is on a journey, or perhaps he is asleep and must be awakened."

The prophets of Baal cried louder. They drew swords and lances. They cut themselves — arms, chests, faces — slashing their own skin as was their custom, until blood gushed over them. It ran down their forearms and dripped from their elbows. It streaked their robes. They raved and shouted and staggered around their altar, their voices cracking, their limbs smeared red.

Midday passed. The afternoon wore on. The shadows of the rocks began to lengthen across the dry grass. The prophets of Baal continued — crying out, bleeding, staggering — until the time of the evening offering. Their voices had gone thin. Some of them knelt in the dust, barely able to speak.

No voice. No answer. No one paid attention.

The altar of Baal sat cold and dark in the late afternoon light. Flies had begun to gather on the raw meat.

Elijah turned to the people.

"Come near to me," he said.

The crowd moved forward. Feet scraped over rock and gravel. The people pressed closer, gathering around Elijah on the open ridge. Before them, on the ground, lay the ruins of an altar of the Lord that had been thrown down. The stones were scattered and half-buried in dirt. Grass grew between them.

Elijah knelt beside the wreckage. He began pulling stones from the ground, brushing off the dirt, setting them upright. He took twelve stones — one for each of the tribes of the sons of Jacob, to whom the word of the Lord had come saying, "Israel shall be your name." One by one he set them in place, fitting them together with his hands, building the altar back up from its ruin.

With the twelve stones he built an altar in the name of the Lord.

Then he dug a trench around the altar. He worked with his hands and perhaps a blade, scooping out the rocky soil, widening and deepening the channel until it could hold two measures of seed. The dirt piled up on either side. The people watched every movement.

He arranged the wood on the rebuilt altar. He cut the bull in pieces. He laid the pieces on the wood. The meat glistened. The wood was dry and pale in the evening light.

Then he gave an instruction no one expected.

"Fill four jars with water," Elijah said, "and pour it on the burnt offering and on the wood."

Men brought the jars. They filled them and carried them to the altar and tipped them over the meat and the wood. Water splashed down over the offering, soaked into the grain of the wood, ran down the surfaces of the twelve stones.

"Do it a second time," Elijah said.

They filled the jars again and poured. The wood darkened completely. Water pooled between the pieces of meat and dripped from the edges of the altar into the dust below.

"Do it a third time."

They poured a third time. Water streamed off the altar now, running in rivulets down the stones, flowing into the trench. The trench filled. The surface of the water reflected the sky. The entire altar stood soaked — the wood waterlogged, the meat drenched, the stones slick and gleaming. Twelve jars of water poured out on a sacrifice that was meant to burn.

The crowd stood silent. The prophets of Baal stood bleeding and exhausted nearby, their altar still cold.

At the time of the evening offering, Elijah stepped forward.

He stood alone before the drenched altar. The trench of water lay at his feet. The crowd pressed behind him. The wind had dropped. The ridge was still.

He prayed once. One prayer.

"O Lord, God of Abraham, Isaac, and Israel," he said, "let it be known this day that you are God in Israel, and that I am your servant, and that I have done all these things at your word. Answer me, O Lord, answer me, that this people may know that you, O Lord, are God, and that you have turned their hearts back."

Fire fell from the Lord.

It came down from above and struck the altar. It consumed the burnt offering. It consumed the wood beneath it. It consumed the twelve stones — the very rocks split and broke apart in the heat. It consumed the dust around the altar. It licked up every drop of water standing in the trench. The heat rolled outward in a wave that pressed against the faces of the crowd.

Where the altar had stood, the ground was scorched bare. Stone, wood, meat, water — all of it gone.

All the people saw it. Every man and woman on that ridge went down. Knees struck rock. Faces pressed into the dust. Their voices rose together across the summit of Mount Carmel, hundreds of them calling out the same words at once.

"The Lord, he is God! The Lord, he is God!"
