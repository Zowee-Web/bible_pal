The kings west of the Jordan gathered with one accord to fight against Joshua. But the people of Gibeon, who had heard what Israel did to Jericho and Ai, chose a ruse instead. They loaded their donkeys with worn-out sacks and cracked wineskins, put on patched sandals and old clothes, took dry and crumbling bread, and came to the camp at Gilgal. "We have come from a far country," they said. "Make a covenant with us."

The men of Israel put the question to them — "Perhaps you live right among us; how then could we make a covenant with you?" — but the Gibeonites pressed their story: they had come from a very far land because of the fame of Yahweh their God; and here was the proof, they said — bread gone dry, wineskins split, clothes and sandals worn by the long road. The men of Israel sampled their provisions. But they did not ask counsel from the mouth of Yahweh. So Joshua made peace and a covenant to let them live, and the leaders swore an oath to them.

Three days later the truth came out: these people were their neighbors. Israel came to their cities but did not strike them, because the leaders had sworn by Yahweh. The congregation murmured, but the leaders held to the oath. "We have sworn to them by Yahweh; we cannot touch them," they said, "lest wrath come on us."

Then Joshua confronted them. "Why did you deceive us, saying you were far off, when you live among us? Now you are cursed — you will always be slaves for the house of my God." They answered that they had heard Yahweh had given Israel all the land and commanded the destruction of its peoples, and so had feared for their lives. "Now we are in your hand; do as seems right to you." And Joshua spared them, and made them woodcutters and water-carriers for the congregation and for the altar of Yahweh.
