For years the reports had reached the queen of Sheba along the trade roads, carried north and south by merchants who had heard them from other merchants. There was a king in the west, they said. Solomon, of Israel. Wiser than any man alive. Rich past reckoning. And over all of it, the favor of the God of Israel, whose name he set on everything he built and every judgment he gave.

She did not believe it.

She had sat on her own throne long enough to know what happens to a story on the road. A true thing leaves a man's mouth, and it travels a thousand miles, and by the time it reaches you it has swelled to twice its size and lost its shape. Kings were always wiser in the telling than in the room. So the queen made up her mind. She would go to Jerusalem herself, and she would weigh this man with her own hand.

She came with a great caravan. A long line of camels stretched out behind her, bearing spices and gold and precious stones, more wealth than many kings would gather in a lifetime. But the heaviest thing she brought could not be loaded on any animal. She brought her questions. Hard ones. The riddles and the deep matters that had defeated the wise men of her own court, the knots no one in all her land had been able to loose. She had not come to admire him. She had come to find the bottom of him.

They received her in Jerusalem, and she sat down with Solomon, and she began.

She laid it all before him, everything that was in her heart. She held nothing back, and she spared him nothing. She gave him the questions she had carried so long that she had half stopped believing they had answers at all, and she watched his face as she asked.

He did not falter.

Solomon answered every one. Nothing was too hard for him. Nothing was hidden from him. Whatever she set before him, he took it up and opened it to her, clear and unhurried, the way a man explains a thing he has known since he was young. The riddles she had guarded like treasure, he turned over once in the light and handed back to her, solved. She asked another. He answered that one too. She asked again, reaching deeper, pressing for the place where his wisdom would finally run out.

It did not run out.

Day gave way to day, and still she questioned, and still he answered. Somewhere in those days the thing she had come to do quietly reversed itself. She had come to test him. Now she was the one being undone. Each answer landed and settled and left her less certain than the last, because each one was not only right. It was deeper than the question she had asked.

And then, at some point, the queen stopped asking. She simply looked.

She saw the house he had built, stone fitted to stone, rising clean against the sky. She saw the food laid out along the length of his table. She saw his servants, each one standing in his appointed place, and the men who waited on his cup, and the cut and the color of the robes they wore, and the order that held all of it together, the whole house moving like a single body that knew its own mind. And she watched the king rise from his place and go up by his own ascent to the house of the LORD.

The breath went out of her.

She stood there with nothing left. Every question was answered. Every doubt was spent. There was no more spirit in her — no more argument, no more guard, no more distance between what she had heard and what stood in front of her.

When her voice came back, it was not the voice of a queen testing a rival. It was lower than that, and truer.

"It was a true report," she said. "Everything I heard in my own land of your deeds and your wisdom was true. And still I did not believe it — not until I came here, and my own eyes had seen it." She shook her head slowly, as if to clear it. "The half was not told me. Your wisdom and your wealth run far past anything that ever reached my country."

She looked at the men gathered around him, standing each in his place. "Happy are these, your servants, who stand before you day after day, and hear your wisdom."

Then she lifted her eyes higher than the king, higher than the house, past all of it, to the One she could not see.

"Blessed be the LORD your God, who delighted in you, and set you on the throne of Israel. Because the LORD loved Israel forever, he has made you king, to do justice and righteousness."

She gave him a hundred and twenty talents of gold. She gave him spices in such abundance that no such store ever came into Israel again, and precious stones beyond counting. And Solomon, for his part, withheld nothing from her. He gave her everything she desired, whatever she asked, and more besides, out of the open bounty of his own hand.

So the gifts passed both ways between them, and the matter was finished.

Then the queen of Sheba turned and went back to her own land, she and her servants. The long line of camels moved east, out of the city and into the morning, and she rode among them carrying home a thing she had not packed and could not weigh — the knowledge that the half had not been told, and that it had all been true.
