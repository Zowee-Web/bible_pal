The high place at Gibeon stood on a hill above the town, the largest of the high places in the land. Solomon had gone up there with his retinue, and he offered a thousand burnt offerings upon the altar. The smoke rose thick and white through the day, lifting above the stone platform and drifting over the surrounding hills. The wood was stacked and lit and stacked again. The priests moved around the altar with their robes pulled close, their faces dark with soot. The fires burned through the afternoon and into the evening. The wood cracked and split in the heat. Ash drifted down onto the stones and gathered in the seams.

The hills around Gibeon went red, then gray, then dark. The town below pulled its shutters closed. Lamps came on in the houses one by one and then went out. Night came to Gibeon. The fires on the high place burned lower, the coals red and pulsing under a thin shell of ash. The stars appeared over the ridgeline. The air cooled. Solomon slept.

And the LORD appeared to him in a dream by night.

God said, "Ask what I shall give you."

Solomon answered. He did not rush. He spoke the way a man speaks when he is aware of who is listening.

"You have shown your servant David my father great mercy," he said, "according as he walked before you in truth and in righteousness and in uprightness of heart with you. And you have kept for him this great kindness, that you have given him a son to sit on his throne, as it is this day."

He continued.

"And now, O LORD my God, you have made your servant king instead of David my father. And I am but a little child. I do not know how to go out or come in."

The young king's words were plain and without decoration. He described what he saw around him.

"And your servant is in the midst of your people which you have chosen, a great people that cannot be numbered or counted for multitude."

Then he made his request. One request. He did not ask for a second thing.

"Give your servant an understanding heart to judge your people, that I may discern between good and bad. For who is able to judge this your so great a people?"

The speech pleased the Lord. It pleased him that Solomon had asked this thing.

God spoke again. The words came with the clarity of something decided, something already accomplished.

"Because you have asked this thing, and have not asked for yourself long life, neither have asked riches for yourself, nor have asked the life of your enemies, but have asked for yourself understanding to discern judgment — behold, I have done according to your words."

God said, "I have given you a wise and an understanding heart, so that there was none like you before you, neither after you shall any arise like you."

There was more. God gave beyond what had been requested.

"And I have also given you that which you have not asked, both riches and honor, so that there shall not be any among the kings like you all your days."

Then God set a condition. One condition, clear as the rest.

"And if you will walk in my ways, to keep my statutes and my commandments, as your father David did walk, then I will lengthen your days."

Solomon awoke. He was at Gibeon. The night air was on his face. The coals on the altar still glowed somewhere below the ridge, faint and red. The stars had moved. It was a dream.

He lay still for a while. Then he rose. He called his servants. The horses were brought and the men gathered, and in the early light he traveled from Gibeon to Jerusalem. The road descended from the high place and wound south through the hills. The dust was pale under the morning light. Goats moved on the slopes. A few farmers were already in their fields, bent over the stones at the edges of the terraces. His men walked with him. No one spoke much. The sun rose higher and the heat came down on the road.

When he reached Jerusalem, he went to the ark of the covenant of the LORD. The ark sat in the tent David had pitched for it. The flaps were drawn back and the inside was dim and cool after the road. Solomon stood before it. He waited a moment, looking at the ark, at the carved cherubim, at the poles set in the rings on its sides. Then he offered up burnt offerings on the altar there. The fire was kindled and the wood took, and the smoke rose inside the tent and curled toward the open sky above. He offered peace offerings as well. The fat was laid on the coals. It hissed and ran and burned. The scent of the roasting meat filled the space around the ark and drifted out through the tent flaps into the courtyard.

Then Solomon made a feast for all his servants. The word went out and the cooks set to work and the tables were set in the open courts. Bread was carried out in baskets. The meat from the offerings was brought on platters, still steaming. Wine was poured into cups and the cups passed down the tables. His officials came in from their rooms. His household gathered. Captains and stewards and scribes took their places. The servants sat and ate together in the presence of the king.

The sounds of the meal carried across the courtyard. Voices rose and overlapped. Cups clinked against cups. Men laughed and leaned toward one another and reached across the tables for the bread. A low hum of conversation settled over the courts, the hum of men eating together after an offering had been made. The afternoon sun came down over the walls and lit the white stones.

Solomon sat among them. The feast stretched before him, the plates full, the cups lifted, the servants gathered on every side.
