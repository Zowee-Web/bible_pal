For years the reports had reached the queen of Sheba along the trade roads, carried north and south by merchants who had heard them from other merchants. There was a king in the west, they said. Solomon, of Israel. Wiser than any man alive. Rich past reckoning. And over all of it, the favour of the God of Israel, whose name he set upon everything he built and every judgment he gave.

She did not believe it.

She had sat upon her own throne long enough to know what becomes of a story on the road. A true thing leaves a man's mouth, and it travels a thousand miles, and by the time it reaches a far country it has swelled to twice its size and lost its shape. Kings were always wiser in the telling than in the room. So the queen made up her mind. She would go to Jerusalem herself, and she would prove this man with her own hand.

She came with a very great train. A long line of camels stretched out behind her, bearing spices and much gold and precious stones, more wealth than many kings would gather in a lifetime. But the heaviest thing she brought could not be laid upon any animal. She brought her questions. Hard ones. The riddles and the deep matters that had defeated the wise men of her own court, the knots no one in all her land had been able to loose. She had not come to admire him. She had come to find the bottom of him.

They received her in Jerusalem, and she sat down with Solomon, and she began.

She communed with him of all that was in her heart. She held nothing back, and she spared him nothing. She gave him the questions she had carried so long that she had half stopped believing they had answers at all, and she watched his face as she asked.

He did not falter.

Solomon told her all her questions. Nothing was too hard for him. There was not any thing hid from the king which he told her not. Whatsoever she set before him, he took up and opened to her, clear and unhurried, as a man explains a thing he has known since he was young. The riddles she had guarded like treasure, he turned over once in the light and handed back to her, solved. She asked another. He answered that one too. She asked again, reaching deeper, pressing for the place where his wisdom would at last run out.

It did not run out.

Day gave way to day, and still she questioned, and still he answered. Somewhere in those days the thing she had come to do quietly reversed itself. She had come to prove him. Now she was the one being undone. Each answer landed and settled and left her less certain than the last, because each one was not only right. It was deeper than the question she had asked.

And then, at some point, the queen ceased her asking. She simply looked.

She saw the house that he had built, stone fitted to stone, rising clean against the sky. She saw the meat of his table along its whole length. She saw the sitting of his servants, each in his appointed place, and the attendance of his ministers, and his cupbearers, and the apparel that they wore, and the order that held all of it together, the whole house moving like a single body that knew its own mind. And she saw the ascent by which the king went up unto the house of the LORD.

There was no more spirit in her.

She stood there with nothing left. Every question was answered. Every doubt was spent. There was no more argument, no more guard, no more distance between what she had heard and what stood before her.

When her voice returned, it was not the voice of a queen proving a rival. It was lower than that, and truer.

"It was a true report," she said, "that I heard in mine own land of thy acts and of thy wisdom. Howbeit I believed not the words, until I came, and mine eyes had seen it. And behold, the half was not told me. Thy wisdom and prosperity exceedeth the fame which I heard."

She looked upon the men gathered about him, standing each in his place. "Happy are thy men, happy are these thy servants, which stand continually before thee, and that hear thy wisdom."

Then she lifted her eyes higher than the king, higher than the house, past all of it, to the One she could not see.

"Blessed be the LORD thy God, which delighted in thee, to set thee on the throne of Israel. Because the LORD loved Israel for ever, therefore made he thee king, to do judgment and justice."

She gave the king an hundred and twenty talents of gold. She gave him of spices very great store, such as never came into Israel again, and precious stones beyond counting. And Solomon, for his part, withheld nothing from her. He gave unto the queen of Sheba all her desire, whatsoever she asked, beside that which he gave her of his royal bounty.

So the gifts passed both ways between them, and the matter was finished.

Then the queen of Sheba turned and went unto her own country, she and her servants. The long train of camels moved east, out of the city and into the morning, and she rode among them carrying home a thing she had not packed and could not weigh — the knowledge that the half had not been told, and that it had all been true.
