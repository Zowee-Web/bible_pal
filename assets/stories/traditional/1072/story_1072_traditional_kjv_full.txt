The king of Aram was at war with Israel. He held councils with his officers in inner chambers behind heavy doors. Together they chose the places wherein they would set up camp and lay their snares.

But every time, the prophet Elisha sent a message unto the king of Israel. "Pass not by that place," Elisha warned, "for thither the Arameans are come down."

The king of Israel sent men to search. They found it was true. He kept his soldiers away, and the Aramean snares caught nothing. This came to pass not once nor twice but again and again.

The king of Aram waxed wroth. He paced before his officers, his robes sweeping the stone floor. "Tell me!" he demanded. "Which one of you doth betray our counsel unto the king of Israel?"

The chamber went quiet. The officers looked one to another.

One servant stepped forward. "None of us, my lord, O king. Elisha, the prophet that is in Israel, telleth the king of Israel the words which thou speakest in thy bedchamber."

The king's jaw tightened. "Go and spy where he is," he commanded, "that I may send and fetch him."

His scouts returned. "Behold, he is in Dothan," they reported.

Dothan was a small city set among rolling hills, the walls thereof low and pale in the sun. The king of Aram sent not a handful of soldiers. He sent horses. He sent chariots. He sent a great host. They marched through the dark hours of the night, the wheels grinding over the dry ground, the armour clinking softly. By the time the first grey light touched the sky, they had compassed the city round about.

Elisha's servant arose early that morning. He pushed open the door and stepped into the cool air. Then he stood still.

Soldiers everywhere. Horses stamping in lines along the way. Chariots set on every hill. Spear points catching the early light. The host wrapped about Dothan like a fist.

He stumbled back within, his breath coming fast. "Alas, my master!" he cried. "What shall we do?"

Elisha looked upon him. His voice was steady and unhurried. "Fear not: for they that be with us are more than they that be with them."

The servant blinked. He looked behind him. He looked at Elisha. It was but the two of them standing in that chamber, the morning light slanting through the window.

Elisha bowed his head. "Lord, I pray thee," he prayed, "open his eyes, that he may see."

And the Lord opened the eyes of the young man.

He looked out again at the hills round about Dothan. His hands gripped the doorframe. The slopes were covered. The mountain was full of horses and chariots of fire round about Elisha, bright as the sun on bronze, filling the spaces between the trees, stretching along the hilltops in every direction. They encompassed the Aramean army on every side.

The servant stood in the doorway, staring.

Then the Aramean soldiers began their advance. Their feet crunched on the gravel way. Their horses snorted and pulled forward.

Elisha prayed again. "Smite this people, I pray thee, with blindness."

And the Lord smote them. Every soldier, every horseman. They stumbled. They reached out with empty hands. They cried one to another, confused, turning in circles on the way.

Elisha went forth from the city unto them. Dust rose about his sandals. He stood before the smitten host.

"This is not the way," he said unto them, his voice clear and plain. "Neither is this the city. Follow me, and I will bring you unto the man whom ye seek."

The soldiers turned toward his voice. They followed. The whole host — horses, chariots, footmen — followed the prophet's footsteps along the dirt way. They walked for many miles, through open country, past fields and low stone walls. The sun rose higher and warmed their backs.

Elisha led them all the way to Samaria, the chief city of Israel. He brought them through the gates, into the heart of the city, where the walls rose high on every side and the king of Israel's own men of war stood armed and watchful along the ramparts.

When they were well within, Elisha prayed once more. "Lord, open the eyes of these men, that they may see."

The Lord opened their eyes. The soldiers blinked. They looked round about. Stone walls. Armed guards. The palace of the king of Israel rising before them. They were standing in the midst of Samaria, compassed about.

The king of Israel beheld the whole Aramean host standing helpless in his city. He ran unto Elisha, scarce able to contain himself. "My father, shall I smite them?" he asked. "Shall I smite them?"

"Thou shalt not smite them," Elisha said. "Wouldest thou smite those whom thou hast taken captive with thy sword and with thy bow? Set bread and water before them, that they may eat and drink, and let them go unto their master."

The king obeyed. He commanded a great feast to be prepared. Servants brought forth bread and meat and pitchers of water. The Aramean soldiers sat down upon the ground in the open square of Samaria. They did eat. They did drink. The bread was warm and the water cool.

When they had finished, they arose and brushed the crumbs from their garments. The king of Israel sent them away, back unto the king of Aram.

After this, the bands of Aram came no more into the land of Israel.
