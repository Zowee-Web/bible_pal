The king of Aram was at war with Israel. He held war councils in the inner rooms of his palace, behind walls of thick stone, where no outside ear could hear. Oil lamps flickered on the table as he leaned over maps with his officers. Together they chose the places where they would set up camp and lay their traps — a valley here, a crossroads there.

But every time the king made his plans, the prophet Elisha sent a message to the king of Israel. "Beware of passing that place," Elisha warned, "because the Arameans are going down there."

The king of Israel sent scouts to check. They returned with the same report each time — soldiers waiting, just where Elisha had said. The king of Israel kept his men away, and the Aramean traps closed on nothing but wind and dust.

This happened not once. Not twice. It happened again and again.

The king of Aram grew furious. He summoned his officers to the council room. The lamps were lit. The heavy door was shut. His face was dark as a storm cloud.

"Tell me!" he demanded. His voice rang off the stone walls. "Which one of you is giving away our plans to the king of Israel?"

Silence. The officers looked at one another. No one moved.

Then one of his servants stepped forward and bowed low. "None of us, my lord the king," he said. "It is Elisha, the prophet who is in Israel. He tells the king of Israel the very words you speak in your bedroom."

The king stared at the servant. He gripped the edge of the table. "Go and find out where he is," he ordered, "so I can send men to capture him."

Scouts went out. They traveled through the hill country, asking quiet questions in the villages. They came back with their answer.

"He is in Dothan."

Dothan was a small city set among rolling, grassy hills. Its walls were low and pale, baked by years of sun. Fig trees grew along the paths outside the gates. It was not a fortress. It was not built for war.

The king of Aram did not send a small patrol. He did not send a squad of ten men. He sent horses. He sent chariots. He sent a great army — hundreds of soldiers armed with swords and spears, their shields strapped tight. They left in the last hours of daylight and marched through the night. The wheels of the chariots ground over dry earth. The horses' hooves drummed steadily on the road. Metal clinked against metal in the darkness.

By the time the first thin gray light spread along the horizon, the army had reached Dothan. They fanned out around the city, filling every hill, blocking every road. The circle was complete. Not even a rabbit could have slipped through.

Inside the city, Elisha's servant woke early. The air was still cool. Birds had just begun to stir in the branches overhead. He pushed open the wooden door and stepped outside, stretching his arms.

Then he stopped.

His arms dropped to his sides. His breath caught in his throat.

Soldiers. Everywhere. Lines and lines of them standing on the hills in the pale morning light. Horses stamping and snorting, their breath rising in little clouds. Chariots parked wheel to wheel along every ridge. Spear points glinted in rows like a field of metal thorns. The entire city was wrapped in iron and leather and dust.

The servant turned and ran back inside. His sandals slapped the stone floor. His face was white.

"Oh, my master!" he cried out to Elisha. His voice cracked with fear. "What shall we do?"

Elisha was sitting on a low stool. He looked up at his servant. His face was calm. His hands rested on his knees. He did not rise. He did not rush to the door.

"Don't be afraid," Elisha said. His voice was even and steady, the way a father speaks to a child who has woken from a bad dream. "Those who are with us are more than those who are with them."

The servant stared at him. He looked over his shoulder at the open door, where the morning light poured in and the sounds of horses and clanking armor drifted on the breeze. He looked back at Elisha. It was just the two of them in that small room. Two men. No swords. No shields. No army.

Elisha bowed his head and closed his eyes. "O Lord," he prayed, "open his eyes so he may see."

And the Lord opened the servant's eyes.

The servant turned toward the door again. He walked to it slowly. He gripped the wooden frame and looked out.

The hills around Dothan had changed.

Between the Aramean soldiers and the city walls, covering every slope and every ridge, stood horses and chariots of fire. They blazed with light — not the soft yellow of oil lamps, but a fierce, clean brightness, like the heart of a furnace or the center of a lightning strike. The horses were tall and still, their manes streaming with flame. The chariots gleamed. Figures stood in them, silent, facing outward. They filled the hills on every side — north, south, east, and west — row after row after row, stretching back beyond where the eye could follow, surrounding the Aramean army completely.

The servant's knuckles were white on the doorframe. His mouth hung open. He could not speak.

Then the Aramean soldiers began their advance. Officers shouted commands. Boots crunched on gravel. The horses surged forward. The army moved toward Dothan like a wave.

Elisha prayed once more, his head still bowed. "Strike this army with blindness."

And the Lord struck them blind.

The advance crumbled. Soldiers stopped mid-step, arms outstretched, grasping at nothing. Horses veered sideways. Men bumped into one another, calling out in confusion. Officers shouted orders, but no one could see which way to go. The great army that had surrounded Dothan stumbled and staggered on the open hillside like men lost in a fog.

Elisha stood. He walked to the door, stepped over the threshold, and made his way down through the city gate. His sandals raised small puffs of dust on the road. The morning sun was warm now.

He walked straight to the blinded army. Soldiers turned their heads at the sound of his footsteps. They reached toward the sound with uncertain hands.

Elisha spoke in a clear, plain voice. "This is not the right road," he said. "And this is not the right city. Follow me, and I will lead you to the man you are looking for."

His voice was the only thing they could follow. So they did. The whole army — soldiers and officers, horses and chariots — turned and followed the sound of the prophet's footsteps.

He led them along the road, away from Dothan. The land opened up into wide fields. Low stone walls lined the path. Wild grass brushed against the soldiers' legs as they walked. The sun climbed higher and warmed their shoulders. No one spoke. The only sounds were footsteps, the jingle of bridles, and the creak of chariot wheels on dirt.

They walked for miles. Past orchards of olive trees. Past shepherds' fields dotted with stones. Through shallow valleys where the air smelled of dry sage. The blind army followed, step after careful step, trusting the voice of a man they had come to capture.

Elisha led them all the way to Samaria.

Samaria was the capital city of Israel. Its walls were high and thick. Its gates were broad. The king of Israel's own soldiers stood along the ramparts, armed and watchful. When the guards saw the prophet approaching with the entire Aramean army shuffling behind him, they gripped their spears and stared.

Elisha brought the army through the gates. The soldiers' boots echoed off the stone pavement inside the walls. The chariots rolled in one by one. When the last horse had passed through, the gates stayed open behind them.

The whole army now stood in the wide central square of Samaria. High walls on every side. Armed Israelite soldiers looking down from above.

Elisha prayed one final time. "Lord, open the eyes of these men so they may see."

The Lord opened their eyes.

The soldiers blinked. Light flooded in. They looked left. Stone walls. They looked right. Armed guards. They looked up. Spear points lined the ramparts above them. They looked at one another. Slowly, the understanding crept across their faces. They were standing in the heart of the enemy's capital city, completely surrounded, completely unarmed in every way that mattered.

The king of Israel saw them. He came running down the palace steps, his robes gathered in his fists, barely able to contain the excitement rising in his chest. He reached Elisha, breathing hard.

"My father!" he said. "Shall I kill them? Shall I kill them?"

Elisha looked at the king. "Do not kill them," he said. "Would you kill those you have captured with your own sword and bow? Set food and water before them so that they may eat and drink, and then send them back to their master."

The king paused. Then he obeyed.

He gave the order, and servants hurried out from the palace kitchens. They carried bread — flat loaves, still warm — and roasted meat on wide wooden platters. They brought clay jars of cool water and set them on the ground. More food came. Bowls of grain. Dried figs pressed into cakes. It was not a small meal. It was a great feast, spread across the open square of Samaria.

The Aramean soldiers sat down. They ate the warm bread. They drank the water. They chewed the figs and the roasted meat. Crumbs gathered on their laps. Their jaws worked slowly. No one spoke much. The Israelite soldiers watched from the walls above.

When the meal was finished, the Aramean soldiers rose. They brushed the dust and crumbs from their clothes. The king of Israel sent them away, back through the gates, back down the long road toward the land of Aram, back to their own king.

After that, the raiding bands from Aram stopped coming into the land of Israel.
