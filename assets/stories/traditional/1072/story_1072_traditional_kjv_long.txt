The king of Aram was at war with Israel. He held councils of war in the inner chambers of his palace, behind walls of thick stone, where no outside ear could hear. Oil lamps flickered upon the table as he leaned over maps with his officers. Together they chose the places wherein they would set up camp and lay their snares — a valley here, a crossway there.

But every time the king made his counsel, the prophet Elisha sent a message unto the king of Israel. "Beware that thou pass not by such a place," Elisha warned, "for thither the Arameans are come down."

The king of Israel sent scouts to search. They returned with the same report each time — soldiers waiting, even where Elisha had said. The king of Israel kept his men away, and the Aramean snares closed on nothing but wind and dust.

This came to pass not once. Not twice. It came to pass again and again.

The king of Aram waxed wroth. He summoned his officers to the council chamber. The lamps were lit. The heavy door was shut. His countenance was dark as a storm cloud.

"Tell me!" he demanded. His voice rang off the stone walls. "Which one of you doth betray our counsel unto the king of Israel?"

Silence. The officers looked one to another. No man moved.

Then one of his servants stepped forward and bowed himself low. "None of us, my lord, O king," he said. "Elisha, the prophet that is in Israel, telleth the king of Israel the very words which thou speakest in thy bedchamber."

The king stared upon the servant. He gripped the edge of the table. "Go and spy where he is," he commanded, "that I may send and fetch him."

Scouts went forth. They journeyed through the hill country, asking quiet questions in the villages. They came back with their answer.

"Behold, he is in Dothan."

Dothan was a small city set among rolling, grassy hills. The walls thereof were low and pale, baked by years of sun. Fig trees grew along the paths without the gates. It was not a stronghold. It was not built for war.

The king of Aram sent not a small company. He sent not a band of ten men. He sent horses. He sent chariots. He sent a great host — hundreds of soldiers armed with swords and spears, their shields strapped tight. They departed in the last hours of daylight and marched through the night. The wheels of the chariots ground over dry earth. The hooves of the horses drummed steadily upon the way. Metal clinked against metal in the darkness.

By the time the first thin grey light spread along the horizon, the host had reached Dothan. They fanned out around the city, filling every hill, blocking every road. The circle was complete. Not even a coney could have slipped through.

Within the city, Elisha's servant awoke early. The air was still cool. Birds had just begun to stir in the branches overhead. He pushed open the wooden door and stepped without, stretching his arms.

Then he stood still.

His arms fell to his sides. His breath caught in his throat.

Soldiers. Everywhere. Lines and lines of them standing upon the hills in the pale morning light. Horses stamping and snorting, their breath rising in little clouds. Chariots set wheel to wheel along every ridge. Spear points glinted in rows like a field of metal thorns. The whole city was wrapped in iron and leather and dust.

The servant turned and ran back within. His sandals slapped the stone floor. His face was white.

"Alas, my master!" he cried unto Elisha. His voice cracked with fear. "What shall we do?"

Elisha was sitting upon a low stool. He looked up at his servant. His countenance was calm. His hands rested upon his knees. He arose not. He hastened not unto the door.

"Fear not," Elisha said. His voice was even and steady, the way a father speaketh unto a child who hath awakened from a troubling dream. "They that be with us are more than they that be with them."

The servant stared upon him. He looked over his shoulder at the open door, whereat the morning light poured in and the sounds of horses and clanking armour drifted on the breeze. He looked back unto Elisha. It was but the two of them in that small chamber. Two men. No swords. No shields. No host.

Elisha bowed his head and closed his eyes. "Lord, I pray thee," he prayed, "open his eyes, that he may see."

And the Lord opened the eyes of the young man.

The servant turned toward the door again. He walked unto it slowly. He gripped the wooden frame and looked out.

The hills round about Dothan were changed.

Between the Aramean soldiers and the city walls, covering every slope and every ridge, the mountain was full of horses and chariots of fire round about Elisha. They blazed with light — not the soft yellow of oil lamps, but a fierce, clean brightness, like the heart of a furnace or the centre of a lightning bolt. The horses were tall and still, their manes streaming with flame. The chariots gleamed. Figures stood within them, silent, facing outward. They filled the hills on every side — north, south, east, and west — row after row after row, stretching back beyond where the eye could follow, encompassing the Aramean army completely.

The servant's knuckles were white upon the doorframe. His mouth hung open. He could not speak.

Then the Aramean soldiers began their advance. Officers shouted commands. Boots crunched on gravel. The horses surged forward. The host moved toward Dothan like a wave.

Elisha prayed once more, his head still bowed. "Smite this people, I pray thee, with blindness."

And the Lord smote them with blindness.

The advance crumbled. Soldiers stopped mid-step, arms outstretched, grasping at nothing. Horses veered sideways. Men bumped one against another, crying out in confusion. Officers shouted orders, but no man could see which way to go. The great host that had compassed Dothan stumbled and staggered on the open hillside as men lost in a mist.

Elisha arose. He walked unto the door, stepped over the threshold, and made his way down through the city gate. His sandals raised small clouds of dust upon the way. The morning sun was warm now.

He went straight unto the smitten host. Soldiers turned their heads at the sound of his footsteps. They reached toward the sound with uncertain hands.

Elisha spake in a clear, plain voice. "This is not the way," he said. "Neither is this the city. Follow me, and I will bring you unto the man whom ye seek."

His voice was the only thing they could follow. So they did. The whole host — soldiers and officers, horses and chariots — turned and followed the sound of the prophet's footsteps.

He led them along the way, away from Dothan. The land opened up into wide fields. Low stone walls lined the path. Wild grass brushed against the soldiers' legs as they walked. The sun climbed higher and warmed their shoulders. No man spake. The only sounds were footsteps, the jingle of bridles, and the creak of chariot wheels upon dirt.

They walked for many miles. Past oliveyards. Past shepherds' fields dotted with stones. Through shallow valleys wherein the air bore the savour of dry sage. The blind host followed, step after careful step, trusting the voice of a man whom they had come to take.

Elisha led them all the way to Samaria.

Samaria was the chief city of Israel. The walls thereof were high and thick. The gates were broad. The king of Israel's own men of war stood along the ramparts, armed and watchful. When the guards beheld the prophet drawing near with the whole Aramean host shuffling behind him, they gripped their spears and stared.

Elisha brought the host through the gates. The soldiers' feet echoed off the stone pavement within the walls. The chariots rolled in one by one. When the last horse had passed through, the gates stayed open behind them.

The whole host now stood in the wide central square of Samaria. High walls on every side. Armed men of Israel looking down from above.

Elisha prayed one final time. "Lord, open the eyes of these men, that they may see."

The Lord opened their eyes. The soldiers blinked. Light flooded in. They looked to the left. Stone walls. They looked to the right. Armed guards. They looked up. Spear points lined the ramparts above them. They looked one to another. Slowly, the understanding crept across their countenances. They were standing in the heart of the enemy's chief city, encompassed completely, unarmed in every way that mattered.

The king of Israel beheld them. He came running down the palace steps, his robes gathered in his fists, scarce able to contain the gladness rising in his breast. He came unto Elisha, breathing hard.

"My father!" he said. "Shall I smite them? Shall I smite them?"

Elisha looked upon the king. "Thou shalt not smite them," he said. "Wouldest thou smite those whom thou hast taken captive with thy sword and with thy bow? Set bread and water before them, that they may eat and drink, and then send them back unto their master."

The king paused. Then he obeyed.

He gave the commandment, and servants hastened out from the palace kitchens. They carried bread — flat loaves, still warm — and roasted meat on wide wooden platters. They brought pitchers of cool water and set them upon the ground. More food came. Bowls of grain. Dried figs pressed into cakes. It was no small meal. It was a great feast, spread across the open square of Samaria.

The Aramean soldiers sat down. They did eat the warm bread. They drank the water. They chewed the figs and the roasted meat. Crumbs gathered on their laps. Their jaws worked slowly. No man spake much. The men of Israel watched from the walls above.

When the meal was finished, the Aramean soldiers arose. They brushed the dust and crumbs from their garments. The king of Israel sent them away, back through the gates, back down the long road toward the land of Aram, back unto their own king.

After this, the bands of Aram came no more into the land of Israel.
