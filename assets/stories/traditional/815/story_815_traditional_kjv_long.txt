It was a calm afternoon, and the sun was slowly sliding down in the sky. The light turned soft and golden over the hills and roads near Jerusalem. The air was warm and still, and long shadows stretched across the quiet countryside. Upon one of the dusty roads, two men walked together, going toward the village called Emmaus. The one was called Cleopas, and the other was his friend. Their sandals shuffled through the dust, leaving gentle prints as they went. The world felt slow and quiet after the busy days within the city.

Their hearts were heavy. They talked quietly as they walked, speaking of all the things which had come to pass in Jerusalem. They remembered the great teacher, Jesus, whom they had loved. They remembered following him, hearing his kind words, and beholding his wondrous miracles. But now all things seemed lost and confounding. Their hopes seemed small, and questions filled their minds. The gentle sound of their voices moved with them as they spake of their sadness and wonder.

And it came to pass, while they yet were on the road, that Jesus himself drew near, and went with them. But their eyes were holden that they should not know him. As the three walked together, the new traveller hearkened unto the talk. He said unto them, "What manner of communications are these that ye have one to another, as ye walk, and are sad?" This question made the two friends to pause for a moment. They stood still, and their faces shewed their sorrow. Cleopas answered, "Art thou only a stranger in Jerusalem, and hast not known the things which are come to pass there in these days?"

The stranger looked upon them and said, "What things?" The two friends began to pour forth that which lay heavy upon their hearts. They spake of Jesus, which was a prophet mighty in deed and word before God and all the people. They told him how their chief priests and rulers had delivered Jesus up, and how the things they had hoped for seemed to be vanished away. "But we trusted that it had been he which should have redeemed Israel: and beside all this, to day is the third day since these things were done," they added. Their sadness spilled out with every word.

Then Cleopas spake of a strange thing which had come to pass earlier that morning. Certain women, which were of their company, had gone early unto the sepulchre of Jesus. They found not his body there. The women returned, saying that they had seen a vision of angels, which said that he was alive. Certain of the men also went unto the sepulchre, and found it even as the women had said: but him they saw not. The road seemed even more silent as Cleopas made an end of speaking, and the air was full of wonder and confusion.

The stranger, yet walking with them, began to speak. His voice was gentle but strong. He said, "O fools, and slow of heart to believe all that the prophets have spoken: Ought not Christ to have suffered these things, and to enter into his glory?" The two friends hearkened earnestly. And beginning at Moses and all the prophets, he expounded unto them in all the scriptures the things concerning himself. With every step his words brought new hope. The dusty road seemed less heavy as they heard the stories of how God ever kept his promises.

The sun was lower in the sky, and the little company at length drew nigh unto the village called Emmaus. The two friends would not that this stranger should leave them. They felt a deep comfort and warmth when he spake. The day was ending, and the dusk was beginning to spread its blue shadows across the fields. So they constrained him, saying, "Abide with us: for it is toward evening, and the day is far spent." And the traveller went in with them.

Within the little house, a soft lamp was lit. It filled the room with a gentle, golden glow. The table was made ready for supper. The three sat down together to eat. The warm savour of fresh bread mingled with the rest of the simple meal. The stranger took bread, and blessed it, and brake, and gave to them. And in that very moment their eyes were opened, and they knew him. The stranger which had walked with them, taught them, and shared their table—he was Jesus! Of a sudden, they knew him. All things were made plain. Their sorrow was turned into joy and amazement.

But as soon as they knew him, Jesus vanished out of their sight. The room was quiet for a moment. The friends looked one upon another, their eyes shining with wonder. They said one to another, "Did not our heart burn within us, while he talked with us by the way, and while he opened to us the scriptures?" Joy welled up within them. The deep hope which had been gone now returned, stronger than before.

Though it was late and the road was long, Cleopas and his friend rose up the same hour. They were no longer weary. Their steps were light as they hasted back unto Jerusalem, eager to tell that which had come to pass. When they came unto the city through the dark and quiet streets, they found the other disciples gathered together. The room was busy with voices and gladness. Doors opened, warm light spilled out, and the friends shared that which they had seen.

Within, the other disciples greeted them with shining eyes and hope. One said, "The Lord is risen indeed, and hath appeared to Simon!" Cleopas and his friend told all things which had come to pass on the road, and how they had known Jesus at last in the breaking of bread. The wonder and the joy in the room grew and grew. None felt afraid nor alone. Every heart was filled with hope. The story of the walk unto Emmaus became a bright day in their lives, putting them in remembrance that Jesus was alive indeed, and that they might find him even in the most unlooked-for company.

And so, upon that road and within that little house, the friends knew that howsoever sad or confounded they were, Jesus could draw nigh. He would walk with them, talk with them, and fill their hearts with hope again. As the night grew still without, the disciples abode together, safe and comforted by the light of Jesus' presence, even as the scriptures had promised.

(Luke 24:13-35, KJV)

As the hours slipped by, soft voices filled the small room. Their laughter was quiet, their words gentle with wonder. Some spake of remembrances, while others sat in the peace which now filled their hearts. They remembered how easily hope had been lost, and how swiftly Jesus could bring it again. One put an arm about a shoulder, and another brake yet another loaf of bread. None desired the night to end. The golden lamp flickered, and without, the darkness felt less empty than before. The men and women knew that withersoever they should go from that hour, they would bear this new hope with them. Though the road were long, they would remember the quiet breaking of bread and the warmth within their hearts.
