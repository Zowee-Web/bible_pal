A long time without rain had turned the whole land brown and cracked. Streams dried up. Fields turned to dust. King Ahab ruled Israel, but the people had ceased from following God. Instead, they bowed themselves down to a false god called Baal.

The prophet Elijah sent word unto King Ahab. "Gather all Israel unto Mount Carmel," he said. "Bring the four hundred and fifty prophets of Baal."

Ahab sent the message throughout the land. The people came from every direction, climbing the rocky slopes of Mount Carmel. The prophets of Baal arrived in a great multitude — four hundred and fifty of them. King Ahab came with his servants and men of war. The wind blew across the mountaintop, and every man waited.

Elijah stood before them all. One man only, facing the whole multitude. "How long halt ye between two opinions?" he said. "If the Lord be God, follow him: but if Baal, then follow him."

The people answered him not a word. Not a single voice was lifted.

Elijah spake again. "I, even I only, remain a prophet of the Lord. But Baal's prophets are four hundred and fifty men." He told them the manner. Two bullocks should be brought. Baal's prophets would choose one, cut it in pieces, and lay it upon their altar of wood. Elijah would do likewise. But no man would put fire under.

"And call ye on the name of your gods," Elijah said, "and I will call on the name of the Lord: and the God that answereth by fire, let him be God."

And all the people answered and said, "It is well spoken."

The prophets of Baal went first. They chose their bullock, dressed it, and laid it upon the wood. Then they began to call upon Baal. "O Baal, hear us!" they cried.

They called and called. They leaped upon the altar, stamping their feet upon the dry ground. Morning stretched toward noon. No voice answered. No fire came. The wood lay cold. The altar sat still.

At noon, Elijah mocked them. "Cry aloud," he said. "For he is a god. Either he is talking, or he is pursuing, or he is in a journey, or peradventure he sleepeth, and must be awaked."

So they cried aloud, louder than before. They leaped wildly upon their altar. They cried out until their voices were spent and the afternoon shadows grew long. But there was no voice, nor any to answer, nor any that regarded.

The altar of Baal sat cold and silent on the mountaintop.

Then Elijah said unto the people, "Come near unto me." And all the people came near unto him.

He found the altar of the Lord that was broken down. Stone by stone he repaired it, using twelve stones — one for each of the twelve tribes of Israel. He set the stones in place with his own hands.

About the base of the altar he made a trench, deep enough to hold a good measure of seed. He laid the wood in order upon the stones. He cut the bullock in pieces and laid the pieces carefully on the wood.

Then he did that which no man looked for.

"Fill four barrels with water," Elijah said. "And pour it on the burnt sacrifice, and on the wood."

Servants carried the barrels and poured. Water splashed across the meat and soaked into the wood.

"Do it the second time," Elijah said.

They poured the second time. Water ran down the sides of the altar in streams.

"Do it the third time."

They poured the third time. Water flooded everything. It ran off the stones and filled the trench round about the altar until the trench was full unto the brim. The wood was drenched. The offering glistened. The ground round about the altar turned to mire.

The multitude watched in silence. Four hundred and fifty prophets of Baal stood on one side with their cold, dry altar. Elijah stood alone beside his altar, which was dripping wet.

Elijah came near. The afternoon light fell across the mountain. He lifted his voice and prayed.

"Lord God of Abraham, Isaac, and of Israel," he said, "let it be known this day that thou art God in Israel, and that I am thy servant, and that I have done all these things at thy word. Hear me, O Lord, hear me, that this people may know that thou art the Lord God."

Then the fire of the Lord fell.

It struck the altar. It consumed the burnt sacrifice. It consumed the wood. It consumed the twelve stones. It consumed the dust beneath them. It licked up the water that was in the trench.

The heat swept outward. The people stumbled back. Then every one of them — men, women, children, hundreds upon hundreds — fell on their faces to the ground.

"The Lord, he is the God!" they cried. "The Lord, he is the God!"

Their voices echoed across Mount Carmel, over the dry brown valley, into the empty sky.
