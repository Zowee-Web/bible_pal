A long time without rain had turned the whole land brown and cracked. Streams dried up. Fields turned to dust. King Ahab ruled Israel, but the people had stopped following God. Instead, they bowed down to a pretend god called Baal.

The prophet Elijah sent word to King Ahab. "Gather all of Israel to Mount Carmel," he said. "Bring the four hundred and fifty prophets of Baal."

Ahab sent the message throughout the land. The people came from every direction, climbing the rocky slopes of Mount Carmel. The prophets of Baal arrived in a great crowd — four hundred and fifty of them. King Ahab came with his servants and soldiers. The wind blew across the mountaintop, and everyone waited.

Elijah stood before them all. Just one man, facing the entire crowd. "How long will you go back and forth between two sides?" he said. "If the Lord is God, follow Him. If Baal is god, follow him."

The people said nothing. Not a single voice answered.

Elijah spoke again. "I am the only prophet of the Lord left here — just me, alone. But Baal has four hundred and fifty prophets." He told them the plan. Two bulls would be brought. Baal's prophets would choose one, cut it up, and lay it on their altar of wood. Elijah would do the same. But no one would light a fire underneath.

"Then you call on the name of your god," Elijah said, "and I will call on the name of the Lord. The god who answers by fire — he is God."

All the people agreed. "That is a good plan," they said.

The prophets of Baal went first. They chose their bull, prepared it, and laid it on the wood. Then they began to call out to Baal. "O Baal, answer us!" they shouted.

They called and called. They danced around the altar, stamping their feet on the dry ground. Morning stretched toward noon. No voice answered. No fire came. The wood stayed cold. The altar sat still.

At noon, Elijah spoke to them. "Shout louder," he said. "Surely he is a god. Perhaps he is deep in thought. Perhaps he is busy, or traveling. Maybe he is sleeping and must be awakened."

So they shouted louder than before. They leaped wildly around their altar. They cried out until their voices went hoarse and the afternoon shadows grew long. But there was no response. No one answered. No one paid attention.

The altar of Baal sat cold and silent on the mountaintop.

Then Elijah said to the people, "Come near to me." And they came close.

He found the old altar of the Lord that had been broken down. Stone by stone he rebuilt it, using twelve stones — one for each of the twelve tribes of Israel. He set the stones in place with his own hands.

Around the base of the altar he dug a wide trench, deep enough to hold a good measure of seed. He arranged the wood on top of the stones. He cut the bull in pieces and laid the pieces carefully on the wood.

Then he did something no one expected.

"Fill four large jars with water," Elijah said. "Pour it over the offering and over the wood."

Servants carried the jars and poured. Water splashed across the meat and soaked into the wood.

"Do it again," Elijah said.

They poured a second time. Water ran down the sides of the altar in streams.

"Do it a third time."

They poured a third time. Water flooded everything. It ran off the stones and filled the trench around the altar until the trench was full to the brim. The wood was drenched. The offering glistened. The ground around the altar turned to mud.

The crowd watched in silence. Four hundred and fifty prophets of Baal stood on one side with their cold, dry altar. Elijah stood alone beside his altar, which was dripping wet.

Elijah stepped forward. The afternoon light fell across the mountain. He lifted his voice and prayed.

"Lord, God of Abraham, Isaac, and Israel," he said, "let it be known today that You are God in Israel, and that I am Your servant, and that I have done all these things at Your word. Answer me, Lord. Answer me, so these people will know that You, Lord, are God."

Then fire fell from heaven.

It struck the altar. It burned the offering. It burned the wood. It burned the twelve stones. It burned the dust beneath them. It licked up every last drop of water standing in the trench.

The heat swept outward. The people stumbled back. Then every one of them — men, women, children, hundreds upon hundreds — fell facedown on the ground.

"The Lord, He is God!" they cried out. "The Lord, He is God!"

Their voices echoed across Mount Carmel, over the dry brown valley, into the empty sky.
