A long time without rain had turned the whole land brown and cracked. Streams dried to nothing but stones and mire. Fields that once grew tall with grain now lay flat and dusty. Beasts wandered seeking water. The sky was a hard, pale blue, day after day, with no cloud.

King Ahab ruled Israel in those days. But the people had ceased from following the Lord their God. Instead they bowed themselves down to a false god called Baal. They built altars unto Baal. They offered sacrifices unto Baal. And the old altars of the Lord lay broken and forgotten.

Into this dry and troubled time, the prophet Elijah carried a message. He sent word unto King Ahab: gather all Israel unto Mount Carmel. Bring the four hundred and fifty prophets of Baal.

Ahab sent messengers throughout the land. The word spread from village to village, from town to town.

And they came.

People walked the dusty ways toward the mountain. Families came in groups. Shepherds left their flocks. Husbandmen left their empty fields. They climbed the rocky slopes of Mount Carmel, picking their way between boulders and dry brush. The wind bore the savour of dust and dried grass.

The four hundred and fifty prophets of Baal arrived in a great multitude, their robes swaying as they walked. King Ahab came with his servants and men of war, his chariot wheels grinding against the gravel. By the time the sun was high, hundreds and hundreds of people stood gathered on the mountaintop. The ground was packed with sandals and bare feet. Children sat upon their parents' shoulders to see over the crowd.

And there was Elijah. One man only. Standing before them all.

He lifted up his voice so that every man could hear. "How long halt ye between two opinions?" he said. "If the Lord be God, follow him: but if Baal, then follow him."

The people answered him not a word. Not a whisper. The wind moved across the hilltop and the silence held.

Elijah went on. "I, even I only, remain a prophet of the Lord. I alone. But Baal's prophets are four hundred and fifty men." He let those numbers settle over the multitude. One against four hundred and fifty.

Then he told them the manner. Two bullocks should be brought. The prophets of Baal would choose one bullock, cut it in pieces, and lay it upon their altar on top of wood. Elijah would do the same with the second bullock on another altar. But neither side would put fire under.

"And call ye on the name of your gods," Elijah said, "and I will call on the name of the Lord: and the God that answereth by fire, let him be God."

And all the people answered together. "It is well spoken," they said.

Elijah turned unto the prophets of Baal. "Choose ye one bullock for yourselves," he said. "For ye are many. Dress it. Call on the name of your gods. But put no fire under."

The prophets of Baal chose their bullock. They killed it and cut it in pieces, laying the meat across the wood on their altar. The multitude stepped back and beheld.

Then the prophets of Baal began to call. "O Baal, hear us!" they cried.

They called and called. Their voices echoed off the rocks. They went round about the altar, stamping their feet upon the hard ground. Some lifted their hands toward the sky. Some bowed low. They called Baal's name over and over.

An hour passed. The sun climbed higher. Two hours passed. The morning stretched on toward noon. The multitude shifted on weary feet. Children tugged at their parents' garments. Still the prophets of Baal called out.

No voice answered. No fire came. The wood lay still upon the altar, cold and dry.

At noon, Elijah mocked them. His voice was calm and steady. "Cry aloud," he said. "For he is a god. Either he is talking, or he is pursuing, or he is in a journey, or peradventure he sleepeth, and must be awaked."

So the prophets of Baal cried aloud. They leaped and jumped about the altar. They cried out until their throats were raw. They kept on through the heat of the afternoon, when the sun beat down upon the mountaintop and sweat ran down every face.

Nothing came to pass. No voice. No fire. None that answered.

The altar of Baal sat cold and quiet on the mountainside. The wood stirred not. The offering moved not.

The afternoon light began to shift. Shadows stretched longer across the stones. The prophets of Baal at last grew still, their chests heaving, their voices spent.

Then Elijah said unto the people, "Come near unto me."

The multitude moved closer. Hundreds of them pressed forward, craning their necks, stepping over rocks to see.

Elijah walked unto an old altar — the altar of the Lord, which was broken down and scattered. Stones lay tumbled in the dirt. Weeds grew between them. He knelt and began to work, lifting each stone and setting it back in place. One stone. Two stones. Twelve stones in all, according to the number of the tribes of the sons of Jacob, unto whom the word of the Lord came, saying, "Israel shall be thy name."

He set the last stone carefully and stood back. The altar was whole again.

About the base he made a trench with his hands, scooping out the dry earth, deep enough to contain two measures of seed. Dust rose about his fingers as he wrought.

He laid the wood in order upon the stones, piece by piece. He cut the bullock and laid the pieces upon the wood. Everything was set in order.

Then Elijah did a thing very strange.

"Fill four barrels with water," he said. "And pour it on the burnt sacrifice, and on the wood."

Servants carried the heavy barrels forward. They tipped them and water came down, splashing over the meat and soaking deep into the wood. It ran down the sides of the stones in dark streams.

"Do it the second time," Elijah said.

They filled the barrels a second time and poured. More water splashed across the altar. The wood was soaked through now, dark and dripping.

"Do it the third time."

They poured a third time. Water ran every where. It flooded down the stones, pooled in the dirt, and filled the trench all the way round about the altar until it brimmed like a little moat. The ground turned to mire. The offering glistened. Twelve barrels of water — and not a dry place anywhere on that altar.

The multitude stared. Some whispered one to another. Over on the other side, the four hundred and fifty prophets of Baal stood near their cold, silent altar. And here was Elijah, alone, standing next to an altar so drenched with water that it seemed not possible that anything could burn.

The afternoon sun hung low. The light turned golden across the mountaintop.

Elijah came near. He cried not aloud. He leaped not, neither did he dance. He stood still and lifted up his voice in prayer.

"Lord God of Abraham, Isaac, and of Israel," he said. "Let it be known this day that thou art God in Israel, and that I am thy servant, and that I have done all these things at thy word."

He paused. The wind shifted. Every man on the mountain held still.

"Hear me, O Lord, hear me," Elijah prayed, "that this people may know that thou art the Lord God, and that thou hast turned their heart back again."

Then the fire of the Lord fell.

It came down like a great bright bolt and struck the altar. The fire consumed the burnt sacrifice. It consumed the wood. It consumed the twelve stones themselves, cracking them with heat. It consumed the dust beneath. It swept outward and licked up the water that was in the trench. Steam rose and the heat pushed through the multitude like a wave.

The people stumbled backward. Some shielded their faces. And then — every one of them — men and women and children, hundreds upon hundreds — fell on their faces to the ground. Their knees struck the dirt. Their foreheads pressed into the dust.

"The Lord, he is the God!" they cried. "The Lord, he is the God!"

Their voices rang across Mount Carmel, over the dry valley, past the broken fields, into the wide and empty sky.
