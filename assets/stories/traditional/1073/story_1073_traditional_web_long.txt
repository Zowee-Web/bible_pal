A long time without rain had turned the whole land brown and cracked. Streams dried to nothing but stones and mud. Fields that once grew tall with grain now lay flat and dusty. Animals wandered looking for water. The sky was a hard, pale blue, day after day, with no clouds.

King Ahab ruled Israel in those days. But the people had stopped following the Lord their God. Instead they bowed down to a pretend god called Baal. They built altars to Baal. They offered sacrifices to Baal. And the old altars of the Lord lay broken and forgotten.

Into this dry and troubled time, the prophet Elijah carried a message. He sent word to King Ahab: gather all of Israel to Mount Carmel. Bring the four hundred and fifty prophets of Baal.

Ahab sent messengers throughout the land. The word spread from village to village, from town to town.

And they came.

People walked the dusty roads toward the mountain. Families came in groups. Shepherds left their flocks. Farmers left their empty fields. They climbed the rocky slopes of Mount Carmel, picking their way between boulders and dry brush. The wind carried the smell of dust and dried grass.

The four hundred and fifty prophets of Baal arrived in a great crowd, their robes swaying as they walked. King Ahab came with his servants and soldiers, his chariot wheels grinding against the gravel. By the time the sun was high, hundreds and hundreds of people stood gathered on the mountaintop. The ground was packed with sandals and bare feet. Children sat on their parents' shoulders to see over the crowd.

And there was Elijah. One man. Standing before them all.

He raised his voice so everyone could hear. "How long will you go back and forth between two sides?" he said. "If the Lord is God, follow Him. If Baal is god, follow him."

The people said nothing. Not a word. Not a whisper. The wind moved across the hilltop and the silence held.

Elijah went on. "I am the only prophet of the Lord left here. I alone. But Baal has four hundred and fifty prophets." He let those numbers settle over the crowd. One against four hundred and fifty.

Then he told them the plan. Two bulls would be brought. The prophets of Baal would choose one bull, cut it in pieces, and lay it on their altar on top of wood. Elijah would do the same with the second bull on another altar. But neither side would light a fire.

"You call on the name of your god," Elijah said, "and I will call on the name of the Lord. The god who answers by fire — he is God."

All the people answered together. "That is a good plan," they said.

Elijah turned to the prophets of Baal. "You go first," he said. "There are many of you. Choose your bull. Prepare it. Call on your god. But do not light a fire."

The prophets of Baal chose their bull. They killed it and cut it into pieces, laying the meat across the wood on their altar. The crowd stepped back and watched.

Then the prophets of Baal began to call out. "O Baal, answer us!" they shouted.

They called and called. Their voices echoed off the rocks. They circled the altar, stamping their feet on the hard ground. Some raised their hands to the sky. Some bent low. They called Baal's name over and over.

An hour passed. The sun climbed higher. Two hours passed. The morning stretched on toward noon. The crowd shifted on tired feet. Children tugged at their parents' clothing. Still the prophets of Baal called out.

No voice answered. No fire came. The wood lay still on the altar, cold and dry.

At noon, Elijah spoke to them. His voice was calm and steady. "Shout louder," he said. "Surely he is a god. Perhaps he is deep in thought. Perhaps he is busy, or away on a journey. Maybe he is sleeping and someone needs to wake him up."

So the prophets of Baal shouted louder. They leaped and jumped around the altar. They cried out until their throats were raw. They kept going through the heat of the afternoon, when the sun beat down on the mountaintop and sweat ran down every face.

Nothing happened. No voice. No fire. No answer of any kind.

The altar of Baal sat cold and quiet on the mountainside. The wood did not stir. The offering did not move.

The afternoon light began to shift. Shadows stretched longer across the stones. The prophets of Baal finally grew still, their chests heaving, their voices spent.

Then Elijah said to the people, "Come near to me."

The crowd moved closer. Hundreds of them pressed forward, craning their necks, stepping over rocks to see.

Elijah walked to an old altar — the altar of the Lord, which had been broken down and scattered. Stones lay tumbled in the dirt. Weeds grew between them. He knelt and began to work, lifting each stone and setting it back in place. One stone. Two stones. Twelve stones in all, one for each of the twelve tribes of Israel, the sons of Jacob, to whom the Lord had said, "Israel shall be your name."

He set the last stone carefully and stood back. The altar was whole again.

Around its base he dug a wide trench with his hands, scooping out the dry earth, deep enough to hold two measures of seed. Dust rose around his fingers as he worked.

He arranged the wood on top of the stones, piece by piece. He cut the bull and laid the pieces on the wood. Everything was in order.

Then Elijah did something very strange.

"Fill four large jars with water," he said. "Pour it over the offering and over the wood."

Servants carried the heavy jars forward. They tipped them and water cascaded down, splashing over the meat and soaking deep into the wood. It ran down the sides of the stones in dark streams.

"Do it again," Elijah said.

They filled the jars a second time and poured. More water splashed across the altar. The wood was soaked through now, dark and dripping.

"Do it a third time."

They poured a third time. Water ran everywhere. It flooded down the stones, pooled in the dirt, and filled the trench all the way around the altar until it brimmed like a little moat. The ground turned to mud. The offering glistened. Twelve jars of water — and not a dry spot anywhere on that altar.

The crowd stared. Some people whispered to each other. Over on the other side, the four hundred and fifty prophets of Baal stood near their cold, silent altar. And here was Elijah, alone, standing next to an altar so drenched with water it seemed impossible that anything could burn.

The afternoon sun hung low. The light turned golden across the mountaintop.

Elijah stepped forward. He did not shout. He did not leap or dance. He stood still and lifted his voice in prayer.

"Lord, God of Abraham, Isaac, and Israel," he said. "Let it be known today that You are God in Israel, and that I am Your servant, and that I have done all these things at Your word."

He paused. The wind shifted. Every person on the mountain held still.

"Answer me, Lord," Elijah prayed. "Answer me, so these people will know that You, Lord, are God, and that You are turning their hearts back again."

Then fire fell from heaven.

It came down like a great bright bolt and struck the altar. The fire burned the offering. It burned the wood. It burned the twelve stones themselves, cracking them with heat. It burned the dust beneath. It swept outward and licked up every last drop of water standing in the trench. Steam rose and the heat pushed through the crowd like a wave.

The people stumbled backward. Some shielded their faces. And then — every one of them — men and women and children, hundreds upon hundreds — fell facedown on the ground. Their knees hit the dirt. Their foreheads pressed into the dust.

"The Lord, He is God!" they cried. "The Lord, He is God!"

Their voices rang across Mount Carmel, over the dry valley, past the broken fields, into the wide and empty sky.
