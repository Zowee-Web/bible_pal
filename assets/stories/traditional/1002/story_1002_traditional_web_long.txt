Nebuchadnezzar's face darkened. The muscles along his jaw tightened, and he spoke through clenched teeth, ordering his guards to bring Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego before him. The throne room was vast, its walls lined with glazed brick in deep blues and yellows, depicting striding lions and coiling serpents. Oil lamps burned along the columns, their flames reflected in the polished stone floor. The king sat elevated on a broad seat of carved cedar overlaid with gold, and the hem of his robe pooled across the steps beneath him.

The guards brought the three men forward. Their sandals scraped against the floor as they walked between rows of officials, satraps, and counselors who stood along the walls in layered robes of linen and wool. The room was warm from the press of bodies and the burning lamps. Nebuchadnezzar leaned forward, his gold bracelets catching the light as he gripped the arms of his throne.

He spoke directly to them. "Is it true, Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego, that you don't serve my gods and don't worship the golden image which I have set up?" His voice carried through the hall, and the gathered officials stood still. No one shifted. No one whispered.

The king continued without waiting for an answer. "Now if you are ready, at the time you hear the sound of the horn, flute, zither, lyre, harp, pipe, and all kinds of music, to fall down and worship the image which I have made, good. But if you don't worship, you shall be cast the same hour into the middle of a burning fiery furnace." He paused, and his next words rang off the tiled walls. "Who is that god who will deliver you out of my hands?"

Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego stood before him. They did not step back. They did not lower their heads to the floor. Their robes hung straight, their hands at their sides. The nearest officials could see their faces clearly in the lamplight.

Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego answered the king. "Nebuchadnezzar, we have no need to answer you in this matter. If it be so, our God whom we serve is able to deliver us from the burning fiery furnace, and he will deliver us out of your hand, O king. But if not, be it known to you, O king, that we will not serve your gods or worship the golden image which you have set up."

The sound of their voices faded into the stone ceiling. For a moment the hall was silent except for the faint hiss of oil burning in the lamps. Then Nebuchadnezzar rose from his seat. The expression on his face changed. His nostrils flared, and the cords in his neck stood taut. He gave orders that the furnace should be heated seven times more than it was usually heated. His voice was sharp and loud, and servants near the doorway turned and ran to carry the command.

He commanded certain mighty men of valor who were in his army to bind Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego. Soldiers stepped forward from the edges of the room. They were broad-shouldered men in leather and bronze, and they took hold of the three. Rough cords were wrapped around their wrists and pulled tight. The three men were bound in their trousers, their tunics, their robes, and their other garments — all the clothing still on their bodies. The soldiers hauled them toward the doors of the throne room, and the gathered officials watched them go.

Outside, the air was different. The plain of Dura stretched wide and flat under a white sky, and the golden image stood tall in the distance, gleaming dully. But closer, much closer, the furnace dominated the ground. It was a massive structure of brick and clay, blackened along its upper edges. Waves of heat rippled the air above its opening. Workers had been stoking it with fuel — bundles of dried wood, pitch, and whatever else could burn — and now, at the king's command, they fed it further. The roar of the fire inside was audible from a distance, a low, continuous thunder that vibrated in the chest. The ground near the furnace was hot underfoot, and the workers who approached it shielded their faces with their forearms.

The soldiers pushed the three bound men forward. The heat pressed against their skin like a wall. The closer they came, the more the air shimmered and distorted the shapes of the bricks. The soldiers gripped the arms of Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego and drove them toward the mouth of the furnace. The opening glowed orange and white, and the sound of the fire swallowed every other noise.

Because the king's command was urgent and the furnace exceedingly hot, the flame of the fire killed those men who took up Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego. The soldiers who had carried them to the opening fell where they stood, struck down by the blast of heat that poured from the furnace mouth. Their bodies crumpled on the scorched ground.

Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego fell down bound into the middle of the burning fiery furnace. They dropped through the opening and into the roaring interior, still wrapped in their cords, still wearing every layer of clothing they had been bound in.

Then Nebuchadnezzar, who had followed to watch, startled. He rose up in haste. He turned to his counselors, who stood at a safer distance, their robes pulled close against the heat. He spoke to them, and his voice was strained. "Didn't we cast three men bound into the middle of the fire?"

They answered the king. "True, O king."

He answered and said, "Look, I see four men loose, walking in the middle of the fire, and they are unharmed. The appearance of the fourth is like a son of the gods."

The counselors stepped closer, squinting against the glare. Through the furnace opening, visible against the white-hot interior, four figures moved upright. Not crouched. Not writhing. Walking. The cords that had bound the three men were gone. Their robes hung from their shoulders as they moved. And beside them walked a fourth figure, bright against the brightness, distinct from the other three.

Nebuchadnezzar approached the door of the burning fiery furnace. The heat pushed against him, and he raised his voice above the roar. "Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego, you servants of the Most High God, come out and come here!"

His words carried into the fire. And Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego came out of the middle of the fire. They stepped through the opening, one after another, onto the scorched ground. Their feet touched the earth. They stood in the open air.

The satraps, the deputies, the governors, and the king's counselors gathered around them. They pressed close. They examined them. The hair on their heads was not singed. Not a single strand was curled or blackened. Their robes were intact — the fabric whole, the weave undamaged. Their trousers, their tunics, every garment they wore was unchanged. The officials leaned in, turning the men's arms, looking at their hands, studying the cloth of their sleeves.

And the smell of fire had not even come on them. No smoke clung to the fabric. No scent of ash or char rose from their skin. The counselors who stood closest breathed in and found nothing — no trace of the furnace that had killed the soldiers who merely approached its mouth. The three men stood among the crowd on the plain of Dura, bound in nothing, burned by nothing, and the furnace still roared behind them, heat still rippling the air above its blackened bricks.
