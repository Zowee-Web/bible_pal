Then was Nebuchadnezzar filled with fury, and the form of his visage was changed against Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego. He commanded that they be brought before him, and so they were brought — three men standing upon the stone floor of that great hall, the air heavy with incense and the murmur of gathered officials.

The king spoke, his voice carrying sharp across the pillared chamber. He asked whether it was true — whether these three had purposed in themselves not to serve his gods, nor to worship the golden image he had set up. He gave them one chance more. When they should hear the sound of the cornet, the flute, the harp, the sackbut, the psaltery, the dulcimer, and all manner of instruments, they were to fall down and worship the image he had made. And if they would not, they should be cast that same hour into the midst of a burning fiery furnace.

He paused. The hall was still, save for the creak of armor and the distant hiss of flame from the braziers set along the walls. Then the king spoke again, and his words rang against the stone: Who is that god, he demanded, that shall deliver you out of his hands?

The three stood together before the throne. They answered the king plainly. They told him they were not careful to answer him in this matter. If it be so, they said, the God whom they served was able to deliver them from the burning fiery furnace, and he would deliver them out of the king's hand. But if not — and here their voices did not waver — they would have the king know that they would not serve his gods, nor worship the golden image which he had set up.

The sound of their words fell upon the assembly. No man stirred. The torchlight threw long shadows across the faces of the princes, the governors, the captains who stood ranked along the walls.

Then was Nebuchadnezzar full of fury, and the form of his countenance was changed against those three men. He turned and gave commandment that the furnace should be heated seven times more than it was wont to be heated. The order passed from his lips to the servants, and from the servants to the stokers who labored below, and the roar of the furnace grew until it shook the ground beneath the feet of all who stood near.

He commanded certain mighty men of valour that were in his army to bind Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego. Soldiers came forward — men broad of shoulder and strong of arm — and they seized the three. They bound them in their coats, their hosen, their hats, and their other garments, even as they were, making no allowance for the removal of any covering. The cords were drawn tight about their limbs. The three were lifted and carried toward the mouth of the furnace.

The heat grew as they drew near. The stones of the floor radiated warmth that rose through the soles of the soldiers' sandals. The air above the furnace opening shimmered and bent, distorting the shapes of all things seen through it. The roar of the fire was such that a man could scarce hear his own voice beside it. Sparks rose in columns, spiraling upward into the open air and vanishing against the pale sky.

The mighty men who bore Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego came to the mouth of the furnace. The heat that poured forth was beyond the heat of any forge or kiln. The flames leapt outward, tongues of fire reaching past the opening and licking the air where the soldiers stood. And because the king's commandment was urgent and the furnace exceeding hot, the flame of the fire slew those men that took up Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego. Their bodies fell upon the stones before the furnace mouth, struck down by the very heat they had carried their charges toward.

And the three men — Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego — fell down bound into the midst of the burning fiery furnace.

The flames closed over them. The assembly watched from the distance the guards had kept, the orange light playing upon their faces, the heat pressing against them even at that remove. The sound of the fire was a deep and steady thunder, punctuated by the crack and pop of fuel consumed within.

Then Nebuchadnezzar the king rose up in haste from his seat. He was astonied. He turned to his counsellors, and his voice carried above the roar of the furnace. He asked them whether they had not cast three men bound into the midst of the fire. They answered the king and confirmed it: True, O king.

He answered and said that he looked into the furnace and saw not three men, but four, loose and walking in the midst of the fire. They had no hurt upon them. And the form of the fourth, he declared, was like the Son of God.

The counsellors pressed closer, shielding their eyes against the glare. Through the opening of the furnace, the figures could be seen — four shapes moving upright among the flames, their garments visible, their limbs unbound, walking as men walk upon a road in the cool of the evening. The cords that had held them were gone. The fire billowed and surged around them, yet they moved freely within it, and no harm was upon them.

Then Nebuchadnezzar came near to the mouth of the burning fiery furnace. The heat struck his face and arms, and the light of the flames was such that he narrowed his eyes against it. He called out, and his voice was loud above the roar. He named them servants of the most high God, and he commanded them to come forth.

Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego came forth from the midst of the fire. They stepped out of the furnace and stood upon the stones before the assembly. The princes, the governors, the captains, and the king's counsellors gathered themselves together around the three men. They came close. They looked upon them.

The hair of their heads was not singed. Not a single strand was blackened or curled by flame. Their coats were whole and unmarred, the fabric intact, the garments hanging upon them as they had before they were cast in. Their hosen, their hats, their other coverings — all remained as they had been. The gathered officials leaned near and drew breath through their nostrils. The smell of fire had not passed upon them. No scent of smoke clung to their clothing. No ash dusted their shoulders. No soot marked their skin.

The furnace still roared behind them, its heat pressing outward, the bodies of the slain soldiers still lying where they had fallen upon the scorched stones. The flames within continued their furious burning, consuming the fuel that had been heaped sevenfold beyond the ordinary measure. Yet the three men stood whole and unharmed before the king and the full company of his court, the firelight casting its glow upon faces that bore no mark of burning.

Nebuchadnezzar spoke. He blessed the God of Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego, who had sent his angel and delivered his servants that trusted in him. They had changed the king's word, he declared, and yielded their bodies, that they might not serve nor worship any god except their own God. His voice carried across the assembly, and every man gathered there stood witness to what had been spoken and what had been seen — the furnace still thundering at their backs, and the three men standing unburned before them all.
