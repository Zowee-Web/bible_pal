The vineyard sat on the slope below Jezreel, close enough to the palace wall that a man on the upper terrace could see it. The vines were old. They had been planted by Naboth's grandfather, and trained along stone supports by Naboth's father, and pruned each spring by Naboth himself. The soil between the rows had been worked so long it held the print of a sandal. This was the inheritance of his fathers. This was the ground that bore them.

Ahab came down the path one morning with two attendants behind him. He stood at the low wall and looked across the rows. The leaves were green. The fruit was beginning to set. He called out to Naboth, who came up from among the vines.

"Give me your vineyard," Ahab said. "I will have it for a garden of herbs, because it is near my house. I will give you a better vineyard than this. Or, if it seems good to you, I will give you its worth in money."

Naboth listened. He did not speak at once. He looked at the king, and then he looked at the rows behind him.

"May the Lord forbid me," he said, "that I should give the inheritance of my fathers to you."

Ahab turned and went back up the path. He did not speak to the attendants. He went into the palace and into his chamber. He laid himself down on his bed, and turned his face to the wall, and would not eat the bread that was brought to him. The light in the chamber slid across the wall through the long hours. He did not move.

Jezebel came in at evening. She stood by the bed and looked down at him.

"Why is your spirit so sad," she said, "that you eat no bread?"

He told her, his face still to the wall. He told her about Naboth, and the vineyard, and the inheritance of fathers. She listened to the end.

"Do you now govern the kingdom of Israel?" she said. "Arise, and eat bread, and let your heart be merry. I will give you the vineyard of Naboth the Jezreelite."

She went out. In another room she sat down at a table. She called for ink and for parchment. She wrote letters in Ahab's name. When each letter was written she folded it and pressed the king's seal into the wax. She sent the letters to the elders and to the nobles of the city where Naboth lived. The letters told them to proclaim a fast. To set Naboth on high among the people. To place two wicked fellows before him, and to have them testify that he had cursed God and the king. And then to carry him out and stone him to death.

The men of the city did as the letters instructed. They proclaimed the fast. They set Naboth on high. The two wicked fellows came in and sat before him and gave their testimony in the hearing of the people. Then they took Naboth, and they carried him out of the city, and they stoned him with stones until he died.

A messenger went to Jezebel. "Naboth has been stoned and is dead."

She went to Ahab. "Arise," she said. "Take possession of the vineyard of Naboth the Jezreelite, which he refused to give you for money. For Naboth is not alive, but dead."

Ahab rose. He took his attendants. He went down the path to the vineyard. The gate was open. He stepped through it.

The vines were quiet in the sun. The fruit had begun to set on them. The rows ran the way Naboth's father had laid them out, and the soil between the rows still held the print of a sandal. Ahab stood among the vines and looked at the ground.

A man came down the path behind him. Ahab heard the steps. He turned.

Elijah stood at the gate.

The word of the Lord had come to him in Samaria. "Arise, go down to meet Ahab king of Israel, who dwells in Samaria. Behold, he is in the vineyard of Naboth, where he has gone down to take possession of it. You shall speak to him."

Ahab looked at the prophet across the rows. "Have you found me, my enemy?"

"I have found you," Elijah said. The vines were between them. The sun was on the leaves. "Because you have sold yourself to do that which is evil in the sight of the Lord."

He stood at the gate of Naboth's vineyard and spoke the word he had been sent to speak.

"Have you killed, and also taken possession?"

Ahab said nothing. The vineyard held still around them. The print of the sandal was in the soil between the rows.
