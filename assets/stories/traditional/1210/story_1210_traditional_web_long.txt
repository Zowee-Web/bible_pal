The vineyard sat on the slope below Jezreel. It was close enough to the palace wall that a man on the upper terrace could look down and see the rows. The vines were old. They had been planted by Naboth's grandfather. The stone supports along the rows had been set by Naboth's father. The vines had been pruned each spring by Naboth himself, since he was a boy. The soil between the rows had been worked so long that it held the print of a sandal. This was the inheritance of his fathers. This was the ground that bore them.

In the early summer, Ahab king of Samaria came down the path with two attendants behind him. He stood at the low wall of the vineyard and looked across the slope. The leaves were green. The fruit was beginning to set, hard and pale against the leaves. He called out to Naboth, and Naboth came up from among the vines, wiping his hands on the front of his garment.

"Give me your vineyard," Ahab said. "I will have it for a garden of herbs, because it is near my house. And I will give you a better vineyard than this one. Or, if it seems good to you, I will give you its worth in money."

Naboth listened. He did not speak at once. He looked at the king. Then he looked back at the rows behind him, at the supports his father had set, and at the prints of his own work in the soil.

"May the Lord forbid me," he said, "that I should give the inheritance of my fathers to you."

He said no more. He stood in the gate of the vineyard and waited.

Ahab turned and went back up the path. He did not speak to the attendants. The attendants did not speak to him. He went into the palace and through the courtyards and into his chamber. He laid himself down on his bed. He turned his face to the wall. A servant brought bread on a tray and set it beside him, and he would not eat it. The light slid across the chamber wall through the long hours of the afternoon. Ahab did not move.

Jezebel came in at evening. She stood by the bed and looked down at him.

"Why is your spirit so sad," she said, "that you eat no bread?"

He told her, his face still to the wall. He told her about the vineyard, and the offer, and the inheritance of fathers. She listened to the end.

"Do you now govern the kingdom of Israel?" she said. "Arise, and eat bread, and let your heart be merry. I will give you the vineyard of Naboth the Jezreelite."

She went out. In another room she sat down at a table. She called for ink and for parchment. She wrote the letters in Ahab's name. Her hand was steady. When each letter was finished she folded it and pressed the king's seal into the wax. The wax took the print clean. She sent the letters by messengers to the elders and to the nobles of the city where Naboth lived. The letters told them to proclaim a fast. To set Naboth on high among the people. To seat two wicked fellows before him, and to have them testify in the hearing of all the city that Naboth had cursed God and the king. And then to carry him out, and to stone him to death.

The men of the city did as the letters instructed. They proclaimed the fast. They gathered the people. They set Naboth on high among them, as a man being honored. The two wicked fellows came in and sat before him and gave their testimony, the same words spoken with the same voice, that Naboth had cursed God and the king. The people heard it. The elders rose. They took Naboth, and they carried him out of the city, and they stoned him with stones in the place outside the gate until he died.

A messenger went back to the palace at Samaria. He spoke to Jezebel.

"Naboth has been stoned and is dead."

She went to Ahab. He was at table. She stood beside him.

"Arise," she said. "Take possession of the vineyard of Naboth the Jezreelite, which he refused to give you for money. For Naboth is not alive, but dead."

Ahab rose. He called for his attendants. He went out from the palace and down the path that led to the slope below the wall.

The gate of the vineyard was open. He stepped through it.

The vines were quiet in the sun. The leaves had not yet begun to turn for the year. The fruit had set on them, hard and pale, the way it had been when he first stood at the gate. The rows ran the way Naboth's father had laid them out. The stone supports were where Naboth's father had set them. The soil between the rows had been worked so recently that the print of a sandal was still in it, near one of the older vines, where the worker had braced himself to reach. Ahab walked along the row. He did not call to the attendants. He stopped near the print in the soil and looked at it.

A man came down the path behind him. Ahab heard the steps before he turned. The steps did not hurry. They came on with the rhythm of a man who had walked a long way and had been sent.

Ahab turned.

Elijah stood at the gate.

The word of the Lord had come to him in Samaria. "Arise, go down to meet Ahab king of Israel, who dwells in Samaria. Behold, he is in the vineyard of Naboth, where he has gone down to take possession of it. You shall speak to him." Elijah had risen and had come.

He stood at the open gate of Naboth's vineyard. His cloak was dusty from the road. He did not step in. He looked across the rows at the king.

Ahab looked back at him over the vines. He spoke first.

"Have you found me, my enemy?"

"I have found you," Elijah said. The vines were between them. The sun was on the leaves. "Because you have sold yourself to do that which is evil in the sight of the Lord."

He did not lift his voice. He did not raise his hand. He stood in the gate of the vineyard where Naboth had stood, and he spoke the word he had been sent to speak.

"Have you killed, and also taken possession?"

Ahab said nothing. The vines were quiet between them. The fruit had set on them, hard and pale. The print of the sandal was in the soil, near the older vine where the worker had braced himself to reach. The vineyard held still around the king and the prophet, and the sun was on the leaves.
