And there went a man of the house of Levi, and took to wife a daughter of Levi. And the woman conceived and bare a son. When she saw him that he was a goodly child, she hid him three months.

The house was small and close. The walls were of mud brick, and every sound passed through them — a cough, a cry, a neighbour's step upon the packed earth without. Pharaoh had given his commandment. Every son that was born to the Hebrews should be cast into the river. The midwives had defied the charge. They had let the boys live, telling Pharaoh the Hebrew women were delivered ere the midwives came unto them. But the danger had not passed. Soldiers came through the streets at strange hours. They stayed at doors and hearkened. They watched women bearing bundles.

Three months the woman hid the babe. She nursed him in the darkened hinder part of the house, behind a curtain, behind stacked baskets. She held her hand near his mouth when he stirred in sleep. When he cried, she pressed him close and swayed, her own breathing drawn tight, until the sound was stilled. Her elder daughter helped. The girl learned to tread softly, to bear water without splashing, to watch the road for any who drew near. She was young, but she understood what manner of thing hung upon it.

The babe grew. His lungs grew with him. His cries became harder to muffle, and the walls grew not thicker.

When the woman could not longer hide him, she went down to the river's brink and gathered bulrushes. She cut them with a stone blade and brought them to the house. She wove them into an ark, tight and careful, large enough to hold the child. She daubed it with slime and with pitch, pressing the dark matter into every seam with her fingers until no water might pass through. She smoothed the inside and lined it with soft cloth.

She laid the child therein. He fit within it, his small arms drawn up beside his face. His eyes were open. He looked upon her.

She carried the ark to the river in the early morning, before the heat. The path was worn smooth by the feet of women who came daily to draw water. She waded into the shallows and set the ark among the flags at the water's edge. The current moved about it, slow and brown. The tall flags closed upon either side. The ark held steady, rocking with the river.

She turned and went from the bank. She looked not back. Had she looked back, she could not have departed.

And his sister stood afar off. She found a place behind the tall grass where she could see the ark but none could see her. Her feet sank in the warm mire. She settled to wait and watch. The ark rocked gently. The babe was quiet.

Time passed. The sun climbed higher. Heat pressed down upon the river, and insects moved over the face of the water.

Then the daughter of Pharaoh came down to wash herself at the river. Her maidens walked along by the riverside, their voices bright and heedless. Pharaoh's daughter stepped into the water. She saw the ark among the flags and sent her maid to fetch it.

The maid waded out and brought it to the bank. Pharaoh's daughter opened it and saw the child. The babe wept, his face red and twisted. She looked upon him a long moment. She had compassion on him. "This is one of the Hebrews' children," she said. She knew. And still she reached forth her hand.

Then said his sister, stepping forth from behind the flags. Her heart beat fierce within her, but her voice came forth steady. She said unto Pharaoh's daughter, "Shall I go and call to thee a nurse of the Hebrew women, that she may nurse the child for thee?"

And Pharaoh's daughter looked upon the girl — a Hebrew maiden, standing barefoot in the mire, offering help as though it were the most natural thing. She said unto her, "Go."

And the maid ran. Her feet smote the wet ground. She found her mother and brought her to the river's brink, both of them spent of breath. And Pharaoh's daughter said unto the woman, "Take this child away and nurse it for me, and I will give thee thy wages."

And the woman took her own child. She carried him home. The same house. The same thin walls. But now she bore him openly, paid by Pharaoh's own daughter to raise the boy she had hidden in the dark.

And the child grew. When he was grown, she brought him unto Pharaoh's daughter, and he became her son. And she called his name Moses. "Because," she said, "I drew him out of the water."
