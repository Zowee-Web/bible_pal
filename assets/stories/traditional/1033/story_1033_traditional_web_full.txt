A man of the house of Levi went and married a daughter of Levi. The woman conceived and bore a son. When she saw that he was a fine child, she hid him three months.

The house was small and close. The walls were mud brick, and every sound traveled through them — a cough, a cry, a neighbor's footstep on the packed-earth path outside. Pharaoh had given his command. Every son born to the Hebrews was to be thrown into the Nile. The midwives had defied the order. They had let the boys live, telling Pharaoh that the Hebrew women gave birth before the midwives arrived. But the danger had not passed. Soldiers came through the streets at odd hours. They stopped at doors and listened. They watched women carrying bundles.

For three months, the woman hid the baby. She nursed him in the darkened back of the house, behind a curtain, behind stacked baskets. She held her hand near his mouth when he stirred in sleep. When he cried, she pressed him close and swayed, her own breathing tight, until the sound faded. Her older daughter helped. The girl learned to move quietly, to carry water without splashing, to watch the road for anyone coming near. She was young, but she understood what was at stake.

The baby grew. His lungs grew with him. His cries became harder to muffle, and the walls did not grow thicker.

When the woman could hide him no longer, she went down to the riverbank and gathered bulrushes. She cut them with a stone blade and brought them back to the house. She wove them into a basket, tight and careful, large enough to hold the child. She daubed it with tar and with pitch, pressing the dark, sticky mixture into every seam with her fingers until no water could pass through. She smoothed the inside and lined it with soft cloth.

She laid the child in it. He fit exactly, his small arms drawn up beside his face. His eyes were open. He looked at her.

She carried the basket down to the river in the early morning, before the heat. The path was worn smooth by the feet of women who came daily to draw water. She waded into the shallows and set the basket among the reeds at the water's edge. The current moved around it, slow and brown. The tall reeds closed in on either side. The basket held steady, rocking with the river.

She turned and walked away from the bank. She did not look back. If she had looked back, she could not have left.

His sister stationed herself at a distance. She found a spot behind the tall grass where she could see the basket but not be seen. Her feet sank in the warm mud. She settled in to wait and watch. The basket rocked gently. The baby was quiet.

Time passed. The sun climbed higher. Heat pressed down on the river, and insects moved over the water's surface.

Then Pharaoh's daughter came down to the river to bathe. Her attendants walked along the riverside, their voices bright and careless. Pharaoh's daughter stepped into the water. She saw the basket among the reeds and sent her servant woman to fetch it.

The servant waded out and brought it to the bank. Pharaoh's daughter opened it and saw the child. The baby was crying, his face red and twisted. She looked at him a long moment. She had compassion on him. "This is one of the Hebrews' children," she said. She knew. And still she reached for him.

Then his sister stepped forward from behind the reeds. Her heart hammered in her chest, but her voice came out steady. She said to Pharaoh's daughter, "Shall I go and call a nurse from the Hebrew women, so that she may nurse the child for you?"

Pharaoh's daughter looked at the girl — a Hebrew girl, standing barefoot in the mud, offering help as though it were the most natural thing. She said, "Go."

The girl ran. Her feet slapped the wet ground. She found her mother and brought her back to the riverbank, both of them breathless. Pharaoh's daughter said to the woman, "Take this child away and nurse him for me, and I will give you your wages."

So the woman took her own child. She carried him home. The same house. The same thin walls. But now she carried him openly, paid by Pharaoh's own daughter to raise the boy she had hidden in the dark.

The child grew. When he was old enough, she brought him to Pharaoh's daughter, and he became her son. She named him Moses. "Because," she said, "I drew him out of the water."
