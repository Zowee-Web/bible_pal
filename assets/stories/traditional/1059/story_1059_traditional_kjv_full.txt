Nehemiah had a place of honour. He was the king's cupbearer unto King Artaxerxes — the man that tasted the king's wine ere the king drank thereof. Every day, Nehemiah stood within the goodly palace, arrayed in fine raiment, compassed about with guards and officers. It was one of the most trusted places in all the kingdom. If the king could not trust his cupbearer, he could trust no man.

But Nehemiah bare a heavy sorrow within him.

He had heard tidings from Jerusalem — the city wherein his fathers had dwelt, the city that was meant to be holy and strong and set apart. But it was none of those things any more. The walls were broken down. The great gates that once kept the city had been burned to ashes. Heaps of rubbish sat where strong towers once stood. They that dwelt therein were a reproach, and without defence.

Nehemiah could not cease to think upon it. And on a day, his sorrow shewed upon his countenance.

The king beheld him. Artaxerxes was a man of understanding. He marked all things. "Why is thy countenance sad?" the king asked. "Thou art not sick. This is nothing else but sorrow of heart."

Nehemiah's heart sank within him. To be sad before the king was perilous. Kings loved not that their servants should look unhappy — it might make the king wonder whether somewhat were amiss with the wine, or whether the servant were ill content with his place, or worse.

But Nehemiah was minded to speak the truth.

"Let the king live for ever," said he. "Why should not my countenance be sad, when the city, the place of my fathers' sepulchres, lieth waste, and the gates thereof are consumed with fire?"

The king leaned forward. "For what dost thou make request?"

This was the moment. Even there, standing before the mightiest man Nehemiah had ever known, holding an empty cup, his heart beating fast within his breast — this was when all things might change, or all things might fall to pieces.

Nehemiah prayed. Not aloud. There was no time thereof. He did but pray swiftly in his heart — a swift, silent prayer unto God. Help me, I pray thee. Give me the right words.

Then he spake. "If it please the king, wilt thou send me unto Jerusalem? I would build it again."

The king looked unto the queen that sat beside him. He looked back unto Nehemiah.

"How long shall thy journey be? When wilt thou return?" the king asked.

He said yea.

Nehemiah could scarce believe it. But he stayed not there. He asked for letters — papers of authority that should suffer him to travel safely through every province between the palace and Jerusalem. He asked for a letter unto the keeper of the king's forest, that he might have timber for the gates and the walls.

The king granted him all that he asked.

Nehemiah journeyed all the way unto Jerusalem. When he was come thither, he made not his purpose known unto any man. Not yet. First, he must needs see the ruins with his own eyes.

He tarried there three days. Then, in the night, he arose. He took but a few men and one beast. Under the stars, with no torches, they rode quietly forth by the valley gate.

The walls were worse than he had supposed. Great stones lay scattered upon the ground like broken teeth. Charred timber from the old gates lay in black heaps. Thorns and weeds grew up between the rubbish. In some places the fallen stones were piled so high that the beast could not pass through, and Nehemiah must needs go afoot.

He rode the whole way about the city in the darkness, viewing every part of the broken wall, marking the damage. Then he went back within.

On the morrow, he called the people together — the rulers, the priests, the workmen, all of them.

"Ye see the distress that we are in," said Nehemiah. "Jerusalem lieth waste. The walls are broken. The gates are gone. We are become a reproach to all that are round about us."

The people hearkened. They knew it was true.

"But hearken yet unto me," Nehemiah went on. "The good hand of my God hath been upon me. The king himself hath granted me his leave and his bounty. Now — let us rise up and build the wall of Jerusalem, that we be no more a reproach!"

The people looked one on another. They looked upon the broken stones. They looked upon Nehemiah, that stood before them with the dust of the night ride yet upon his robes.

And they said, "Let us rise up and build!"

So they girded up their loins. They took up the stones. And they began to build.
