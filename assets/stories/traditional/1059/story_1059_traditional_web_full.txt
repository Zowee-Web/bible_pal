Nehemiah had an important job. He was the cupbearer to King Artaxerxes — the person who tasted the king's wine before the king drank it. Every single day, Nehemiah stood in the beautiful palace, wearing fine clothes, surrounded by guards and officials. It was one of the most trusted jobs in the whole kingdom. If the king couldn't trust his cupbearer, he couldn't trust anyone.

But Nehemiah carried a heavy sadness inside him.

He had heard news from Jerusalem — the city where his ancestors had lived, the city that was supposed to be special and holy and strong. But it wasn't any of those things anymore. The walls were broken down. The big gates that used to protect the city had been burned to ashes. Piles of rubble sat where strong towers once stood. People who lived there were embarrassed and unprotected.

Nehemiah couldn't stop thinking about it. And one day, his sadness showed on his face.

The king looked at him. Artaxerxes was a smart man. He noticed things. "Why does your face look so sad?" the king asked. "You're not sick. This is sadness in your heart."

Nehemiah's stomach dropped. Being sad in front of the king was risky. Kings didn't like their servants to look unhappy — it could make the king wonder if something was wrong with the wine, or if the servant was unhappy with his job, or worse.

But Nehemiah decided to tell the truth.

"May the king live forever," he said. "How can I not be sad? The city where my ancestors are buried lies in ruins. Its gates have been destroyed by fire."

The king leaned forward. "What are you asking for?"

This was the moment. Right here, standing in front of the most powerful person Nehemiah had ever known, holding an empty wine cup, his heart beating fast in his chest — this was when everything could change or everything could fall apart.

Nehemiah prayed. Not out loud. There was no time for that. He just prayed quickly in his heart — a fast, silent prayer to God. Please help me. Give me the right words.

Then he spoke. "If it pleases the king, would you send me to Jerusalem? I want to rebuild it."

The king looked at the queen sitting beside him. He looked back at Nehemiah.

"How long will you be gone?" the king asked. "When will you come back?"

He said yes.

Nehemiah could hardly believe it. But he didn't stop there. He asked for letters — official papers that would let him travel safely through every territory between the palace and Jerusalem. He asked for a letter to the man in charge of the royal forest, so he could get timber for building the gates and the walls.

The king gave him everything he asked for.

Nehemiah traveled all the way to Jerusalem. When he arrived, he didn't announce his plan to anyone. Not yet. First, he needed to see the damage for himself.

He waited three days. Then, in the middle of the night, he got up. He took just a few men and one donkey. Under the stars, with no torches, they rode quietly out through the Valley Gate.

The walls were worse than he'd imagined. Huge stones lay scattered on the ground like broken teeth. Charred wood from the old gates lay in black heaps. Thorns and weeds pushed up between the rubble. In some places the fallen stones were piled so high that the donkey couldn't squeeze through, and Nehemiah had to go on foot.

He rode the whole way around the city in the dark, looking at every section of the broken wall, memorizing the damage. Then he went back inside.

The next day, he called the people together — the leaders, the priests, the workers, everyone.

"You can see the trouble we're in," Nehemiah said. "Jerusalem is a wreck. The walls are broken. The gates are gone. We are a joke to everyone around us."

The people listened. They knew it was true.

"But let me tell you something," Nehemiah continued. "God has been good to me. The king himself gave me permission and supplies. Now — let us rebuild the wall of Jerusalem so we won't be ashamed anymore!"

The people looked at each other. They looked at the broken stones. They looked at Nehemiah, who stood before them with dust on his robes from the night ride.

And they said, "Let us get up and build!"

So they rolled up their sleeves. They picked up stones. And they started to rebuild.
