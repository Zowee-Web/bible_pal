In the town of Capernaum, along the northern shore of the Sea of Galilee, word spread that Jesus had come home. The house where he was staying filled quickly. People pressed through the narrow doorway and crowded into the small rooms. More gathered outside — standing along the walls, craning their necks to see through the windows, pushing close to the entrance. Soon there was no room left, not even around the door. Jesus stood inside, speaking the word to them. His voice carried through the crowded space, reaching the people outside through the open windows.

Outside the house, four men approached through the narrow street. They carried a paralytic between them on a mat — one man at each corner, their arms straining with the weight. The paralytic lay still, his arms at his sides, his legs motionless beneath a thin cloth. His friends had carried him from across the town, moving carefully through the streets, the mat sagging between their grip. When they reached the house and saw the crowd, they stopped. There was no way through. The doorway was blocked. The windows were crowded with faces.

The four men looked at one another. Then they looked up at the flat roof above them.

They carried the paralytic up an outside stairway and onto the roof. The dried clay surface was warm under their feet. They knelt down and began to dig, pulling away the mud and branches that formed the ceiling of the room below. Their hands worked quickly — scraping, tearing, pulling back layers of thatch and dried earth. Piece by piece, they opened a hole in the roof above the place where Jesus stood teaching.

Inside, the people looked up. Dust and bits of dried clay fell into the room. A shaft of sunlight broke through the opening. The crowd pressed back, murmuring, shielding their eyes. And then, through the hole, the four men began to lower the paralytic on his mat. They gripped the corners tightly, letting him down slowly, carefully, hand over hand, until the mat came to rest on the ground at the feet of Jesus.

The room was still. Everyone watched.

Jesus saw their faith — the faith of the four men who had torn apart a roof, and of the one who had allowed himself to be carried. He looked at the paralytic lying before him.

"Son," he said, "your sins are forgiven you."

A ripple moved through the room. Some of the scribes who were sitting near the wall exchanged glances. They did not speak aloud, but in their hearts they were deeply troubled. "Why does this man speak this way?" they thought. "He is blaspheming. Who can forgive sins but God alone?"

Jesus perceived in his spirit what they were thinking. He looked at them directly.

"Why do you reason these things in your hearts?" he said. "Which is easier — to say to the paralytic, 'Your sins are forgiven,' or to say, 'Arise, take up your mat, and walk'?"

The room was silent. No one answered.

"But so that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins —"

He turned back to the paralytic.

"I tell you," he said, "arise. Take up your mat, and go to your house."

The man's fingers moved first. His hands pressed flat against the mat beneath him. Then slowly, with the whole room watching, he pushed himself upward. His arms held. His legs straightened. He stood — right there, in front of them all — steady on his feet for the first time anyone could remember. He bent down, picked up the mat he had been lying on, rolled it under his arm, and walked toward the door. The people stepped back to let him through, some pressing their hands to their mouths, others standing with wide eyes and open hands.

He walked out of the house and into the sunlight. The crowd outside saw him coming and fell silent.

Then they erupted. They gave glory to God, their voices rising together across the rooftops of Capernaum.

"We have never seen anything like this," they said.
