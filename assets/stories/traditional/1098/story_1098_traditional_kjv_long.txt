The temple courts in Jerusalem were quiet in the early morning. The sun had but cleared the eastern wall, and its light fell in long amber shafts across the stone pavement. The great columns cast deep shadows. A few priests moved between the pillars on their duties. Vendors were setting their wares along the outer court — doves in wicker cages, piles of grain, small jars of oil. The air was cool and bore the smell of incense from the evening past.

Jesus came into the temple early, while the courts were yet mostly empty. He sat down upon the steps near one of the porches. People began to arrive — men and women from the city and the towns round about, drawn by his teaching. They gathered about him, and he began to teach.

Then there was a disturbance at the edge of the crowd.

The scribes and Pharisees came through the people, their faces set. Between them they thrust a woman. Their hands gripped her arms. They steered her roughly through the gathering until she stood in the midst, exposed, surrounded, the centre of every eye.

Her garments were disordered. Her hair was loose. Her head was bowed so low her chin nigh touched her breast. Her hands hung at her sides, trembling. She spake not. She stood where they set her, and she waited.

"Master," said the scribes, their voices loud enough for the whole court to hear, "this woman was taken in adultery, in the very act." They suffered the words to hang in the air. "Now Moses in the law commanded us, that such should be stoned: but what sayest thou?"

They cared not for the woman. She was a tool — a lever fashioned to pry open a contradiction. If Jesus said to stone her, he betrayed his own teaching of mercy. If he said release her, he set himself against Moses, and they might accuse him before the council. Either way, they prevailed. The snare was clean.

Jesus answered not. He stood not up. He looked not upon them. He stooped down, and with his finger wrote on the ground.

The dust of the court was fine and smooth, warmed by the morning sun. His finger moved slowly across the surface — letters, words, shapes. The scripture saith not what he wrote.

They continued asking him. They would have an answer. "What sayest thou?"

Jesus lifted up himself. He looked upon them — not in anger, not with contempt. He looked upon them directly.

"He that is without sin among you," he said, "let him first cast a stone at her."

And again he stooped down, and wrote on the ground.

The eldest departed first. They turned and went away without a word. Then the others — one by one, in twos and threes — their certainty dissolving in the morning light. Footsteps scraped upon the stone. The crowd parted and thinned.

The courtyard emptied.

The woman stood alone. The sun had risen higher. The shadows had shortened. The doves in their cages cooed softly. There was none remaining — no accusers, no stones, no crowd. Only the woman, and Jesus, yet kneeling upon the ground.

He lifted up himself and looked upon her. His countenance was calm. His eyes were kind.

"Woman," said he, "where are those thine accusers? Hath no man condemned thee?"

She looked about the empty court. She lifted her head for the first time. Her eyes were wet, but her voice was steady.

"No man, Lord."

Jesus looked upon her — not past her, not through her, but at her. Into her face. Into the person she was, beneath all that had been said of her and done unto her.

"Neither do I condemn thee," he said. "Go, and sin no more."
