Early in the morning, Jesus came to the temple in Jerusalem. The sun was still low, casting long shadows across the stone courtyard. The air was cool. Vendors were beginning to set up along the colonnades. A few priests moved between the columns. The city was stirring but not yet fully awake.

Jesus sat down on the steps near one of the porches, and people began to gather. They came from the streets and the markets and the houses nearby — men and women who had heard him teach before, and others who were curious. He began to speak to them, his voice carrying across the open stone.

Then the scribes and the Pharisees came through the crowd. They were not alone. They pushed a woman ahead of them, gripping her arms, steering her through the gathered people until she stood in the middle of the group, in full view of everyone. Her clothing was disheveled. Her head was bowed. Her hands were trembling. She did not lift her eyes.

"Teacher," they said, "this woman has been caught in adultery, in the very act. Now in the law, Moses commanded us to stone such women." They paused, their voices hard and satisfied. "What do you say?"

They were not asking because they wanted guidance. They were testing him. They wanted to trap him — to force him into a statement they could use against him. If he said stone her, he contradicted his own teaching about mercy and forgiveness. If he said let her go, he contradicted the law of Moses.

Jesus did not answer them. He did not look at them. He bent down and began to write on the ground with his finger. The dust of the courtyard was smooth and warm beneath his hand. He traced slowly — letters, words, shapes — the scripture does not say what he wrote. Only that he wrote, his head bowed, while they stood above him, waiting.

They kept pressing. "What do you say? Answer us."

Jesus straightened up. He looked at them — not with anger, not with contempt, but with a directness that made them shift where they stood. He looked at their faces, their clenched jaws, their hands already thinking about stones.

"Let him who is without sin among you," he said, "be the first to throw a stone at her."

Then he bent down and continued writing on the ground.

The silence that followed was not empty. It was the sound of men examining themselves — each one confronting, in the quiet of his own conscience, the gap between his accusations and his life. One by one, they turned and left. The older ones went first — the ones who had lived long enough to know their own failures. Then the younger ones followed, drifting away in twos and threes, their certainty dissolving in the morning light.

The courtyard emptied. The crowd thinned. The woman stood alone in the center of the court. Jesus was still kneeling on the ground.

He straightened up and looked at her. His face was gentle.

"Woman, where are they?" he said. "Has no one condemned you?"

She looked around the quiet courtyard. The morning light fell warm across the stone. The accusers were gone.

"No one, Lord," she said.

Jesus said to her, "Neither do I condemn you. Go, and from now on, sin no more."
