The prophet spake unto a people who had grown weary.

They had borne their burdens a long time. Longer than some among them could remember. The roads whereon they journeyed were dusty and rent beneath sandaled feet, the earth splitting open in pale seams where no rain had come. The sun pressed down upon bent shoulders and bare heads from the moment it cleared the hills of the east until it fell behind the ridgeline in the west. In between, the hours stretched like the flat land itself, endless and dry.

Hands that once were lifted up in praise now hung limp at their sides. Legs ached from walking. Breath came short upon the hills. Men and women moved as the very old, even the young among them. Mothers bare water jars upon their hips with arms that shook. Fathers stood in fields with hoes and could not bring them down. Boys who ought to have been chasing goats sat instead in patches of shade, their chests heaving, their eyes dull.

The whole land seemed to drag itself forward, step by laboured step.

And unto these people, the prophet lifted up his voice.

Have ye not known?

The words crossed the dry ground. They passed the low stone walls where men sat with their heads bowed and their hands between their knees. They passed the women at the well, drawing water in silence, the ropes fraying in their palms, the earthen jars chipped along the rims. They passed the children who no longer ran in the streets, who sat instead in doorways with their knees drawn up.

Have ye not heard?

A few faces turned toward the sound. A man standing in the shade of a fig tree straightened his back, slowly, in the manner of a man whose muscles have been locked in one position overlong. A woman set her jar down upon the stone rim of the well and held still, her wet hands motionless at her sides. The breeze had died. The air was thick and warm and tasted of dust.

The Lord is the everlasting God, the prophet saith.

His voice was steady. It carried without strain across the packed dirt road, between the houses of mud brick with their flat rooftops, over the walls where garments hung without moving in the still air.

The Creator of the ends of the earth.

A lizard darted across a stone and vanished into a crack. Somewhere in the distance, beyond the last houses, an ass brayed once and fell silent. The sky above was pale, bleached by heat, with no cloud from the one horizon unto the other.

He fainteth not.

The prophet stood in the open, the sun upon his head and shoulders, speaking unto people who understood faintness in their bones. Who felt it in the trembling of their knees when they stood too quickly. Who tasted it in the dryness of their mouths at the end of the day.

He groweth not weary.

A man near the hinder part of the gathering shifted his weight from one foot unto the other. His tunic was stained dark with sweat across the shoulders and chest. His sandals were worn through at the heel, the leather cracked, the stitching pulling loose. He looked upon the ground. He rubbed his face with the back of his hand. He looked up.

His understanding is unsearchable, the prophet continued.

The word hung in the air. Unsearchable. No man could measure it. No man could find where it began or where it ended. No man could walk unto its border and look across to the other side. It was beyond the reach of tired minds and weary hands.

The woman at the well pressed her palm flat against the warm stone rim. The heat thereof seeped into her skin. An old man who had come to hearken leaned forward upon his staff, a piece of acacia wood worn smooth and dark where his hands had gripped it every day for years. The knot at the top was polished to a shine. His knuckles were swollen. His fingers curled about the wood like roots about a stone.

A boy sat upon a low wall nearby, his legs dangling, his bare feet dusty from the road. A fly lighted upon his arm and he did not brush it away. The wind stirred the dry grass at the edge of the road — a faint rustling as one that whispereth — and then nothing.

He giveth power to the faint, the prophet saith.

He paused. The silence gathered. A goat bleated from behind a wall and its kid answered, thin and high.

And to him that hath no might, he increaseth strength.

The words settled over the gathering like shade. A dog lay in the shadow of a doorway, its ribs visible beneath its thin coat, its tongue resting upon the packed dirt. Flies circled above a clay pot left uncovered. The heat pressed close against every surface — against the stone, against the walls of mud, against the skin of every man, woman, and child who stood or sat or leaned in the open air.

The prophet drew a breath and spake again.

Even the youths shall faint and be weary.

His voice was clear and unhurried. The words fell into the quiet like stones into still water.

Young men shall utterly fall.

This was a thing they had seen with their own eyes. Not a warning concerning some distant time. Not a description of some other place. They had watched the strongest among them stumble upon level ground. They had seen the youngest gasping for breath ere the day's work was half done. They had seen legs buckle beneath loads that once were borne without thought, in the manner that a man beareth his own arms, without perceiving it.

Young men sitting down in the midst of a harvested field, unable to rise. Boys casting down their tools at midday, their arms shaking, their faces pale beneath sun-darkened skin. Girls carrying bundles of sticks half the measure they carried the year before, and stopping twice upon the path homeward to set them down and rest. Even the beasts — the oxen standing still in the furrow, the asses refusing the hill.

The people stood in doorways. They sat upon stones. They leaned against walls with their arms folded or hanging loose. No man spake. No man moved to depart. The prophet's words moved through them like water through cracked earth, finding every seam.

But they that wait upon the Lord shall renew their strength.

The prophet's voice lifted. Not louder, not sharper, but fuller. In the manner that a wind filleth a loosened sail.

They shall mount up with wings as eagles.

He raised his face toward the open sky. The motion was slow and deliberate, his chin tilting upward, his eyes narrowing against the brightness. The people followed his gaze. One by one, heads turned upward. The old man with the staff. The woman at the well. The boy upon the wall. The man with the worn sandals. The mothers in the doorways. Even the children, who had not been hearkening, looked up because all others were looking up.

High above them, where the heat shimmered against pale blue, a broad-winged bird turned in a wide, slow circle. An eagle. It rode the warm air that rose from the floor of the valley in pillars unseen. Its wings were spread unto their full reach, each feather distinct at the tips, fanned wide like fingers. The wings did not beat. They did not strain. They did not labour. The bird tilted and climbed, tilted and climbed, borne upward by something beneath it and round about it that could not be seen.

They shall run, and not be weary.

The old man's grip tightened upon his staff. His knuckles whitened about the dark wood. The woman at the well drew a long breath through her nostrils and let it forth slowly through parted lips. The boy upon the wall ceased the swinging of his legs and sat perfectly still, watching the bird.

They shall walk, and not faint.

The eagle turned once more, high and distant now, a dark shape against the bleached sky. Its shadow passed over the dry ground far below, moving across the road, across the low wall, across the feet of the people gathered there. The prophet stood with his face lifted, the sun full upon his skin, the dust settling round about his sandals. The people watched the bird climb. Its wings were wide and still and open.
