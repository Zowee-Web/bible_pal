Jesus took three of his disciples — Peter, James, and John — and led them up a high mountain by themselves. He did not tell them why. He did not say what they would see. They simply followed, climbing through rocky ground where the path narrowed and the scrub brush thinned and the air grew cool with altitude. The valley fell away below them. The towns along the shore of the lake became small clusters of stone. The noise of the world dropped away, replaced by wind and the sound of their own breathing.

At the top, the mountain was bare. The rock was pale and smooth. The sky felt close — a deep, clear blue pressing down on every side. The wind moved across the summit in long, steady gusts.

Then something happened that they were not prepared for.

Jesus' appearance changed before their eyes. His face began to shine — not like firelight, not like lamplight, but like the sun itself, blazing and unbearable. The light came from within him, as though the skin and the bone and the ordinary appearance of the man they had walked with for months had been a covering, and now the covering was pulled back. His clothing became white — brilliant, searing white, whiter than bleached linen, whiter than snow, a white that had no comparison in the natural world. He stood on the bare rock, and the mountain was flooded with light.

Then two figures appeared beside him. They materialized out of the brightness, standing with Jesus, speaking with him as though they knew him — and they did. The disciples recognized them, not by face, for they had never seen their faces, but by something deeper than sight. They were Moses and Elijah — the giver of the law and the greatest of the prophets — and they stood with Jesus on the mountaintop, talking with him.

Peter was overwhelmed. He was trembling. He did not understand what he was seeing, and he reached for the only response he could find.

"Lord," he said, "it is good for us to be here." His voice was unsteady, the words tumbling out. "If you wish, I will make three shelters here — one for you, one for Moses, and one for Elijah."

While he was still speaking, a cloud moved over the mountain. It was not an ordinary cloud — not gray, not dark. It was bright, luminous, dense with light, and it settled over them like a presence. The disciples could not see through it. They could not see each other. They fell to the ground face-first, pressing their foreheads into the rock, their whole bodies trembling.

A voice spoke from the cloud.

"This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased. Listen to him."

The words filled the air. They filled the cloud. They filled the disciples' chests and their ears and the ground beneath them. The fear that came was not the fear of danger but the fear of being in the presence of something immeasurably vast.

Then a hand touched them. It was Jesus' hand — a human hand, calloused from carpenter's work, warm and real.

"Rise," he said. "Do not be afraid."

They lifted their faces from the rock. The cloud was gone. Moses and Elijah were gone. The blazing light had faded. Jesus stood alone on the bare mountaintop, looking the way he always looked — dusty robe, sun-browned face, gentle eyes.

They came down the mountain together in silence. The valley spread out below them, ordinary and green, the lake glinting in the late afternoon light. No one spoke about what they had seen. The wind blew. The path wound downward. And the man walking ahead of them looked, once again, like any other man — except they now knew he was not.
