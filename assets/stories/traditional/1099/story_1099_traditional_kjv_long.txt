Jesus took three of his disciples — Peter, James, and John his brother — and led them apart from the others. He told them not whither they went nor what they should see. He said only, "Come with me," and they followed.

They walked out from the village and toward the base of an high mountain. The path began gently, winding through olive groves and patches of wild grass. But as they climbed, the growth thinned. The olives gave way to scrub, then to bare rock. The path narrowed. The stones shifted beneath their sandals. The air grew cool with every step.

Below them, the valley opened wide. The lake glinted in the distance as hammered silver. The towns along the shore shrank to small clusters of stone. The sounds of the world faded — the voices, the beasts, the clatter of common life — replaced by the steady rush of wind across the rock.

At the summit, the ground was bare. Pale stone stretched in every direction, smoothed by ages of wind and rain. The sky was vast — a deep, clear blue pressing down upon the mount from every quarter. There was nothing here but rock and sky and the four of them.

Then something came to pass for which none of them had words.

The face of Jesus began to change. It was not gradual. His face did shine as the sun — blazing, unbearable — radiating light from within, as though the form they had walked with these many months had been a covering, and the covering was now drawn back. His raiment became white as the light — not the white of linen nor of snow, but a white that was not a colour at all, but the presence of every colour at once.

The three disciples stumbled backward. They shielded their eyes. The man they had walked with, eaten with, slept beside upon the open road — the man who grew weary, who sweated, who brake bread and asked for water — was blazing as the sun.

Then two figures appeared beside him. They appeared out of the brightness as though they had waited behind a curtain now drawn aside. They stood with Jesus, speaking with him. The disciples knew them — not by sight, for they had never beheld their faces, but by a knowing that went beyond the eyes. They were Moses and Elias. The giver of the law and the greatest of the prophets — the two most towering figures in all their history — standing upon a mountaintop, talking with Jesus as old friends.

Peter was sore afraid. His body trembled. His mind reached for something he might do. He opened his mouth.

"Lord," he said, "it is good for us to be here." The words came forth unsteady. "If thou wilt, let us make here three tabernacles; one for thee, and one for Moses, and one for Elias."

While he yet spake, a bright cloud overshadowed them. It was not grey, not dark. It was luminous, thick with light, pressing in from every side. The air became dense and heavy, charged with a presence that made their skin tingle and their lungs feel full ere they drew breath. They could see nothing — not Jesus, not one another, not the rock beneath their feet.

They fell upon their faces. All three — pressing their foreheads into the stone, their hands flat upon the rock, their bodies shaking. They were sore afraid.

A voice spake out of the cloud.

"This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased; hear ye him."

Then a hand touched them. A human hand — warm, real, calloused from years of labour with wood. It touched Peter's shoulder. Then James's. Then John's.

"Arise," said Jesus, "and be not afraid."

They looked up. The cloud was gone. Moses and Elias were departed. The blazing light had faded. The summit was bare rock once more, and the sky was ordinary blue.

Jesus stood alone. He looked as he ever looked — dusty robe, sun-browned face, gentle eyes, calloused hands. The same hands that had touched them awake.

They came down from the mountain in silence. The path wound through the rocks and the scrub and back into the olive groves. The valley rose to meet them. The lake appeared again, and the towns, and the sounds of common life.

None spake of what they had seen.
