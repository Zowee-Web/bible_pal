Jesus chose three of his disciples — Peter, James, and John — and led them away from the others. He did not tell them where they were going or why. He simply said, "Come with me," and they followed.

They walked out of the village and toward the base of a high mountain. The path began gently, winding through olive groves and patches of wild grass. But as they climbed, the vegetation thinned. The olive trees gave way to scrub brush, then to bare rock. The path narrowed. The stones shifted under their sandals. The air grew cooler with every hundred feet of elevation.

Below them, the valley opened wide. The lake glinted in the distance like a sheet of hammered silver. The towns along the shore — Capernaum, Bethsaida, Chorazin — shrank to small clusters of stone and thatch. The sounds of the world faded — the voices, the animals, the clatter of daily life — replaced by the steady rush of wind across the rock.

At the top of the mountain, the ground was bare. Pale stone stretched in every direction, smoothed by centuries of wind and rain. The sky was enormous — a deep, clear blue that seemed to press down on the summit from all sides. There was nothing up here but rock and sky and the four of them.

Peter wiped the sweat from his face. James leaned against a boulder to catch his breath. John stood with his hands on his knees, looking out over the valley below. Jesus walked ahead of them, toward the center of the summit.

Then something happened that none of them had words for.

Jesus' face began to change. It was not a gradual shift, not a trick of the light. His face began to shine — to radiate light from within, as though the sun itself had been placed behind his skin. The brightness built and built until it was unbearable, a blazing, white-hot luminance that turned the pale rock around him into a stage of pure light. His clothes changed too — they became white, a white beyond description, beyond anything that existed in the natural world. Not the white of linen or of snow or of bleached cloth. A white that was not a color at all, but the presence of every color at once, searing and absolute.

The three disciples stumbled backward. They shielded their eyes. The man they had walked with, eaten with, slept beside on the open road — the man who got tired, who sweated, who laughed at their jokes and asked for bread — was blazing like the sun.

Then two figures appeared beside him. They materialized out of the brightness as though they had been waiting behind a curtain that was now drawn aside. They stood with Jesus, speaking with him. The disciples recognized them — not by their faces, for they had never seen their faces, but by a knowing that bypassed the eyes entirely. They were Moses and Elijah. The giver of the law and the greatest of the prophets, the two most towering figures in the history of their people, standing on a mountaintop, talking with Jesus as though they were old friends.

Peter was terrified. His whole body was shaking. His mind reached for something — anything — to do, some response that would make this manageable. He opened his mouth.

"Lord," he said, "it is good for us to be here." The words came out unsteady, almost stammering. "If you wish, I will make three shelters here — one for you, one for Moses, and one for Elijah."

While he was still speaking, a cloud descended on the mountain. It was not a gray cloud, not a storm cloud. It was bright — luminous, thick with light, pressing in from every direction. It enveloped them. The air became dense and heavy, charged with a presence that made their skin tingle and their lungs feel full before they had drawn a breath. Visibility dropped to nothing. They could not see Jesus. They could not see each other. They could not see the rock beneath their feet.

They fell to the ground. All three of them — face-first, pressing their foreheads into the stone, their hands flat on the rock, their bodies shaking. They were terrified.

Then a voice spoke from the cloud.

"This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased. Listen to him."

The disciples pressed their faces harder into the rock.

Then a hand touched them.

It was a human hand — warm, real, calloused from years of working with wood. It touched Peter's shoulder. Then James's. Then John's. One by one.

"Rise," Jesus said. "Do not be afraid."

They looked up. Slowly. The cloud was gone. Moses and Elijah were gone. The blazing light had faded. The mountaintop was bare rock again, and the sky was ordinary blue, and the wind moved across the summit the way it always had.

Jesus stood alone. He looked the way he always looked — dusty robe, sun-browned face, gentle eyes, calloused hands. The same hands that had just touched them awake. The same face that had, moments before, shone like the sun.

They descended the mountain in silence. The path wound down through the rocks and the scrub and back into the olive groves. The valley rose to meet them. The lake appeared again, and the towns, and the sounds of ordinary life.

None of them spoke about what they had seen.
