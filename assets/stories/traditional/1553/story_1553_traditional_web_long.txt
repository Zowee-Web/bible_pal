David sat in his house. He had been sitting there since morning. The roof of the palace was the same roof he had walked on the evening the trouble began, and the window looked out over the same Jerusalem. He was a king in the city he had taken with his own hand. He was no longer the man who had walked the hillsides with the harp.

The court had grown thinner around him in recent weeks. He had not summoned them. They had withdrawn of their own accord, the way men do when something has happened that no one will name. Joab knew. Joab had been told what to do, and had done it. The few who knew anything at all were quieter than they had been before. The wife the king had brought into the house was carrying the child, and the child of the previous house had been born and was sick.

David did not pray. He had stopped praying without deciding to stop. The harp lay in its corner unused. He had eaten little. He had slept badly. He was not yet ready to look at any of this directly. He sat in the chair, and he waited for the day to pass.

Nathan came up from the gate at midday. He had been sent. He did not ask for an audience. He walked through the open doors and stood before the king, and the few attendants in the room saw the look on the prophet's face and stepped back without being told.

"There were two men in one city," Nathan began. "The one rich, and the other poor. The rich man had very many flocks and herds. But the poor man had nothing except one little ewe lamb, which he had bought and raised. It grew up together with him and with his children. It ate of his own food, drank of his own cup, lay in his bosom. It was to him as a daughter.

"A traveler came to the rich man, and the rich man spared to take of his own flock or his own herd to dress for the wayfarer who had come to him. He took the poor man's lamb, and dressed it for the man who had come to him."

David listened. He sat forward in the chair. The injustice of it landed in him as it would have landed in him at twelve years old, in the fields, watching his brothers come back from market with empty hands. The picture was clear. The lamb in the poor man's bosom. The hand of the rich man taking what was not his.

His anger burned hot.

"As Yahweh lives," he said, "the man who has done this deserves to die. He must restore the lamb fourfold, because he did this thing, and because he had no pity."

Nathan looked at him for a long moment. The look held something the king had not seen on a face for many years — not anger, exactly. Something underneath anger. Something patient and sorrowful.

"You are the man," Nathan said.

There was no sound in the room. The wind moved at the window. The attendants did not breathe. David did not stand up. He did not call for the guard. He did not order the prophet's death.

He sat very still.

Nathan went on. He spoke the word of the Lord, and he spoke it without softening. The gifts Yahweh had given the king — the throne taken from Saul's hand, the rescue from the cave at En Gedi, the wives given into his bosom, the kingdom and the house of Israel and Judah, the more that would have been added had the king asked. The despising of Yahweh's word. The taking of the wife. The killing of the husband. The sword that would not depart from his own house from this day on. The evil that would come up against him out of his own house. The thing he had done in secret, that would be done back to him in the light of the sun, before all Israel.

The prophet's voice was steady. He was not shouting. He was reading aloud what had already been written.

When the prophet finished, the room was silent for a long time.

The king's mouth opened. The words that came out were the only words he had left to say.

"I have sinned against the Lord."

He did not say more. He did not explain. He did not bargain. There was nothing to bargain with. The man whose anger had burned hot at the rich man for the sake of one ewe lamb was the same man who had taken a ewe lamb of his own without thought, and he knew it now.

Nathan looked at him a moment more.

"Yahweh has put away your sin," the prophet said. "You will not die."

He paused, as though he might say more. He did not. He had said what he had been sent to say.

The prophet turned, and went down the stairs the way he had come up. His steps were quiet. The guards at the gate let him pass. He did not look back.

The king sat alone in the room. The attendants drifted away one by one without being told. The afternoon light moved slowly across the floor, and the sounds of the city came up through the window — a hammer somewhere, a woman calling a child, the distant work of the gate that had nothing to do with him. The child of the previous house lay sick in the inner room and would not live.

On the same roof. In the same city. The long judgment had begun, and the long mercy had begun, and there was nothing he could do about either one of them now but receive them. And so it was on the day Nathan the prophet stood before David the king, in his house in Jerusalem, and told the king his own life back to him in the shape of a poor man's ewe lamb.
