David sat in his house. He had been sitting there since morning. The roof of the palace was the same roof upon which he had walked the evening the trouble began, and the window looked out over the same Jerusalem. He was a king in the city he had taken with his own hand. He was no longer the man who had walked the hillsides with the harp.

The court had grown thinner around him in recent weeks. He had not summoned them. They had withdrawn of their own accord, the way men do when something hath come to pass that no one will name. Joab knew. Joab had been bidden a thing, and had done it. The few who knew anything at all were quieter than they had been before. The wife the king had brought into the house was with child, and the child of the former house had been born and was sick.

David did not pray. He had ceased to pray without choosing to cease. The harp lay in its corner untouched. He had eaten little. He had slept ill. He was not yet ready to look at any of this directly. He sat in the chair, and he waited for the day to pass.

Nathan came up from the gate at midday. He had been sent. He did not ask for an audience. He walked through the open doors and stood before the king, and the few attendants in the room saw the look on the prophet's face and drew back without being told.

"There were two men in one city," Nathan began. "The one rich, and the other poor. The rich man had exceeding many flocks and herds. But the poor man had nothing, save one little ewe lamb, which he had bought and nourished up. It grew up together with him and with his children. It did eat of his own meat, and drank of his own cup, and lay in his bosom. It was unto him as a daughter.

"There came a traveller unto the rich man, and the rich man spared to take of his own flock and of his own herd to dress for the wayfaring man that was come unto him. He took the poor man's lamb, and dressed it for the man that was come to him."

David listened. He sat forward in the chair. The injustice of it landed in him as it would have landed in him at twelve years old in the fields, watching his brethren come back from market with empty hands. The picture was plain. The lamb in the poor man's bosom. The hand of the rich man taking what was not his.

His anger was greatly kindled.

"As the Lord liveth," he said, "the man that hath done this thing shall surely die. He shall restore the lamb fourfold, because he did this thing, and because he had no pity."

Nathan looked at him for a long moment. The look held something the king had not seen on a face for many years — not anger, exactly. Something underneath anger. Something patient and sorrowful.

"Thou art the man," Nathan said.

There was no sound in the room. The wind moved at the window. The attendants did not breathe. David did not stand up. He did not call for the guard. He did not order the prophet's death.

He sat very still.

Nathan went on. He spake the word of the Lord, and he spake it without softening. The gifts the Lord had given the king — the throne taken from the hand of Saul, the deliverance from the cave at En Gedi, the wives given into his bosom, the kingdom and the house of Israel and of Judah, the more that should have been added if the king had asked. The despising of the commandment of the Lord. The taking of the wife. The slaying of the husband. The sword that should not depart from his own house from this day on. The evil that should be raised up against him out of his own house. The thing he had done secretly, that should be done back unto him in the sight of the sun, before all Israel.

The prophet's voice was steady. He was not shouting. He was reading aloud what had already been written.

When the prophet finished, the room was silent a long time.

The king's mouth opened. The words that came out were the only words he had left to say.

"I have sinned against the Lord."

He said no more. He did not explain. He did not bargain. There was nothing wherewith to bargain. The man whose anger had been kindled at the rich man for the sake of one ewe lamb was the same man who had taken a ewe lamb of his own without thought, and he knew it now.

Nathan looked at him a moment more.

"The Lord also hath put away thy sin," the prophet said. "Thou shalt not die."

He paused, as though he might say more. He did not. He had said what he had been sent to say.

The prophet turned, and went down the stairs the way he had come up. His steps were quiet. The guards at the gate let him pass. He looked not back.

The king sat alone in the room. The attendants drifted away one by one without being told. The afternoon light moved slowly across the floor, and the sounds of the city came up through the window — a hammer somewhere, a woman calling a child, the distant work of the gate that had nothing to do with him. The child of the former house lay sick in the inner room, and should not live.

On the same roof. In the same city. The long judgment had begun, and the long mercy had begun, and there was nothing he could do about either one of them now but receive them. And so it was on the day Nathan the prophet stood before David the king, in his house in Jerusalem, and told the king his own life back to him in the shape of a poor man's ewe lamb.
