David sat in his house. He had been sitting there since morning. The roof of the palace was the same roof upon which he had walked the evening the trouble began, and the window looked out over the same Jerusalem. He was a king in the city he had taken with his own hand. He was no longer the man who had walked the hillsides with the harp.

The court had grown thinner around him in recent weeks. He had not summoned them. They had withdrawn of their own accord, the way men do when something hath come to pass that no one will name. Joab knew. Joab had been bidden a thing, and had done it. The few who knew anything at all were quieter than they had been.

Nathan came up from the gate at midday. He had been sent. He did not ask for an audience. He walked through the open doors and stood before the king and began to speak.

"There were two men in one city," he said. "The one rich, and the other poor. The rich man had exceeding many flocks and herds. But the poor man had nothing, save one little ewe lamb, which he had bought and nourished up. It grew up together with him and with his children. It did eat of his own meat, and drank of his own cup, and lay in his bosom. It was unto him as a daughter.

"There came a traveller unto the rich man, and the rich man spared to take of his own flock and of his own herd to dress for the wayfaring man that was come unto him. He took the poor man's lamb, and dressed it for the man that was come to him."

David listened. He sat forward in the chair. The injustice of it landed in him as it would have landed in him at twelve years old in the fields, watching his brethren come back from market with empty hands.

His anger was greatly kindled.

"As the Lord liveth," he said, "the man that hath done this thing shall surely die. He shall restore the lamb fourfold, because he did this thing, and because he had no pity."

Nathan looked at him for a long moment.

"Thou art the man," he said.

There was no sound in the room. David did not stand up. He did not call for the guard. He did not order the prophet's death. He sat very still.

Nathan went on. He spake the word of the Lord, and he spake it without softening. The gifts the Lord had given the king — the throne, the deliverance from the hand of Saul, the wives given into his bosom, the kingdom and the house of Israel, the more that should have been added if the king had asked. The despising of the commandment of the Lord. The sword that should not depart from his own house from this day on. The evil that should be raised up against him out of his own house. The thing he had done secretly, that should be done back unto him in the sight of the sun.

The prophet's voice was steady. He was not shouting. He was reading aloud what had been written upon the day already.

When the prophet finished, the king's mouth opened, and the words that came out were the only words he had left to say.

"I have sinned against the Lord."

Nathan looked at him a moment more.

"The Lord also hath put away thy sin," he said. "Thou shalt not die."

And the prophet turned, and went down the stairs the way he had come up. His steps were quiet. The guards at the gate let him pass. He looked not back.

The king sat alone in the room. The afternoon light moved across the floor, and the sounds of the city came up through the window — a hammer somewhere, a woman calling a child, the distant work of the gate that had nothing to do with him. On the same roof. In the same city. The long judgment had begun, and the long mercy had begun, and there was nothing he could do about either one of them now but receive them. And so it was on the day Nathan the prophet stood before David the king, in his house in Jerusalem, and told the king his own life back to him in the shape of a poor man's ewe lamb.
