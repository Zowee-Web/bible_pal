David sat in his house. The roof of the palace was the same roof he had walked on the evening the trouble began. The window looked out over the same Jerusalem. He was a king in the city he had taken with his own hand, and he was no longer the man who had walked the hillsides with the harp.

Nathan came up from the gate. He had been sent. He stood before the king and said: there were two men in one city, the one rich and the other poor. The rich man had very many flocks and herds. But the poor man had nothing except one little ewe lamb, which he had bought and raised. It grew up with him and with his children. It ate of his own food, drank of his own cup, lay in his bosom, and was to him as a daughter.

A traveler came to the rich man, the prophet said. And the rich man spared to take of his own flock or his own herd to dress for the wayfarer, but took the poor man's lamb, and dressed it for the man who had come to him.

David's anger burned hot. As Yahweh lives, he said, the man who has done this deserves to die. He must restore the lamb fourfold, because he did this thing, and because he had no pity.

Nathan looked at him for a long moment.

You are the man, he said.

There was no sound in the room. David did not stand up. He did not call for the guard. He did not order the prophet's death. He sat very still while Nathan spoke the word of the Lord — the gifts Yahweh had given him, the wives and the kingdom and the house of Israel, and the sword that would not depart from his own house from this day on.

When the prophet finished, the king's mouth opened, and the words that came out were the only words he had left to say.

I have sinned against the Lord.

Nathan said: Yahweh has put away your sin. You will not die.

And the prophet turned, and went down the stairs the way he had come up. The king sat alone in the room, on the same roof, in the same city, and the long judgment had begun, and the long mercy had begun, and there was nothing he could do about either one of them now but receive them.
