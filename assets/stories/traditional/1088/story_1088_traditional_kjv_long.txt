The upper room was upon the second floor of a house in Jerusalem. A narrow stone stair led up from the street to a wooden door that opened into a long, low-ceilinged chamber. The walls were plastered and pale. Oil lamps sat in small niches, their flames steady in the still air, casting pools of warm light across the floor. A low table ran the length of the room, set about with bread and wine and dishes of herbs. Cushions were laid along both sides. The evening air came in at an open window, carrying the sounds of the city — voices, the rumble of carts, the distant bleating of lambs being made ready for the passover feast.

It was the night before the passover. Jesus and his twelve disciples had come unto this room for the evening meal. They reclined about the table, their sandals set aside near the door, their feet dusty from the day's walking. The room bore the smell of bread and lamp oil and the faint sweetness of wine.

Jesus knew what was at hand. He knew that his hour was come — the hour of which he had spoken, the hour toward which all things had been moving. He knew that Judas, the son of Simon Iscariot, had already made his compact with the chief priests. He knew that before the sun rose again, he would be taken. He knew what the morrow held, and the afternoon that followed. He loved his disciples. He had loved them from the beginning, and he loved them now, unto the very end.

He rose from the table.

The disciples looked up. Jesus laid aside his outer garments — the cloak he wore above his tunic — and set them upon one of the cushions. He took up a long linen towel and girded himself therewith. Then he lifted a great clay vessel and poured water into a bason. The sound of the water falling into the bason was the only sound in the room.

He bore the bason to the far end of the table, where the first disciple sat. He kneeled upon the floor. He took the man's foot in his hands.

The foot was dusty. The skin was hard and calloused — the foot of a man that walketh everywhere, that climbeth hills, that standeth long hours in the sun. Jesus cupped water from the bason and poured it over the foot, washing away the dust. He rubbed gently between the toes, along the sole, about the ankle. Then he dried the foot with the towel wherewith he was girded.

He moved to the next disciple. And the next. He wrought slowly, carefully, giving to each the same unhurried care. No man spake. The room was still — only the sound of water, the dripping from the bason, the soft rustle of the towel against skin. Some turned their faces aside, their countenances flushed. Some watched with wide eyes. The teacher, upon his knees, doing the work of a household servant.

He cometh to Simon Peter. Peter drew his feet back beneath him. His face was set with feeling.

"Lord," said he, "dost thou wash my feet?"

Jesus looked up at him from the floor. His knees were wet. His hands were damp. "What I do thou knowest not now," he said, "but thou shalt know hereafter."

Peter shook his head. His voice was firm. "Thou shalt never wash my feet."

The room was still as stone. The other disciples held their breath.

Jesus saith unto him, "If I wash thee not, thou hast no part with me."

The words struck Peter as cold water. His countenance changed — from refusal to something near unto alarm. "Lord," he said, his voice breaking, "not my feet only, but also my hands and my head."

A gentle look passed across the face of Jesus. "He that is washed needeth not save to wash his feet, but is clean every whit," he said. "And ye are clean, but not all."

He said this because he knew who should betray him. Not all of them were clean.

He finished washing Peter's feet. He went on to the rest, one by one, until the bason had been borne the full length of the table. When the last foot had been washed and dried, he set the bason down near the door. He loosed the towel from his waist, dried his hands, and took his garments again.

He returned to his place at the table. The lamps flickered. The bread sat untouched. The wine was yet in the cups. The disciples sat in silence, looking upon the man who had kneeled before every one of them.

Jesus looked about the room. His eyes rested upon each face in its turn.

"Know ye what I have done to you?" said he. "Ye call me Master and Lord: and ye say well; for so I am. If I then, your Lord and Master, have washed your feet; ye also ought to wash one another's feet."

He suffered the words to settle.

"I have given you an example," he said, "that ye should do as I have done to you. Verily, verily, I say unto you, The servant is not greater than his lord; neither he that is sent greater than he that sent him."

He paused. The room was perfectly still. The lamps burned. The bread waited. The bason of soiled water sat near the door, the towel laid across its edge — a silent witness unto what had been done.

"If ye know these things," said Jesus, "happy are ye if ye do them."

Without, the city of Jerusalem moved through its last quiet evening. Somewhere in the streets below, a door was shut. Somewhere a child laughed and was hushed. Somewhere a lamb bleated in the dark. The night before the passover went on, common and unregarded — save in that upper room, where all things had already begun to change.
