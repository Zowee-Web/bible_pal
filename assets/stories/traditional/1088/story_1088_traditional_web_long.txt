The upper room was on the second floor of a house in Jerusalem. A narrow stone stairway led up from the street to a wooden door that opened into a long, low-ceilinged space. The walls were plastered and pale. Oil lamps sat in small niches, their flames steady in the still air, casting pools of warm light across the floor. A low table ran the length of the room, set with bread and wine and bowls of herbs. Cushions were arranged along both sides. The evening air drifted through an open window, carrying the sounds of the city — voices, the clatter of carts, the distant bleating of lambs being prepared for the Passover feast.

It was the night before Passover. Jesus and his twelve disciples had come to this room for the evening meal. They reclined around the table, their sandals set aside near the door, their feet dusty from the day's walking. The room smelled of bread and lamp oil and the faint sweetness of wine.

Jesus knew what was coming. He knew that the hour had arrived — the hour he had spoken of, the hour toward which everything had been moving. He knew that Judas, the son of Simon Iscariot, had already made his arrangement with the chief priests. He knew that before the sun rose again, he would be arrested. He knew what the morning held, and the afternoon after that. He loved his disciples. He had loved them from the beginning, and he loved them now, to the very end.

He rose from the table.

The disciples looked up. Jesus took off his outer garment — the cloak he wore over his tunic — and laid it over one of the cushions. He picked up a long linen towel and wrapped it around his waist. Then he lifted a large clay jar and poured water into a basin. The sound of the water filling the basin was the only sound in the room.

He carried the basin to the far end of the table, where the first disciple sat. He knelt down on the floor. He took the man's foot in his hands.

The foot was dusty. The skin was rough and calloused — the foot of a man who walked everywhere, who climbed hills, who stood for hours in the sun. Jesus cupped water from the basin and poured it over the foot, washing away the dust. He rubbed gently between the toes, along the sole, around the ankle. Then he dried the foot with the towel around his waist.

He moved to the next disciple. And the next. He worked slowly, carefully, giving each person the same unhurried attention. No one spoke. The room was quiet — only the sound of water, the occasional drip from the basin, the soft rustle of the towel against skin. Some of the disciples looked away, their faces flushed. Some watched him with wide eyes. The teacher, on his knees, doing the work of a household servant.

When he came to Simon Peter, Peter drew his feet back. His face was tight with emotion.

"Lord," he said, "are you going to wash my feet?"

Jesus looked up at him from the floor. His knees were wet. His hands were damp. "What I am doing, you do not understand now," he said. "But afterward, you will understand."

Peter shook his head. His voice was firm. "You will never wash my feet."

The room went still. The other disciples held their breath.

Jesus said to him quietly, "If I do not wash you, you have no part with me."

The words hit Peter like cold water. His expression changed — from resistance to something close to panic. "Lord," he said, his voice cracking, "then not just my feet — wash my hands and my head too!"

A gentle expression crossed Jesus' face. "A person who has bathed only needs to wash his feet," he said. "The rest of him is clean. And you are clean — though not every one of you."

He said this because he knew who would betray him. Not all of them were clean.

He finished washing Peter's feet. He moved on to the remaining disciples, one by one, until the basin had been carried the full length of the table. When the last foot had been washed and dried, he set the basin down near the door. He removed the towel from his waist, dried his hands, and put his outer garment back on.

He returned to his place at the table. The lamps flickered. The bread sat untouched. The wine was still in the cups. The disciples sat in silence, looking at the man who had just knelt before each of them.

Jesus looked around the room, his eyes resting on each face in turn.

"Do you understand what I have done for you?" he said. "You call me Teacher and Lord, and you are right, for that is what I am. If I then — your Lord and your Teacher — have washed your feet, you also ought to wash one another's feet."

He let the words settle.

"I have given you an example," he said, "that you should do as I have done to you. A servant is not greater than his master. Nor is a messenger greater than the one who sent him."

He paused. The room was perfectly still. The lamps burned. The bread waited. The basin of dirty water sat near the door, the towel draped over its edge — a silent witness to what had happened.

"If you know these things," Jesus said, "blessed are you if you do them."

Outside, the city of Jerusalem moved through its last quiet evening. Somewhere in the streets below, a door closed. Somewhere a child laughed and was hushed. Somewhere a lamb bleated in the dark. The night before Passover continued, ordinary and unremarkable — except in that upper room, where everything had already begun to change.
