It was the night before Passover. Jesus and his disciples had gathered in an upper room in Jerusalem for the evening meal. The room was simple — a low table set with bread and wine, cushions arranged on the floor, oil lamps burning in small niches along the walls. The warm light moved with every draft of air. The sounds of the city drifted up through the windows — voices, footsteps, the distant bleating of lambs being led to slaughter for the feast.

Jesus knew what was coming. He knew that the hour had arrived for him to leave this world and go to the Father. He knew that Judas, the son of Simon Iscariot, would betray him before the night was over. He knew what the morning held. But he did not speak of it. Not yet.

Instead, he rose from the table.

The disciples watched as he took off his outer garment and laid it aside. He picked up a linen towel and wrapped it around his waist. Then he poured water from a jar into a large basin — the sound of it filling the quiet room. He carried the basin to where the first disciple sat and knelt down on the floor.

He took the man's foot in his hands — a dusty, calloused foot, the foot of a fisherman or a laborer, a foot that had walked the same roads Jesus had walked. He washed it gently, carefully, running the water over the skin and between the toes. Then he dried it with the towel. The disciple said nothing. Jesus moved to the next.

He washed their feet one by one. The room was quiet. The only sound was the water — the dipping, the pouring, the soft splash of it falling back into the basin. Some of the disciples looked away, uncomfortable. Some watched him in silence, their hands still in their laps.

When he came to Simon Peter, Peter pulled his feet back beneath him. "Lord," he said, "are you going to wash my feet?"

Jesus looked up at him from the floor. "What I am doing, you don't understand now," he said. "But afterward, you will understand."

Peter shook his head firmly. "You will never wash my feet."

Jesus said to him quietly, "If I don't wash you, you have no part with me."

Peter's expression changed. "Lord," he said quickly, "then not just my feet — wash my hands and my head too!"

Jesus smiled gently. "A person who has bathed only needs to wash his feet, and he is completely clean," he said. "And you are clean — though not all of you." He said this because he knew who was going to betray him. Not all of them were clean.

When he had washed the last pair of feet, he set the basin aside. He dried his hands on the towel, took off the linen, and put his outer garment back on. He returned to his place at the table. The oil lamps flickered. The bread sat untouched. The disciples sat quietly, unsure of what they had just witnessed.

Jesus looked around the room, his eyes moving from one face to the next.

"Do you understand what I have done for you?" he said. "You call me Teacher and Lord, and you are right, for that is what I am. If I then — your Lord and Teacher — have washed your feet, you also ought to wash one another's feet. For I have given you an example, that you should do as I have done to you."

He paused. The room was perfectly still.

"A servant is not greater than his master," he said. "Nor is a messenger greater than the one who sent him. If you know these things, blessed are you if you do them."

The oil lamps burned low. The basin of water sat near the door, the towel draped over its edge. Outside, the city of Jerusalem settled into the quiet of the evening — the last night before everything would change.
