The psalmist lifted his voice and cried out.

The cry was doubled, the way grief doubles when it has no other shape. The first word was the name of God. The second word was the name of God again. And then the question that had no end to it.

"My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? Why are you so far from helping me, and from the words of my groaning?"

He cried in the daytime. The sun rose, and his voice rose with it. He cried in the night season. The dark came down, and his voice did not stop. He was not silent.

And no answer came down to meet him.

But the psalmist named what he still knew. "You are holy, you who inhabit the praises of Israel." The God who lived where the songs of the people went up. The God whose throne was made out of those praises, layered one upon another over generations. The fathers had trusted in that God, and had been delivered. They had cried, and had not been disappointed. The memory of their deliverance pressed against him in the silence where his own had not yet come.

Then he named himself.

A worm, and no man. A reproach of men. Despised by the people. Those who saw him mocked him with their lips. They shook their heads and said, "He trusts in the Lord. Let him deliver him. Let him rescue him, since he delights in him."

The mockery worked itself into the psalmist's hearing the way smoke works itself into cloth. It did not leave. And still he turned back to God. "You brought me out of the womb. You made me trust at my mother's breasts. You are my God since my mother bore me." The first breath he had drawn, the first cry he had made — God had been there for those too.

Then the enemies came in close.

The bulls of Bashan surrounded him. Strong bulls, broad-shouldered, thick-necked, the cattle of the rich pasturelands beyond the Jordan. They were the strongest of their kind. They encircled him on every side. They opened their mouths wide against him, the way lions open their mouths over the prey they have torn — roaring, breath hot, teeth bared.

Dogs encompassed him. The wild dogs that came in packs to scavenge what had fallen, gathering wherever death was near. A company of evildoers enclosed him. The ring of them tightened.

And inside the ring, the body broke.

The psalmist sang what his body felt. He was poured out like water on the ground. There was no holding shape to him anymore. His bones were out of joint, one from another, every place where a joint should have held now loose and wrong. His heart inside him had melted like wax in the heat of a furnace. The strength that had stood inside him was dried up like a potsherd left in the sun, the moisture gone out of it, brittle. His tongue clung to the roof of his mouth. The mouth itself was thirst.

He had been brought into the dust of death.

His hands and his feet were pierced through. He could count all of his bones — every rib visible, every line of the frame standing out beneath the skin. They looked and stared at him. They divided his garments among themselves and cast lots over his clothing while he watched. The garments were taken before the body was finished with them.

And out of that dust the cry came again. Not different. Not corrected. The same cry, lifted out of the deepest place. "Don't be far off, Lord. Hurry to help me. Deliver my soul from the sword, my precious life from the power of the dog. Save me from the lion's mouth."

The line bent.

In the same breath that had pleaded for rescue, a different word arrived in the psalmist's mouth. "Yes, you have rescued me from the horns of the wild oxen." Past tense. Done. The rescue was somewhere behind him now, even as the dust still clung to his skin and the marks of the enemies were still upon his body.

The psalm turned. And the psalmist turned with it.

What had begun as the cry of one forsaken became the voice of one who had been heard. The cry that had seemed to fall into nothing had reached him. The Lord had not despised the affliction of the afflicted. He had not abhorred it. He had not hidden his face. When the cry had gone up, he had heard.

That hearing was the pivot. The whole arc of the psalm leaned upon it.

The psalmist turned toward the assembly.

"I will declare your name to my brothers," he sang. "Among the assembly, I will praise you." He would gather the worshipers. He would stand in the great congregation and tell what had been done. The praise that had not yet been sung would be sung now in the open, in the place where the people came together.

He called out to them. To those who feared the Lord. To all the descendants of Jacob. Praise him. Glorify him. The voice that had cried in the night was now calling the assembly to its song.

The praise widened.

The humble would eat and be satisfied. Those who had been hungry would have food set before them. The hearts of those who sought God would live forever. The vows that had been made in the hour of trouble would now be paid before those who feared him.

The widening went further.

All the ends of the earth would remember. All the relatives of the nations would come to worship. The peoples of the far places, the families of every land — they would turn to the Lord, for the kingdom belonged to the Lord, and he was the ruler over the nations. The rich of the earth would eat and worship. Even those who went down to the dust would bow before him. Even the one who could not keep his own soul alive.

And the psalmist sang on, past himself, past his own generation, past the time of his own dying.

Posterity would serve him. Future generations would be told about the Lord. They would come — children not yet born, peoples not yet gathered, voices not yet given to the songs of Israel — and they would declare his righteousness. They would speak it to a people that would be born after them. They would tell what had been done.

The telling would carry. From mouth to mouth, from generation to generation, from the dust where the cry had been lifted into the praises of an assembly that had no end.

He had done it.

The psalm came to rest on that telling.
