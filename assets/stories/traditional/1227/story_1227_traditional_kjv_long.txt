The psalmist lifted up his voice and cried out.

The cry was doubled, the way grief doubleth when it hath no other shape. The first word was the name of God. The second word was the name of God again. And then the question that had no end thereof.

"My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? Why art thou so far from helping me, and from the words of my roaring?"

He cried in the daytime. The sun arose, and his voice arose with it. He cried in the night season. The dark came down, and his voice ceased not. He was not silent.

And no answer came down to meet him.

But the psalmist named what he yet knew. "Thou art holy, O thou that inhabitest the praises of Israel." The God who dwelt where the songs of the people went up. The God whose throne was made out of those praises, layered one upon another over generations. The fathers had trusted in that God, and had been delivered. They had cried, and were not confounded. The memory of their deliverance pressed against him in the silence wherein his own had not yet come.

Then he named himself.

A worm, and no man. A reproach of men. Despised of the people. They that saw him laughed him to scorn with their lips. They shook the head and said, "He trusted on the Lord that he would deliver him. Let him deliver him, seeing he delighted in him."

The mockery wrought itself into the psalmist's hearing the way smoke worketh itself into cloth. It departed not. And yet he turned back unto God. "Thou art he that took me out of the womb. Thou didst make me hope upon my mother's breasts. Thou art my God from my mother's belly." The first breath he had drawn, the first cry he had made — God had been there for those also.

Then the enemies drew nigh.

The bulls of Bashan compassed him about. Strong bulls, broad-shouldered, thick-necked, the cattle of the rich pasturelands beyond Jordan. They were the strongest of their kind. They beset him round on every side. They gaped upon him with their mouths, as lions open their mouths over the prey they have torn — ravening, roaring, the breath hot, the teeth bared.

Dogs compassed him. The wild dogs that came in packs to scavenge what had fallen, gathering wheresoever death was nigh. The assembly of the wicked inclosed him. The ring of them tightened.

And within the ring, the body brake.

The psalmist sang what his body felt. He was poured out like water upon the ground. There was no holding shape unto him any more. All his bones were out of joint, one from another, every place wherein a joint should have held now loose and wrong. His heart within him was melted like wax in the heat of a furnace. The strength that had stood within him was dried up like a potsherd left in the sun, the moisture gone out of it, brittle. His tongue clave unto the roof of his mouth. The mouth itself was thirst.

He had been brought into the dust of death.

His hands and his feet were pierced through. He could tell all his bones — every rib visible, every line of the frame standing out beneath the skin. They looked and stared upon him. They parted his garments among them, and cast lots upon his vesture while he beheld it. The garments were taken ere the body was finished with them.

And out of that dust the cry came again. Not other. Not corrected. The same cry, lifted out of the deepest place. "Be not far from me, O Lord. Haste thee to help me. Deliver my soul from the sword, my darling from the power of the dog. Save me from the lion's mouth."

The line bent.

In the same breath that had pleaded for rescue, another word came into the psalmist's mouth. "Yea, thou hast heard me from the horns of the unicorns." Past tense. Done. The rescue was somewhere behind him now, even as the dust yet clave unto his skin and the marks of the enemies were yet upon his body.

The psalm turned. And the psalmist turned with it.

What had begun as the cry of one forsaken became the voice of one that had been heard. The cry that had seemed to fall into nothing had reached him. The Lord had not despised the affliction of the afflicted. He had not abhorred it. Neither had he hid his face. When the cry had gone up, he had heard.

That hearing was the pivot. The whole arc of the psalm leaned upon it.

The psalmist turned toward the assembly.

"I will declare thy name unto my brethren," he sang. "In the midst of the congregation will I praise thee." He would gather the worshippers. He would stand in the great congregation and tell what had been done. The praise that had not yet been sung should be sung now in the open, in the place wherein the people came together.

He called out unto them. Unto them that feared the Lord. Unto all the seed of Jacob. Praise him. Glorify him. The voice that had cried in the night was now calling the assembly unto its song.

The praise widened.

The meek should eat and be satisfied. They that had been hungry should have food set before them. The hearts of them that sought God should live for ever. The vows that had been made in the hour of trouble should now be paid before them that feared him.

The widening went further.

All the ends of the world should remember. All the kindreds of the nations should come to worship. The peoples of the far places, the families of every land — they should turn unto the Lord, for the kingdom was the Lord's, and he was the governor among the nations. The fat upon earth should eat and worship. Even they that go down to the dust should bow before him. Even he that cannot keep his own soul alive.

And the psalmist sang on, past himself, past his own generation, past the time of his own dying.

A seed should serve him. Future generations should be told of the Lord. They should come — children not yet born, peoples not yet gathered, voices not yet given unto the songs of Israel — and they should declare his righteousness. They should speak it unto a people that should be born after them. They should tell what had been done.

The telling should carry. From mouth to mouth, from generation to generation, from the dust wherein the cry had been lifted up unto the praises of an assembly that hath no end.

He hath done it.

The psalm came to rest upon that telling.
