The psalmist lifted up his voice and cried out.

The cry was doubled, the way grief doubleth when it hath no other shape. "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? Why art thou so far from helping me, and from the words of my roaring?"

He cried in the daytime. He cried in the night season. The voice ceased not. And no answer came down to meet it.

But the psalmist named what he yet knew. "Thou art holy, O thou that inhabitest the praises of Israel." The fathers had trusted, and had been delivered. They had cried, and were not confounded. The memory of their deliverance pressed against him in the silence wherein his own had not yet come.

Then he named himself. A worm, and no man. A reproach. Despised of the people. They that saw him laughed him to scorn with their lips. They shook the head and said, "He trusted on the Lord that he would deliver him. Let him deliver him, seeing he delighted in him."

The mockery wrought itself into him. Yet he turned back unto God. "Thou art he that took me out of the womb. Thou didst make me hope upon my mother's breasts. Thou art my God from my mother's belly."

Then the enemies drew nigh.

The bulls of Bashan compassed him about — broad-shouldered, thick-necked, the strongest of their kind. They gaped upon him with their mouths, as lions ravening and roaring over their prey. Dogs compassed him. The assembly of the wicked inclosed him on every side.

And the body brake under the encirclement. The psalmist sang what his body felt. Poured out like water. Bones out of joint, one from another. The heart within him melted like wax in the heat of a furnace. Strength dried up like a potsherd left in the sun. The tongue cleaving to the roof of the mouth, swollen and dry.

He had been brought into the dust of death.

His hands and his feet were pierced. He could tell all his bones. They parted his garments among them, and cast lots upon his vesture while he beheld it.

And out of that dust the cry came again. "Be not far from me, O Lord. Haste thee to help me. Deliver my soul from the sword, my darling from the power of the dog. Save me from the lion's mouth."

The line bent.

In the same breath that had pleaded for rescue, another word came into the psalmist's mouth. "Yea, thou hast heard me from the horns of the unicorns." Past tense. Done. The rescue was somewhere behind him now, even as the dust yet clave unto his skin.

The psalm turned. And the psalmist turned with it.

"I will declare thy name unto my brethren," he sang. "In the midst of the congregation will I praise thee."

He called out unto the worshippers. Unto them that feared the Lord. Unto all the seed of Jacob. Praise him. Glorify him.

For the Lord had not despised the affliction of the afflicted. Neither had he hid his face. He had heard the cry. The cry that had seemed to fall into nothing had reached him. The whole arc of the psalm turned upon that hearing.

The praise widened.

The meek should eat and be satisfied. The hearts of them that sought God should live for ever. All the ends of the world should remember and turn unto the Lord. All the kindreds of the nations should come and worship before him, for the kingdom was the Lord's, and he was the governor among the nations.

Even they that go down to the dust should bow before him. Even he that cannot keep his own soul alive.

And the psalmist sang on, past himself, past his own generation. A seed should serve him. Future generations should be told of the Lord. They should come, and should declare his righteousness unto a people that should be born — speaking of the cry that had been heard, and of the rescue that had come, and of him that had done it all.

The psalm came to rest upon that telling.
