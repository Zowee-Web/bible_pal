The psalmist lifted his voice and cried out.

The cry was doubled, the way grief doubles when it has no other shape. "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? Why are you so far from helping me, and from the words of my groaning?"

He cried in the daytime. He cried in the night season. The voice did not stop. And no answer came down to meet it.

But the psalmist named what he still knew. "You are holy, you who inhabit the praises of Israel." The fathers had trusted, and had been delivered. They had cried, and had not been disappointed. The memory of their deliverance pressed against him in the silence where his own had not yet come.

Then he named himself. A worm, and no man. A reproach. Despised by the people. Those who saw him mocked him with their lips. They shook their heads and said, "He trusts in the Lord. Let him deliver him. Let him rescue him, since he delights in him."

The mockery worked itself into him. Still, he turned back to God. "You brought me out of the womb. You made me trust at my mother's breasts. You are my God since my mother bore me."

Then the enemies came in close.

The bulls of Bashan surrounded him — broad-shouldered, thick-necked, the strongest of their kind. They opened their mouths wide against him, lions tearing prey and roaring. Dogs encompassed him. A company of evildoers enclosed him on every side.

And the body broke under the encirclement. The psalmist sang what his body felt. Poured out like water. Bones out of joint, one from another. The heart inside him melted like wax in the heat of a furnace. Strength dried up like a potsherd left in the sun. The tongue clinging to the roof of the mouth, swollen and dry.

He had been brought into the dust of death.

His hands and feet were pierced. He could count all of his bones. They divided his garments among themselves and cast lots over his clothing while he watched.

And out of that dust the cry came again. "Don't be far off, Lord. Hurry to help me. Deliver my soul from the sword, my precious life from the power of the dog. Save me from the lion's mouth."

The line bent.

In the same breath that had pleaded for rescue, a different word arrived in the psalmist's mouth. "Yes, you have rescued me from the horns of the wild oxen." Past tense. Done. The rescue was somewhere behind him now, even as the dust still clung to his skin.

The psalm turned. And the psalmist turned with it.

"I will declare your name to my brothers," he sang. "Among the assembly, I will praise you."

He called out to the worshipers. To those who feared the Lord. To all the descendants of Jacob. Praise him. Glorify him.

For the Lord had not despised the affliction of the afflicted. He had not hidden his face. He had heard the cry. The cry that had seemed to fall into nothing had reached him. The whole arc of the psalm pivoted on that hearing.

The praise widened.

The humble would eat and be satisfied. The hearts of those who sought God would live forever. All the ends of the earth would remember and turn to the Lord. All the families of the nations would come and worship before him, for the kingdom belonged to the Lord, and he ruled over the nations.

Even those who went down to the dust would bow before him. Even the one who could not keep his own soul alive.

And the psalmist sang on, past himself, past his own generation. Posterity would serve him. Future generations would be told about the Lord. They would come, and they would declare his righteousness to a people that had not yet been born — speaking of the cry that had been heard, and of the rescue that had come, and of the one who had done it all.

The psalm came to rest on that telling.
