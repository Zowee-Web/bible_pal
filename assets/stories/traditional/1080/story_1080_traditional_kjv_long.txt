The road through the village was thick with people. Dust rose in low clouds from the shuffle of sandals and the pressing of bodies moving together in one direction. The sun stood high, and its light fell white and hard upon the flat roofs and the pale stone walls along the way. The air was warm, tinged with the smell of sweat and dry earth and the faint sweetness of bruised herbs crushed underfoot along the edge of the road.

At the midst of the crowd walked a man called Jesus. His garment was plain and dusty, the hem darkened from the road. He moved at an even pace, but the multitude thronged him from every side — men reaching forward to touch his arm, women calling his name from behind the shoulders of those in front, children weaving between legs and stumbling over feet. Some bore the sick upon mats. Others led the blind by the hand. The voices rose and fell together in a single sound, like the wind among dry grass.

In that crowd, pressed between strangers, was a certain woman whom no man marked. She kept her head bowed. She called not out nor reached forward. She moved slowly, her body thin, her raiment worn. She had had an issue of blood twelve years. Twelve years of the same slow affliction — the same heaviness, the same weakness, the same secret burden that kept her at the edges of every gathering.

She had gone to physicians. One after another, in town after town, she had sought help. She had suffered many things of many physicians — bitter medicines, grievous treatments, remedies that promised healing and gave nothing. She had spent all that she had. Every coin, every possession, every small saving — all poured out for cures that availed not. And she was nothing bettered. She grew worse. The blood continued, and her strength ebbed, and the years kept passing.

But she had heard the things spoken concerning Jesus. Stories came into the village on the lips of travelers and merchants and those who had been healed. Blind men that could see again. Lepers whose flesh was made clean. A man who had not walked in years, seen bearing his own bed through the streets. She hearkened unto these stories the way a drowning soul watcheth a distant shore — not with hope, exactly, but with something older and deeper that would not let go.

That day she followed the crowd. She spake to no man. She lifted her face to none. She came up behind Jesus, pressing through the closely packed bodies, step by step, until she was near enough to reach.

She said within herself, "If I may touch but his clothes, I shall be whole."

She put forth her hand. The crowd surged and shifted. Her fingers touched the border of his garment — the rough wool of the hem, the fringe that hung at the edge of the cloth.

And in that instant, all was changed.

The fountain of her blood was dried up. She felt it in her body — not by degrees, not over time, but at once. A stillness where there had been a constant, quiet draining. A steadiness in her limbs. A warmth in her hands. She stood in the midst of the crowd and knew, with the certainty of one who hath lived with a thing twelve years and then felt it depart, that she was healed of that plague.

In the same moment, Jesus stood still. The crowd pressed forward and then slowed. People pressed upon one another, confused. Jesus turned and looked behind him. He had perceived it — virtue going out of him, drawn forth as surely as water is drawn from a well.

"Who touched my clothes?" he asked.

His disciples looked upon him with wonder. "Thou seest the multitude thronging thee," they said, "and sayest thou, Who touched me?"

But Jesus departed not from that place. He stood still in the midst of the road, and his eyes moved slowly across the crowd — face after face, searching.

The woman felt his gaze ere she saw it. She stood very still, her hands at her sides, her breath shallow. She was trembling. She knew what was done in her. She knew who had done it. And she knew that it could not remain hid.

She came forward. The crowd parted about her. She fell down before him in the dust. Her head was bowed. She was shaking.

Then she looked up. She met his eyes. And she told him all.

She told him of the twelve years. Of the physicians and the suffering. Of the money she had spent and the hope she had lost. Of the day she heard the tidings and the morning she purposed to come. Of the crowd and the pressing and the reaching. Of the moment her fingers touched the hem of his garment.

She told him all the truth. Every word of it.

Jesus looked upon her. The noise of the multitude had fallen away. His countenance was gentle. His voice was warm.

"Daughter," he said, "thy faith hath made thee whole. Go in peace, and be whole of thy plague."

She knelt there a moment longer. The dust was warm beneath her knees. The sun fell upon her bowed head. Then she arose, and the crowd parted to let her pass, and she went her way — straight of back, steady of foot — into the bright heat of the afternoon.
