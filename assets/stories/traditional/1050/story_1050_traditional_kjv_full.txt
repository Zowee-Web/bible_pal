The word of the Lord came to Jeremiah, saying, "Arise, and go down to the potter's house, and there I will cause thee to hear my words."

Then Jeremiah arose and went down from the city. He walked through the narrow streets where the craftsmen laboured — the smell of charcoal from the workers in metal, the ring of hammers upon stone, the hiss of hot bronze quenched in troughs of water. Women bare jars upon their heads, stepping round the puddles of grey water that gathered betwixt the cobbles. The potter's house stood at the lower end of the valley, nigh unto the water where the clay was digged. The path downward was steep and worn smooth by the feet of many generations.

He found the door standing open. The workshop within was dim and cool after the brightness of the street. The ceiling hung low, and the walls were lined with shelves bearing rows of finished vessels — jars with narrow mouths for oil, great-bellied pots for grain, shallow bowls for the kneading of bread. Each one smooth and fired and waiting for its purpose. The air smelled of wet earth and the sharp savour of raw clay. Motes of dust turned slowly in a shaft of light from the single high window.

The potter sat at his wheels. He was a man advanced in years, with thick arms and hands darkened by long seasons of clay, the lines of his knuckles stained beyond any washing. A leather apron covered his breast. A lump of wet earth sat upon the stone wheel before him, grey-brown and glistening. A bowl of water rested nigh his knee, the surface trembling each time the wheel turned.

He set the wheel turning with his foot. The stone hummed. The clay began to spin.

The potter wet his hands and pressed them round about the clay. His thumbs found the centre and pressed downward, opening a hollow. His fingers rose along the outside, drawing the walls upward. The clay yielded where he pressed, held where he gave support. Water ran in thin lines down the turning surface, dripping from the base of the wheel to the packed earth of the floor.

The vessel grew. A jar was forming — narrow at the base, swelling in the middle, the neck beginning to rise. Jeremiah watched the potter's hands, how they wrought together, one within and one without, each knowing its own measure. The wheel hummed and the wet clay caught the light from the window, gleaming as it turned.

And the vessel that he made of clay was marred in the hand of the potter.

Something within the vessel gave way. A weakness in the wall — perchance a pocket of air caught when the clay was wrought, perchance a grain of sand too coarse to hold. The brim buckled. The neck folded inward upon itself. The jar that had been forming fell in upon one side whilst the other held fast. A wet sound as the clay struck the turning wheel.

The potter stayed not the wheel. He cursed not, nor reached for new clay. His hands, yet wet, pressed the ruined vessel down again. He folded the broken walls inward, working the clay into itself with the heel of his palm. The wheel turned and the clay flattened, rounded, became a lump once more — shapeless, wet, the same as before. The shape which had been — the jar, the neck, the rising brim — was gone. The clay was as it had been ere the potter first set his hands upon it. He scraped the water from his forearms with the edge of his hand.

He dipped his hands in the bowl of water. Then he began again.

This time his thumbs opened the clay after a different fashion. The hollow was wider. The walls rose more slowly, built thicker. A different vessel took shape — broader at the base, shorter at the neck, the proportions altogether changed. A bowl, perhaps, or a wide-mouthed jar for the keeping of dried fruit. Whatever it was, it was not what the first vessel had been. So he made it again another vessel, as seemed good to the potter to make it. His foot kept the wheel at an even pace, the stone grinding softly against the packed floor.

The clay held fast. The walls stood even. The brim rounded smoothly beneath his fingers. The wheel hummed its low note.

Then the word of the Lord came unto Jeremiah. "O house of Israel, cannot I do with you as this potter? saith the Lord. Behold, as the clay is in the potter's hand, so are ye in mine hand, O house of Israel."

The wheel turned. The potter lifted his hands away and the vessel stood upon the spinning stone — squat and broad and whole, its walls even, its brim true. Water glistened upon its surface. The potter reached for the wire to cut it free from the wheel, drawing the thin cord beneath the base where clay met stone.
