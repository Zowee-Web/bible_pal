The word of the Lord came to Jeremiah. "Arise, and go down to the potter's house, and there I will let you hear my words."

Jeremiah rose and left the city. He walked down through the narrow streets where the craftsmen worked — the smell of charcoal from the metalworkers, the ring of hammers on stone, the hiss of hot bronze quenched in water troughs. Women carried jars on their heads, stepping around puddles of gray water that pooled between the cobbles. The potter's house sat at the lower end of the valley, near the water source where the clay was dug. The path down was steep and worn smooth by generations of feet.

He found the door open. The workshop inside was dim and cool after the glare of the street. The ceiling was low, the walls lined with shelves holding rows of finished vessels — jars with narrow mouths for oil, wide-bellied pots for grain, shallow bowls for kneading bread. Each one smooth and fired and waiting for its purpose. The air smelled of wet earth and the mineral tang of raw clay. Dust motes turned slowly in a shaft of light from the single high window.

The potter sat at his wheel. He was an older man with thick forearms and hands darkened by years of clay, the lines and cracks of his knuckles permanently stained. A leather apron covered his chest. A lump of wet earth sat on the stone wheel before him, gray-brown and glistening. A bowl of water rested near his knee, the surface trembling each time the wheel turned.

He set the wheel turning with his foot. The stone hummed. The clay began to spin.

The potter wet his hands and pressed them around the clay. His thumbs found the center and pushed down, opening a hollow. His fingers rose along the outside, coaxing the walls upward. The clay responded — yielding where he pressed, holding where he supported. Water ran in thin lines down the spinning surface, dripping from the base of the wheel to the packed-earth floor below.

The vessel grew. A jar was forming — narrow base, swelling middle, neck beginning to rise. Jeremiah watched the potter's hands, the way his fingers worked together, one hand inside and one hand out, each knowing its pressure. The wheel hummed and the wet clay caught the light from the window, gleaming as it turned.

Then the clay failed.

Something within the vessel gave way. A weakness in the wall — perhaps a pocket of air trapped when the clay was wedged, perhaps a grain of sand too large to hold. The rim buckled. The neck folded inward. The jar that had been forming collapsed upon itself, one side caving in while the other held. A wet slapping sound as the clay fell against the wheel.

The potter did not stop the wheel. He did not curse or reach for fresh clay. His hands, still wet, pressed the ruined vessel back down. He folded the collapsed walls inward, working the clay into itself with the heel of his palm. The wheel turned and the clay flattened, rounded, became a lump again — shapeless, wet, the same as it had been before. The shape that had been there — the jar, the neck, the forming rim — was gone. The clay was as it had been before the potter began. He scraped the excess water from his forearms with the edge of his hand.

He dipped his hands in the water bowl. Then he started again.

This time his thumbs opened the clay differently. The hollow was wider. The walls rose slower, thicker. A different vessel was taking shape — broader at the base, shorter at the neck, the proportions changed entirely. A bowl, perhaps, or a wide-mouthed jar for storing dried fruit. Whatever it was, it was not what the first vessel had been. He made it again another vessel, as seemed good to the potter to make it. His foot kept the wheel at a steady speed, the stone grinding softly against the packed floor.

The clay held. The walls were even. The rim rounded smoothly under his fingers. The wheel hummed its low note.

Then the Lord spoke to Jeremiah. "O house of Israel, can I not do with you as this potter has done? Behold, like the clay in the potter's hand, so are you in my hand, O house of Israel."

The wheel turned. The potter lifted his hands away and the vessel stood on the spinning stone — squat and broad and whole, its walls even, its rim true. Water glistened on its surface. The potter reached for a wire to cut it free from the wheel, sliding the thin cord beneath the base where clay met stone.
