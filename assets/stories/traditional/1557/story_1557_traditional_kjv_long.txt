The eleven disciples went away into Galilee, into a mountain where Jesus had appointed them.

They had walked north from Jerusalem in the days after the empty tomb. It was the way they had come down so many times for the feast, but the road felt other now. The country was quieter than it had been at the passover. The roads were not thronged. The talk in the towns they passed through was not of them. Most of those towns knew not yet what had come to pass at the cross, or what had come to pass on the third day. The men walked among people for whom the world had not turned over, and the strangeness of that walking was its own kind of weight.

They were a smaller company than they had been. Judas was not among them. They were eleven now. They spake not his name often as they walked. They spake not much of anything as they walked. They had seen too much in too few days. Peter walked at the front with his head bowed, the weight of the fire in the courtyard yet upon his shoulders, and the breakfast on the shore yet within him. John was near him. The two of them did not speak often, but their walking matched. Thomas, who had asked to put his hand into the wound, was somewhere in the midst of the company. The others — Andrew, James the brother of John, Philip, Bartholomew, Matthew, James the son of Alphaeus, Thaddaeus, Simon the Zealot — were spread along the road, some in pairs, some alone with their thoughts.

The mountain Jesus had named was the one they had walked together before. They knew the slope of it. They had eaten there, upon the grass, that day with the loaves and the fishes — the day with the five thousand, when the basketsful of bread had been gathered up after every one had eaten and could eat no more. They climbed it now in silence, in pairs, the way men climb when they are not sure what is at the top. The sea of Galilee lay below them as they went up. The sun was past the middle of the sky. The fishing boats on the water were small and slow at that distance, and one could see the wakes of them moving from one shore to another.

He was there when they came. They saw him standing in the open place near the summit, with the sea below in the distance and the country of the Decapolis far off across the water. He was not as he had been before the cross, and he was not as the gardener had seemed unto Mary in the dawn. He was himself, and he was changed, and they had no words yet for either of those things. He waited for them.

And when they saw him, they worshipped him. Some of them did so without thinking, falling on their faces. Some of them did so because they could not have done otherwise — their legs gave way under them. And some doubted. The text nameth them not. It saith not it was Thomas, who had already touched the wound. It saith not it was any one in particular. It saith only: some doubted. As though Matthew, looking back upon that day many years later, would be sure to keep the door open for the man who should read these words much later and recognize himself.

He set them not apart. He called them not out. He asked them not to declare themselves before he spake. He made them not to line up the worship on one side and the standing on the other.

And Jesus came and spake unto them, saying:

"All power is given unto me in heaven and in earth. Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost; teaching them to observe all things whatsoever I have commanded you. And, lo, I am with you alway, even unto the end of the world."

He had not asked their certainty. He had asked their feet.

He looked at them — at the ones who had worshipped and at the ones who had not. He looked at the ones whose faces were wet and at the ones whose faces were dry. He had been with them three years upon the roads of this country. He had eaten and slept with them in the houses of fishers and publicans. He knew them. He knew which of them yet doubted and which of them had finally decided. He set them not apart. The promise covered both. He was sending them with the doubt yet in them. He was promising to come with them notwithstanding.

The wind moved over the mountain. The light moved upon the sea far below. A bird crossed the open ground between him and the trees. Somewhere down on the shore, far below, a man was drawing a net, and another man was crying to a third about a boat.

The eleven looked at one another. They did not all look the same as they had looked before he spake. Some of them were no longer at the edge of the company. Some of them yet were. He had not changed that. He had only added the promise that he should be with them all the way home — unto the end of the world, the text saith, and not one day less.

They should go from the mountain back down to the sea, and from the sea to Jerusalem, and from Jerusalem out into the cities and the roads and the ships of the world. Some of them should die in places they had never heard of when they climbed this mountain. They knew not any of that yet. They knew only what he had just said.

After a while, they began to come down. They did not all come down together. Some tarried at the top a long time. Peter sat upon the grass with his hands upon his knees and looked out over the sea. John sat near him. Thomas walked the perimeter of the open place. The rest moved among themselves, slowly, the way men move when they have been told a thing they shall not finish hearing for the rest of their days.

And so it was on the day Jesus sent his disciples out into the world, on the mountain in Galilee, where he had told them he should meet them.
