The eleven disciples went into Galilee, to the mountain where Jesus had sent them.

They had walked north from Jerusalem in the days after the empty tomb. It was the way they had come down so many times for the festival, but the road felt different now. The country was quieter than it had been at Passover. The roads were not crowded. The talk in the towns they passed through was not about them. Most of those towns did not yet know what had happened at the cross, or what had happened on the third day. The men walked among people for whom the world had not turned over, and the strangeness of that walking was its own kind of weight.

They were a smaller group than they had been. Judas was not among them. They were eleven now. They did not say his name often as they walked. They did not say much of anything as they walked. They had seen too much in too few days. Peter walked at the front with his head down, the weight of the fire in the courtyard still on his shoulders, and the breakfast on the beach still inside him. John was near him. The two of them did not speak often, but their walking matched. Thomas, who had asked to put his hand into the wound, was somewhere in the middle of the group. The others — Andrew, James the brother of John, Philip, Bartholomew, Matthew, James the son of Alphaeus, Thaddaeus, Simon the Zealot — were spread out along the road, some in pairs, some alone with their thoughts.

The mountain Jesus had named was the one they had walked together before. They knew the slope of it. They had eaten there, on the grass, that day with the loaves and the fishes — the day with the five thousand, when the basketsful of bread had been gathered up after everyone had eaten and could not eat any more. They climbed it now in silence, in pairs, the way men climb when they are not sure what is at the top. The lake of Galilee lay below them as they went up. The sun was past the middle of the sky. The fishing boats on the water were small and slow at this distance, and you could see the wakes of them moving from one shore to another.

He was there when they arrived. They saw him standing in the open place near the summit, with the lake below in the distance and the country of the Decapolis far off across the water. He was not as he had been before the cross, and he was not as the gardener had seemed to Mary in the dawn. He was himself, and he was changed, and they did not have words yet for either of those things. He waited for them.

They bowed down. Some of them did so without thinking, falling on their faces. Some of them did so because they could not have done anything else — their legs gave out under them. And some of them stood at the edge of the group with their hands at their sides and looked at him and were not sure. The text does not name those ones. It does not say it was Thomas, who had already touched the wound. It does not say it was anyone in particular. It says only: some doubted. As though Matthew, looking back on that day many years later, wanted to be sure to keep the door open for the man who would read these words much later and recognize himself.

He did not separate them. He did not call them out. He did not ask them to declare themselves one way or the other before he spoke. He did not make them line up the bowing on one side and the standing on the other.

He came to them, and he stood among them, and he spoke.

"All authority has been given to me in heaven and on earth. Go, then, and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, teaching them to observe all things that I commanded you. And behold, I am with you always, even to the end of the age."

He had not asked for their certainty. He had asked for their feet.

He looked at them — at the ones who had bowed and the ones who had not. He looked at the ones whose faces were wet and the ones whose faces were not. He had been with them three years on the roads of this country. He had eaten and slept with them in the houses of fishermen and tax collectors. He knew them. He knew which of them still doubted and which of them had finally decided. He did not separate them. The promise covered both. He was sending them with the doubt still in them. He was promising to come with them anyway.

The wind moved over the mountain. The light moved on the lake far below. A bird crossed the open ground between him and the trees. Somewhere down on the shore, far below, a man was hauling a net, and another man was shouting to a third about a boat.

The eleven looked at one another. They did not all look the same as they had looked before he spoke. Some of them were no longer at the edge of the group. Some of them still were. He had not changed that. He had only added the promise that he would be with them all the way home — to the end of the age, the text says, and not one day less.

They would go from the mountain back down to the lake, and from the lake to Jerusalem, and from Jerusalem out into the cities and the roads and the ships of the world. Some of them would die in places they had never heard of when they climbed this mountain. They did not know any of that yet. They knew only what he had just said.

After a while, they began to come down. They did not all come down together. Some stayed at the top a long time. Peter sat on the grass with his hands on his knees and looked out over the lake. John sat near him. Thomas walked the perimeter of the open place. The rest moved among themselves, slowly, the way people move when they have been told a thing they will not finish hearing for the rest of their lives.

And so it was on the day Jesus sent his disciples out into the world, on the mountain in Galilee, where he had told them he would meet them.
