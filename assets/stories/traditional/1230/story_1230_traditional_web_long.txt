The prophet stood before the people. The people were a long way from home. They had set up their tents in a country that was not their country, and the years of it had been many. They were the ones who had said, in low voices that did not always reach the prophet's ear but reached it sometimes, that the Lord did not see them. That the justice due to them had been forgotten somewhere along the road and not picked up again.

The prophet had been sent to those people. He spoke now.

"Who has measured the waters in the hollow of his hand?"

He held out his own hand. A man's hand. The size of it. The hollow of it, where a few mouthfuls of water might lie. Then he set the question down beside that hand, and let it lie there.

"Who has marked off the sky with his span? Who has calculated the dust of the earth in a measure? Who has weighed the mountains in scales, and the hills in a balance?"

He set each phrase down without hurry. The sky measured by a hand's width. The dust of the whole earth held in a basket. The weight of the mountains laid out across the pans of a balance — Hermon on one side, Carmel on the other, the man at the balance setting them right. The prophet was painting a picture his hearers could not really hold inside their eyes. He painted it anyway. The largeness of what was being weighed was the whole point.

"Who has directed the Spirit of the Lord? Or has taught him as his counselor? Who did he take counsel with, and who instructed him, and taught him in the path of justice, and taught him knowledge, and showed him the way of understanding?"

No one. The prophet did not say no one. He let the silence say it.

Then he turned to the nations. The great kingdoms. The men who sat on thrones in distant courts and decided the fates of peoples like the prophet's own.

"Behold, the nations are like a drop in a bucket. They are regarded as a speck of dust on a balance. Behold, he lifts up the islands like a very little thing. Lebanon is not sufficient to burn, nor its animals sufficient for a burnt offering. All the nations are like nothing before him. They are regarded by him as less than nothing, and vanity."

The drop in the bucket. The speck of dust on the scales. The islands lifted like pebbles in a man's hand. The cedars of Lebanon — the forests that crowned the north and roofed the temples of kings — not enough fuel for the altar of the One who sat above all of it. The prophet was naming the things his hearers had feared, and showing them the size those things were when they were set against the Lord.

"To whom then will you liken God? Or what likeness will you compare to him?"

Some of his hearers, he knew, had seen the workmen at their forges. A workman casting an image. A goldsmith overlaying it with gold. Casting silver chains for it, so it would not fall over. A poor man who could not afford even that, choosing a tree that would not rot, finding a skillful workman to carve him a figure that would stand still in his house. The figure stood. The figure was hammered into stillness. The figure did not measure the waters. The figure did not call the stars by name. The figure could be set up, and the figure could be knocked down.

The prophet did not say all of that aloud. He did not need to. The picture was already in his hearers' eyes.

Then the voice that was not his own came on through him.

"It is he who sits above the circle of the earth, and its inhabitants are like grasshoppers — who stretches out the heavens like a curtain, and spreads them out like a tent to dwell in, who brings princes to nothing, who makes the judges of the earth meaningless. They are planted scarcely. They are sown scarcely. Their stock has scarcely taken root in the ground. He merely blows on them, and they wither, and the whirlwind takes them away as stubble."

The princes who had carried Jacob into exile. The judges who had handed down the sentence. Stubble. Blown across the threshing floor. Gone.

"To whom then will you liken me? Who is my equal? — says the Holy One."

The Holy One was not done speaking. The voice came on.

"Lift up your eyes on high, and see who has created these. Who brings out their army by number. He calls them all by name. By the greatness of his might, and because he is strong in power — not one is lacking."

The prophet pointed. He pointed upward. The stars above the people's tents. The great army of light marching out each night across the sky. Each one called by name. Each one accounted for. The watchman at the gate of an army knows the name of every soldier in his charge. The Lord of hosts was that watchman, and the heavens were his ranks, and not one of them was missing.

Then the prophet turned to the voice that had been muttering inside the camp — the voice that had brought him here in the first place.

"Why do you say, Jacob, and speak, Israel — 'My way is hidden from the Lord, and the justice due me is disregarded by my God'? Have you not known? Have you not heard?"

He said it twice. He said it the way a teacher repeats a thing that should already have been remembered. The voice inside the camp was not a voice from outside. It was the voice of the people he had been sent to. The grief of the long road and the long years was inside that voice. The prophet did not condemn the grief. He answered it.

"The everlasting God, the Lord, the Creator of the ends of the earth, doesn't faint. He isn't weary. His understanding is unsearchable."

Then he brought it down to the bodies of those who were hearing him.

"He gives power to the weak. He increases the strength of him who has no might. Even the youths faint and get weary, and the young men utterly fall."

The strongest among them. The ones who should have been able to carry the rest. They went down. The prophet did not soften it.

"But those who wait for the Lord will renew their strength. They will mount up with wings like eagles. They will run, and not be weary. They will walk, and not faint."

The last word fell on the still air. The camp held it. Over their tents, the stars went on burning, each one called by name, none of them missing.
