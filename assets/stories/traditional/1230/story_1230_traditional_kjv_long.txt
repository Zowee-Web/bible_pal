The prophet stood before the people. The people were a long way from home. They had set up their tents in a country that was not their country, and the years of it had been many. They were they that had said, in low voices that did not always reach the prophet's ear but reached it sometimes, that the Lord did not see them. That the judgment due unto them had been forgotten somewhere along the road and not taken up again.

The prophet had been sent unto those people. He spake now.

"Who hath measured the waters in the hollow of his hand?"

He held out his own hand. A man's hand. The size of it. The hollow of it, wherein a few mouthfuls of water might lie. Then he set the question down beside that hand, and let it lie there.

"And meted out heaven with the span? Who hath comprehended the dust of the earth in a measure? Who hath weighed the mountains in scales, and the hills in a balance?"

He set each phrase down without haste. The heaven measured by a hand's width. The dust of the whole earth held within a basket. The weight of the mountains laid out across the pans of a balance — Hermon upon one side, Carmel upon the other, the man at the balance setting them right. The prophet was painting a picture his hearers could not truly hold within their eyes. He painted it notwithstanding. The largeness of what was being weighed was the whole point.

"Who hath directed the Spirit of the Lord, or being his counsellor hath taught him? With whom took he counsel, and who instructed him, and taught him in the path of judgment, and taught him knowledge, and shewed to him the way of understanding?"

None. The prophet said not, None. He let the silence say it.

Then he turned unto the nations. The great kingdoms. The men that sat upon thrones in distant courts and decreed the fates of peoples like the prophet's own.

"Behold, the nations are as a drop of a bucket, and are counted as the small dust of the balance. Behold, he taketh up the isles as a very little thing. And Lebanon is not sufficient to burn, nor the beasts thereof sufficient for a burnt offering. All nations before him are as nothing; and they are counted to him less than nothing, and vanity."

The drop of a bucket. The small dust upon the scales. The isles taken up as pebbles in a man's hand. The cedars of Lebanon — the forests that crowned the north and roofed the temples of kings — not sufficient fuel for the altar of the One that sat above all of it. The prophet was naming the things his hearers had feared, and shewing them the size those things were when they were set against the Lord.

"To whom then will ye liken God? Or what likeness will ye compare unto him?"

Some of his hearers, he knew, had seen the workmen at their forges. A workman melting a graven image. A goldsmith spreading it over with gold. Casting silver chains for it, that it should not fall over. A poor man, so impoverished he could not afford even that, choosing a tree that would not rot, seeking unto him a cunning workman to prepare him a graven image that should not be moved. The image stood. The image was hammered into stillness. The image measured not the waters. The image called not the stars by name. The image could be set up, and the image could be cast down.

The prophet said not all of that aloud. He had no need to. The picture was already within his hearers' eyes.

Then the voice that was not his own came on through him.

"It is he that sitteth upon the circle of the earth, and the inhabitants thereof are as grasshoppers — that stretcheth out the heavens as a curtain, and spreadeth them out as a tent to dwell in. That bringeth the princes to nothing; he maketh the judges of the earth as vanity. Yea, they shall not be planted; yea, they shall not be sown: yea, their stock shall not take root in the earth. And he shall also blow upon them, and they shall wither, and the whirlwind shall take them away as stubble."

The princes that had carried Jacob into exile. The judges that had handed down the sentence. Stubble. Blown across the threshing-floor. Gone.

"To whom then will ye liken me, or shall I be equal? — saith the Holy One."

The Holy One had not ceased speaking. The voice came on.

"Lift up your eyes on high, and behold who hath created these things, that bringeth out their host by number. He calleth them all by names. By the greatness of his might, for that he is strong in power — not one faileth."

The prophet pointed. He pointed upward. The stars above the people's tents. The great host of light marching forth each night across the sky. Each one called by name. Each one accounted for. The watchman at the gate of a host knoweth the name of every soldier in his charge. The Lord of hosts was that watchman, and the heavens were his ranks, and not one of them was wanting.

Then the prophet turned unto the voice that had been muttering within the camp — the voice that had brought him hither in the first place.

"Why sayest thou, O Jacob, and speakest, O Israel — 'My way is hid from the Lord, and my judgment is passed over from my God'? Hast thou not known? Hast thou not heard?"

He said it twice. He said it the way a teacher repeateth a thing that should already have been remembered. The voice within the camp was not a voice from without. It was the voice of the people he had been sent unto. The grief of the long road and the long years was within that voice. The prophet did not condemn the grief. He answered it.

"The everlasting God, the Lord, the Creator of the ends of the earth, fainteth not, neither is weary. There is no searching of his understanding."

Then he brought it down unto the bodies of them that heard him.

"He giveth power to the faint; and to them that have no might he increaseth strength. Even the youths shall faint and be weary, and the young men shall utterly fall."

The strongest among them. They that should have been able to bear up the rest. They went down. The prophet softened it not.

"But they that wait upon the Lord shall renew their strength. They shall mount up with wings as eagles. They shall run, and not be weary. They shall walk, and not faint."

The last word fell upon the still air. The camp held it. Above their tents, the stars went on burning, each one called by name, none of them wanting.
