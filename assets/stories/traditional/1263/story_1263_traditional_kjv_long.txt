The afternoon was wearing on toward evening when the footsteps came. Peter and John had been speaking in the temple court, in the broad pavement that ran along the colonnade of Solomon. The man which was healed stood at Peter's side. The crowd had grown wide round about them — men which had laid down their bundles to hearken, women which had drawn the corners of their veils closer against the lowering sun, boys leaning forward with their mouths open. Peter was speaking of the resurrection. He was speaking the name of Jesus.

The footsteps came from the inner court. The priests came first, their robes hushing the stones. Behind them walked the captain of the temple, his belt drawn tight across his chest, his eyes counting the crowd as he came. After him, the Sadducees, in their long robes of fine linen, their faces set in the look men wear when somewhat hath been said which they cannot suffer to stand.

They came upon Peter and John. The captain raised his hand. The temple guards laid their hands on the two men. They took them by the arms. The crowd drew back. The man which was healed was left standing alone for a moment in the open space, and then he too was led aside.

The captain spake without looking at Peter. "It is now eventide. Put them in hold unto the morrow."

The colonnade had thrown its long shadow halfway across the court. The doors of the holding chamber opened, and Peter and John were led in, and the doors were shut behind them.

But the word they had been speaking did not close with the doors. Out in the court, in the houses along the lower city, men which had heard the speech turned one to another. They named the name they had heard. They asked one another what the speech had been. Many of them which heard the word believed. By the time the lamps were lit, the number of the men was come to be about five thousand.

The morning came grey and cool. The chamber of the council was already filled when the guards brought Peter and John in. Annas the high priest sat at the head, his beard combed white against the dark of his robe, his palms laid flat on the carven arms of his seat. Caiaphas was at his right hand. John was on the bench. Alexander was beside him. As many as were of the kindred of the high priest filled the seats along the curve of the half-circle. The scribes stood against the wall with their tablets and their styluses. The lamps burned in the iron brackets, their flames steady in the still air.

The guards set Peter and John in the midst.

They stood there in the rough robes of fishermen. The dust of the holding chamber was on the hems. Their hands hung at their sides. Their faces were lifted. They did not lower their eyes to the stone floor. They did not bow their heads.

Annas spake first. "By what power, or by what name, have ye done this?"

The chamber waited. The scribes lowered their styluses to the wax. The councilman nearest the door drew a slow breath.

Then Peter, filled with the Holy Ghost, lifted up his voice.

"Ye rulers of the people, and elders of Israel," he said. "If we this day be examined of the good deed done to the impotent man, by what means he is made whole — be it known unto you all, and to all the people of Israel, that by the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, whom ye crucified, whom God raised from the dead, even by him doth this man stand here before you whole."

The chamber did not move. Peter's voice was clear. It carried the level cadence of a man who had stood by a fire one night, and denied a name three times, and then heard the cock crow. It was not that voice now. It was the voice of a man which had been with Jesus on the other side of an empty tomb.

"This is the stone," he said, "which was set at nought of you builders, which is become the head of the corner."

A councilman in the second row laid his palm flat against his thigh. Annas did not look away.

"Neither is there salvation in any other," Peter said. "For there is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved."

The words came down through the chamber like a level stone laid into its place. The scribes' styluses had stopped moving. The councilmen beheld Peter. They beheld John beside him.

They saw the boldness of Peter and John. They perceived that they were unlearned and ignorant men — fishermen, men which had never sat at the feet of the rabbis of the city, men which could not read the law in the tongue wherein it was spoken at Sinai. And yet the speech had come out steady. The cadence had been the cadence of one who knew the writings as a man knoweth the courtyard of his own house.

They marvelled. They took knowledge of them, that they had been with Jesus.

Then their eyes moved further. The man which had been impotent — the man which had been laid daily at the gate called Beautiful, the man whose face every priest had seen for years as he passed in and out of the temple, the man who was above forty years old — was standing among the witnesses. He stood on his own two feet. The councilmen beheld his ankles where the hem of his robe lifted as he shifted his weight. They had known those ankles when they could not bear his weight. They beheld the man which was healed standing with them. They could say nothing against it.

Annas raised his hand.

"Take them aside," he said.

The guards led Peter and John out through a side door. The man which was healed went with them. The doors closed. The council was left to itself.

The murmur began low. It moved along the curved tiers.

"What shall we do to these men?"

"A notable miracle hath been done by them. It is manifest unto all them that dwell in Jerusalem."

"We cannot deny it."

A pause. The lamps burned on in their iron brackets.

Then a voice, lower. "But that it spread no further among the people — let us straitly threaten them, that they speak henceforth to no man in this name."

The council weighed it. The high priest nodded. The matter was settled around the bench. The guards were sent to the side door.

The doors opened again. Peter and John were brought back. They were set once more in the midst.

The high priest spake the council's word. They commanded the two men not to speak at all nor teach in the name of Jesus.

The chamber waited.

Peter and John did not lower their eyes. They looked along the curved benches at the rulers of the people, at the elders, at the scribes against the wall.

"Whether it be right in the sight of God to hearken unto you more than unto God, judge ye," they said.

A councilman drew his robe tighter at his shoulder.

"For we cannot but speak the things which we have seen and heard."

The words rested on the stone floor between them. The council had no answer ready. They threatened them further. They could find nothing how they might punish them, because of the people. For all men in the courts and the streets of the city glorified God for that which was done. The man on whom this miracle of healing was shewed was above forty years old.

The doors were opened.

Peter and John walked out of the chamber. The man which was healed walked beside them. The morning was fuller now over the rooftops of Jerusalem. The priests which had risen early were going about their work. The merchants were opening their stalls along the lower street. The healed man set one foot before the other on the stones of the temple court, the same stones he had been carried over for so many years, and he walked on his own.

Behind them, in the chamber, the council was rising. The scribes were folding their tablets. The doors of the temple stood open unto the new day. And the name which the council had commanded them not to speak was already on the lips of the men in the court, and on the lips of the women at the wells, and in the houses where the bread was being broken before the heat of the day came on.
