The Lord spake unto Moses and appointed feasts for Israel to proclaim as holy convocations. The order began with every week: work might be done for six days, but the seventh was a sabbath of solemn rest. No work was to be done in any dwelling, for the sabbath belonged unto the Lord.

The yearly appointments began in the first month. The Lord's passover fell on the fourteenth day at even. The Feast of Unleavened Bread followed on the fifteenth and continued seven days. The people were to eat unleavened bread, gather in holy convocation on the first and seventh days, set servile work aside, and offer gifts made by fire unto the Lord throughout the feast.

The Lord then looked ahead to the land he was giving Israel. When its harvest was reaped, the first sheaf was to be brought unto the priest and waved before the Lord on the morrow after the sabbath. An he lamb of the first year without blemish was offered with it, along with fine flour mingled with oil and the appointed wine. Bread, parched corn, and green ears were not to be eaten until the offering had been presented unto God. The harvest was received under his command before it was enjoyed.

From the day of the wave offering, Israel counted seven complete sabbaths, numbering fifty days in all even unto the morrow after the seventh sabbath. A new meat offering marked the Feast of Weeks: two loaves baken with leaven and presented as firstfruits. Lambs, a bullock, and rams were brought as burnt offerings with their meat and drink offerings. A kid of the goats served as a sin offering, and two lambs as peace offerings. The priest waved the loaves and lambs before the Lord, and the day was proclaimed an holy convocation without servile work.

The command for harvest also guarded provision for those without a field of their own. Israel was not to make clean riddance of every corner or gather every gleaning. What remained was left unto the poor and the stranger, under the authority of the Lord their God.

The seventh month brought another course of holy days. On its first day Israel observed a sabbath, a memorial of blowing trumpets, an holy convocation, and an offering unto the Lord. On the tenth came the Day of Atonement. The people were to afflict their souls and do no manner of work while atonement was made for them before the Lord. The command carried a grave warning for any soul who refused either affliction or rest. From even on the ninth day unto the next even, the day was kept as a sabbath of rest.

On the fifteenth day the Feast of Tabernacles began and lasted seven days. The first day was an holy convocation without servile work. Offerings were brought for seven days; then an eighth day of solemn assembly and rest closed the observance. These offerings were given beside the regular sabbaths, gifts, vows, and freewill offerings.

After the fruit of the land had been gathered, Israel took boughs of goodly trees, palm branches, thick boughs, and willows of the brook and rejoiced before the Lord for seven days. Israelites born lived in booths throughout the feast. That practice carried the memory of the Lord's deliverance: later generations were to know that he made the children of Israel dwell in booths when he brought them out of the land of Egypt.

Moses declared the feasts of the Lord unto the children of Israel.
