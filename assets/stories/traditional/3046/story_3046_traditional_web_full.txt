Yahweh spoke to Moses and appointed times for Israel to proclaim as holy convocations. The rhythm began with every week: work could be done for six days, but the seventh was a Sabbath of solemn rest. No work was to be done in any dwelling, for the Sabbath belonged to Yahweh.

The yearly appointments began in the first month. Yahweh's Passover fell on the fourteenth day at evening. The Feast of Unleavened Bread followed on the fifteenth and continued for seven days. The people were to eat unleavened bread, gather in holy convocation on the first and seventh days, set regular work aside, and offer gifts by fire to Yahweh throughout the feast.

Yahweh then looked ahead to the land he was giving Israel. When its harvest was reaped, the first sheaf was to be brought to the priest and waved before Yahweh on the day after the Sabbath. A year-old male lamb without defect was offered with it, along with fine flour mixed with oil and the appointed wine. Bread, roasted grain, and fresh grain were not to be eaten until the offering had been presented to God. The harvest was received under his command before it was enjoyed.

From the day of the wave offering, Israel counted seven complete Sabbaths, fifty days in all, reaching the day after the seventh Sabbath. A new meal offering marked the Feast of Weeks: two loaves baked with yeast and presented as firstfruits. Lambs, a bull, and rams were brought as burnt offerings with their meal and drink offerings. A male goat served as a sin offering, and two male lambs as peace offerings. The priest waved the loaves and lambs before Yahweh, and the day was proclaimed a holy convocation without regular work.

The command for harvest also guarded provision for those without a field of their own. Israel was not to reap every corner or gather every gleaning. What remained was left for the poor and the foreigner, under the authority of Yahweh their God.

The seventh month brought another sequence of holy days. On its first day Israel observed solemn rest, a memorial marked by trumpet blasts, a holy convocation, and an offering to Yahweh. On the tenth came the Day of Atonement. The people were to deny themselves and do no kind of work while atonement was made for them before Yahweh. The command carried a grave warning for anyone who refused either self-denial or rest. From evening on the ninth day until the next evening, the day was kept as a Sabbath of solemn rest.

On the fifteenth day the Feast of Booths began and lasted seven days. The first day was a holy convocation without regular work. Offerings were brought for seven days; then an eighth day of solemn assembly and rest closed the observance. These offerings were given in addition to the regular Sabbaths, gifts, vows, and freewill offerings.

After the produce of the land had been gathered, Israel took fruit from majestic trees, palm branches, thick boughs, and willows from the brook and rejoiced before Yahweh for seven days. Native-born Israelites lived in temporary shelters throughout the feast. That practice carried the memory of Yahweh's deliverance: future generations were to know that he made the children of Israel dwell in shelters when he brought them out of Egypt.

Moses declared Yahweh's feasts to the children of Israel.
