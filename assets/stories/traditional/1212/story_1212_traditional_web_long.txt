It was the seventh month, the twenty-first day of the month — the last day of the feast.

The people of Judah were on the temple mount. The foundation of the second temple had been laid. The lower courses of stone had been set, and were set straight, and were holding. The work was rising around the floor of what had once been the most beautiful house in the land. New stone. New mortar. The sound of mallets against chisels carried across the mount, and the sound of men calling to one another across the courses, and the sound of a winch hauling up timber from below.

The new building was small. The men who were working on it could see that. The cedar that was being raised was not the cedar of Lebanon that Solomon had brought up by oxcart. The pillars were not the bronze pillars of Boaz and Jachin. The floor was not overlaid with gold.

Some of the old men had stopped working. They were leaning on their staffs at the edge of the work. They were looking at the stones that were going up, and they were looking past the stones at the empty air where Solomon's temple had stood — the great rooms, the layered courts, the doors plated with gold. The new house was not going to be that house. The old men knew it. One of them shut his eyes. One of them did not.

Then the word of the Lord came to Haggai the prophet.

Haggai walked up into the work site. He found Zerubbabel the son of Shealtiel, governor of Judah, with a stone-tool in his hand and dust along the front of his robe. He found Joshua the son of Jehozadak, the high priest, who had set down his rule of measure to listen. He found the remnant of the people, who had come close in around the prophet when they saw he was about to speak. The work stopped. The voices stopped. A length of cedar that had been swinging from the winch was set carefully down on the stones.

The prophet lifted his face. He looked at the old men first.

"Who is left among you who saw this house in its former glory?" he said. "How do you see it now? Isn't it in your eyes as nothing?"

The old men did not answer. Some of them lowered their faces. Others kept looking at the stones. The man who had shut his eyes opened them again. The man who had not shut them did not move.

"Yet now be strong, Zerubbabel," the prophet said. "Be strong, Joshua son of Jehozadak, the high priest. Be strong, all you people of the land. And work. For I am with you, says the Lord of Armies."

The word "work" landed in the still air. Zerubbabel turned his head toward the prophet. The high priest did not move.

"This is the word that I covenanted with you when you came out of Egypt," the prophet said, "and my Spirit lived among you. Don't be afraid."

Then the prophet's voice changed. He went on.

"Yet once, it is a little while, and I will shake the heavens, the earth, the sea, and the dry land. I will shake all nations. The precious things of all nations will come, and I will fill this house with glory, says the Lord of Armies."

The prophet looked across the small stones. He spoke the next words slowly.

"The silver is mine, and the gold is mine, says the Lord of Armies."

He looked at the old men again.

"The latter glory of this house will be greater than the former, says the Lord of Armies. In this place I will give peace."

The old men did not weep openly. But the hands of one of them, which had been holding the staff loose, tightened on it until the knuckles whitened. The hand of another, which had hung at his side, came up and pressed against his chest. The new building did not become Solomon's temple. The new building did not need to. Something else was being said about it.

Haggai finished speaking. The remnant did not move at once. Then Zerubbabel turned back to his stone. He picked up his tool. The high priest reached for his rule of measure. The man at the winch took the rope again. The cedar that had been set down was lifted back into the air. The work began again.

Two months passed.

The walls went up another course. The cedar was raised, joint by joint. The lamps inside the new rooms were lit at evening. The trumpet of the priests sounded at the appointed hours over the work site, and the men listened to it, and the men kept working. The old men came to the work in the mornings. They stood at the edge. They watched. They went home in the evenings. They came back the next day.

On the twenty-fourth day of the ninth month, the word of the Lord came to Haggai a second time. The prophet went to Zerubbabel where he stood among the workers. The workers gathered again to hear. The work stopped, the way it had stopped before.

"I will shake the heavens and the earth," the prophet said. "I will overthrow the throne of kingdoms. I will destroy the strength of the kingdoms of the nations. I will overthrow the chariots and those who ride in them. The horses and their riders will come down, every one by the sword of his brother."

Zerubbabel did not speak. The workers were still. A wind moved across the stones.

"In that day, says the Lord of Armies, I will take you, Zerubbabel my servant, the son of Shealtiel, says the Lord, and will make you like a signet ring. For I have chosen you, says the Lord of Armies."

The prophet stopped speaking. Zerubbabel lowered his face. The workers did not move for a long moment. Then the high priest picked up his rule of measure. The man at the winch took the rope again. The work went on.
