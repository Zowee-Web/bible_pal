Six days had passed since Jesus had spoken to them about what was coming. On the morning of the seventh day, he chose three. Peter. James. John. He told them to come with him, and he led them away from the others toward a high mountain.

The trail narrowed as they climbed. Loose stone shifted beneath their sandals. The air grew thinner and cooler. No one else was with them. The villages below shrank to small shapes against the valley floor, and the sound of people and animals faded until there was only wind and the scrape of their own footsteps on rock.

They reached the top. The sky stretched wide and open around them. Jesus stood apart.

Then his appearance changed.

It happened in front of their eyes. His clothes became white — not the white of washed linen or bleached cloth, but a burning, clean brightness that no worker on earth could produce. The fabric shone as though lit from inside. His whole form was different, transformed, and the three men stood where they were and stared.

Two figures appeared beside him. Moses stood there. Elijah stood there. Both of them were speaking with Jesus, their voices carrying across the open ground of the summit. The disciples could see their faces clearly, could hear the low steady sound of the conversation, though the wind moved around them all.

Peter opened his mouth. His voice shook when it came.

"Master, it is good for us to be here. Let us make three shelters — one for you, one for Moses, one for Elijah."

He did not know what he was saying. The words tumbled out because he was deeply afraid, and James and John were afraid beside him, their shoulders rigid, their breath short.

Then a cloud came down over them.

It was not the slow drift of weather moving across a ridge. It came and it covered them, thick and close, blocking the sky and the light. Shadow pressed in from every side. The air went heavy. The three disciples could not see beyond their own hands.

A voice spoke out of the cloud.

"This is my beloved Son. Hear him."

The sound filled the mountaintop. It moved through the cloud and through their chests and into the stone under their feet. Peter dropped his gaze. James went still. John did not move.

Then, suddenly, the cloud lifted. Light returned. The wind blew clean across the summit again.

They looked around. Moses was gone. Elijah was gone. No one else stood on the mountain. Only Jesus, alone with them, his clothes no longer blazing, the ordinary dust of the trail on his sandals. He looked at them and they looked back.

He turned and began to walk down.

The descent was steep. The rocks were dry and loose under their feet. The sun was warm on their necks. As they made their way down the trail, Jesus spoke to them.

He told them not to say anything about what they had seen. Not yet. Not until the Son of Man had risen from the dead.

They heard his words and kept them. But the three of them glanced at one another as they walked, and quietly, between themselves, they turned the phrase over. Rising from the dead. They asked each other what it could mean. None of them had an answer. They kept walking.

Then they put the question to Jesus directly.

"Why do the scribes say that Elijah must come first?"

Jesus answered without stopping. His feet were steady on the downward path, his voice clear against the open air. "Elijah does come first," he said, "and restores all things." He paused. "And how is it written of the Son of Man? That he must suffer many things and be treated as nothing."

The trail bent around an outcrop of pale stone. A hawk turned in the air far below them.

"But I tell you," Jesus said, "Elijah has already come. And they did to him whatever they wanted, just as it is written of him."

The three disciples walked behind him, single file on the narrow path. The mountain rose steep and silent at their backs. Below them the valley opened wide, the road leading back toward the towns and the crowds and the dust. Jesus walked ahead, and they followed him down.
