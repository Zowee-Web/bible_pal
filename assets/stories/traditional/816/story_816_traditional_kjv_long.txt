In the grand city of Babylon, beneath vaulted ceilings and behind walls of stone, the palace of King Darius bustled with life and purpose. King Darius, ruler over an immense realm, appointed an hundred and twenty princes to oversee his kingdom. Over these, he set three presidents, that the king's interests might be preserved. Among the three, Daniel was preferred, an exile from Judah, respected and wise. His excellent spirit and faithfulness drew the king's favour, and soon Darius thought to set Daniel over the whole realm. Word of the king's intention spread quickly, and whispers passed among the officials and governors. Some looked upon Daniel's advancement with envy, troubled that an outsider should be set above the native Babylonians.

These men, eager to bring Daniel low, sought occasion against him concerning the kingdom. Day after day, they examined his records, sought error in his judgments, hearkened for careless words, yet found no fault. Daniel was neither corrupt nor negligent; his work was above reproach. Frustrated, the officials gathered in private and spake in hushed tones. "We shall not find any occasion against this Daniel, except we find it against him concerning the law of his God." The men knew that Daniel's stedfast devotion unto the Lord of Israel guided every action and prayer of his day.

Soon a device took form. The officials and satraps approached King Darius with words of flattery and unity. "O King Darius, live for ever," they said as they bowed low before him. "All the presidents of the kingdom, the governors, and the princes, the counsellors, and the captains, have consulted together to establish a royal statute, and to make a firm decree, that whosoever shall ask a petition of any God or man for thirty days, save of thee, O king, he shall be cast into the den of lions. Now, O king, establish the decree, and sign the writing, that it be not changed, according to the law of the Medes and Persians, which altereth not." The king, flattered and trusting his advisors, signed the writing and the decree. The decree became law, unchangeable and sealed with his authority.

Now when Daniel knew that the writing was signed, he made no change to his custom. Each day, as had been his manner from his youth, he went up unto his chamber. The windows being open toward Jerusalem, Daniel kneeled upon the cool stones three times a day. He prayed and gave thanks before his God, unwavering despite the peril. Through the lattice, the sun cast its glow upon him as he spake quietly unto the Lord, his prayers borne like gentle breath upon the drifting air.

But the officials, waiting for this act, assembled together and hasted unto Daniel's quarters. From the hallway of the palace, through parted curtains, they beheld Daniel upon his knees in supplication. Their eyes hardened with satisfaction, and they entered at once, gathering the witness they needed. Without delay, they came before the king with their charge. "O king, hast thou not signed a decree, that every man that shall ask a petition of any God or man within thirty days, save of thee, O king, shall be cast into the den of lions?" Darius answered, "The thing is true, according to the law of the Medes and Persians, which altereth not."

The officers pressed on. "That Daniel, which is of the children of the captivity of Judah, regardeth not thee, O king, nor the decree that thou hast signed, but maketh his petition three times a day." At these words, King Darius was sore displeased with himself. He valued Daniel above others and laboured all the day to deliver him. The heavy weight of the law pressed hard. The going down of the sun drew nigh, and with it, the men returned, putting the king in remembrance that no decree nor statute the king establisheth could be altered. Bound by the decree, King Darius was powerless before his own law.

Then the king commanded, and they brought Daniel, and cast him into the den of lions. The ancient, iron-studded door to the pit creaked open, and the chill of evening swept over Daniel as he was lowered down. The cries and growls of hungry lions echoed below, their shadows flickering in the torchlight. Before the stone was rolled across the entrance, King Darius cried down unto Daniel, his voice heavy with sorrow and reluctant hope. "Thy God, whom thou servest continually, he will deliver thee." The officials laid the stone upon the mouth of the den, and the king sealed it with his own signet, and with the signet of his lords, that the purpose might not be changed concerning Daniel.

Night settled upon the palace like a shroud. In the king's chamber, sleep went from Darius. He passed the night fasting, neither were instruments of musick brought before him; his heart weighted by concern for Daniel. In the stillness, he paced, waiting for the dawn. Down in the den, among the scattered bones and cold stones, Daniel waited in peace. Though the breath of the lions was hot, and their bodies pressed about him, they did him no hurt. For an angel of God descended, silent and unseen, standing between Daniel and the jaws of danger. Through the long hours, the mouths of the lions remained shut, and Daniel, faithful and brave, trusted his God as the night wore on.

Then the king arose very early in the morning, hope and apprehension warring within him, and went in haste unto the den. And when he came to the den, he cried with a lamentable voice, "O Daniel, servant of the living God, is thy God, whom thou servest continually, able to deliver thee from the lions?" From within the pit came Daniel's calm reply, "O king, live for ever. My God hath sent his angel, and hath shut the lions' mouths, that they have not hurt me: forasmuch as before him innocency was found in me; and also before thee, O king, have I done no hurt."

Relief and wonder flooded Darius. He commanded that Daniel be taken up out of the den. When Daniel was brought forth, no manner of hurt was found upon him, because he believed in his God. The officials which had accused Daniel stood nearby. In the presence of all, the king gave commandment, and those men, with their children and their wives, were cast into the den of lions. Or ever they came at the bottom of the den, the lions had the mastery of them, and brake all their bones in pieces.

After these things, King Darius wrote unto all peoples, nations, and languages, that dwelt in all the earth, proclaiming the greatness of the God of Daniel. "Peace be multiplied unto you. I make a decree, That in every dominion of my kingdom men tremble and fear before the God of Daniel: for he is the living God, and stedfast for ever, and his kingdom that which shall not be destroyed, and his dominion shall be even unto the end. He delivereth and rescueth, and he worketh signs and wonders in heaven and in earth, who hath delivered Daniel from the power of the lions."

So this Daniel prospered in the reign of Darius, and in the reign of Cyrus the Persian.

And though the memories of the lions' den lingered, Daniel met each new day with quiet gratitude. Hope stirred among the people, who saw that stedfast faith could outlast any night, and trust might grow strong, even within the shadow of the captivity.
