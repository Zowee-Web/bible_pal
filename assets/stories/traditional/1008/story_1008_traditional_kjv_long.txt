The body of Moses lay upon the mountain, and the mourning of Israel had not yet ceased. The tents of the encampment stretched wide across the plains of Moab, and the dust of that dry country settled upon every surface — upon the ropes of goat-hair, upon the folded garments laid at tent-openings, upon the sandals of men and women who walked with heavy steps between the rows. Fires burned low in the early hours, their smoke rising thin and grey against a pale sky. The sound of weeping had carried through the camp for thirty days, and now, in the stillness that followed, the air itself seemed to hold its breath.

Joshua the son of Nun stood among the tents. His hands were roughened from years of labor and war, his cloak worn at the hem from the long sojourn in the wilderness. He had served Moses from his youth — had stood at the door of the tabernacle, had walked beside the ark, had gone up into the hill country with sword and shield when the Amalekites came against Israel. Now Moses was dead, and the weight of all that lay ahead pressed upon the camp like the heat of midday.

Then the voice of the LORD came unto Joshua.

It came not with thunder, not with the shaking of the ground, but it came, and it was heard. The LORD spoke plainly: Moses His servant was dead. Now therefore Joshua was to arise and go over this Jordan — he and all this people — unto the land which the LORD did give to the children of Israel.

The Jordan lay eastward, its waters running brown and swift in the season of overflow, cutting through the low valley between banks of clay and tangled root. Beyond it rose the land — terraced hills, walled cities set upon high places, fields of grain tended by peoples who had dwelt there for generations. The distance between the camp and the river was not great. A man standing at the edge of the encampment could see the line of green that marked its course.

The LORD declared unto Joshua that every place upon which the sole of his foot should tread, that land had been given unto them, even as He had spoken unto Moses. The boundaries were laid out with the sureness of a surveyor's cord. From the wilderness southward, from Lebanon in the north — from the great river, the river Euphrates, spanning all the land of the Hittites, unto the great sea toward the going down of the sun — this was the territory marked for Israel's possession.

No description of distant kingdoms or foreign courts accompanied these words. The LORD named the places as a man names landmarks upon a road he hath walked many times. The wilderness. Lebanon. The Euphrates. The great sea. These were directions given to a commander standing at the threshold of a campaign, and they were spoken with the authority of one who held every boundary in His hand.

Then the LORD spoke a thing concerning the days ahead. There should not any man be able to stand before Joshua all the days of his life. As the LORD had been with Moses, so He would be with Joshua. He would not fail him, nor forsake him.

The camp around Joshua did not stir at these words. The goats pulled at their tethers. A child's voice rose and fell somewhere among the far tents. The wind moved across the plain, carrying the scent of dry grass and the faint mineral tang of the river. But the words had been spoken, and they hung in the air with the permanence of stone set into mortar.

The LORD continued. Joshua was to be strong and of a good courage, for unto this people he would divide for an inheritance the land which the LORD had sworn unto their fathers to give them. The task was named clearly — not conquest alone, but the dividing of the land afterward, the apportioning of territory among the tribes, the settling of a people who had known nothing but tents and wandering for forty years.

Then the LORD spoke again, and the instruction turned from the land to the law. Joshua was to be strong and very courageous, that he might observe to do according to all the law which Moses His servant had commanded him. He was to turn not from it to the right hand nor to the left, that he might prosper whithersoever he went.

The words were precise. The law of Moses — the commandments written upon tablets, the statutes spoken at Sinai and repeated in the plains of Moab — these were not to be set aside in the press of battle or the labor of settlement. The book of the law was not to depart out of Joshua's mouth. He was to meditate therein day and night, that he might observe to do according to all that was written therein. For then he would make his way prosperous, and then he would have good success.

Day and night. The phrase carried the full span of a man's waking and sleeping hours — the hours before dawn when the camp was dark and only the sentries moved between the rows, and the hours of bright noon when the dust rose white under the feet of marching columns, and the hours of evening when the fires were kindled and the smoke drifted upward into a sky the color of hammered bronze. In all these hours, the law was to remain upon Joshua's lips and in his keeping.

A third time the LORD commanded him to be strong and of a good courage. He was not to be afraid, neither was he to be dismayed. For the LORD his God was with him whithersoever he went.

Three times the command had come. Three times the words strong and courageous had been spoken over the man who stood in the dust of Moab with the Jordan before him and the walled cities beyond. The repetition was not ornament. It was the voice of the LORD setting a charge upon His servant with the weight and deliberation of a covenant oath.

The camp stretched out behind Joshua — thousands of tents, the smoke of cookfires, the lowing of cattle, the murmur of voices in every tribe and clan. The tabernacle stood at the center, its curtains of blue and purple and scarlet visible above the surrounding enclosures, the bronze of its altar catching what light fell upon it. The ark of the covenant rested within, carried by the Levites through every stage of the wilderness journey, borne upon poles of acacia wood overlaid with gold.

Before Joshua lay the river. Its sound carried faintly on the wind — the low, constant rush of water over stone and silt. Beyond the river, the land rose. The walls of Jericho stood in the distance, thick and high, their stones fitted close. Watchmen moved upon those walls. Fields of barley ripened in the lowlands. The roads were hard-packed and well-traveled.

All of this lay before the man to whom the LORD had spoken. The mourning for Moses had ended. The command had been given. The boundaries had been named. The law had been set as the measure of every step. The promise of the LORD's presence had been declared — not once, but woven through every line of the charge like thread through the warp of a loom.

Joshua stood upon the plain. The dust of Moab was beneath his feet. The Jordan ran before him. And the voice of the LORD had spoken.
