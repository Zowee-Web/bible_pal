A river stands ahead, wide and uncrossable by any ordinary measure. Behind stretches a wilderness that has become, over forty years, something almost like home. And in that narrow space between what was and what will be, a voice speaks — not with new instructions, but with repetition. *Be strong and of a good courage.* Three times the phrase returns, as though the speaker knows how deeply fear can settle into bones that have walked a long time without arriving.

Moses is gone. The weight of that absence fills the opening verses like silence fills a room after someone beloved has left it. Yet the commission does not wait for grief to finish its work. It arrives alongside the sorrow, not replacing it.

What lingers is the peculiar shape of the promise — not that the path ahead will be easy or the opposition small, but that presence will persist. *I will not fail thee, nor forsake thee.* The courage being spoken of here seems less like the absence of fear and more like movement that continues even when fear is very much present.

Sometimes the most sacred ground is the space between losing what was familiar and stepping into what has been promised.
