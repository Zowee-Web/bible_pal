The body of Moses lay somewhere on the heights of Mount Nebo, buried by the hand of Yahweh in a valley in the land of Moab, in a place no one had found and no one would find. The mourning had lasted thirty days. Thirty days of wailing that carried across the camp of Israel like wind through dry reeds. Thirty days of torn garments and dust pressed into hair and beards. Now the last of those days had passed, and the camp at the edge of the Jordan sat under a wide, open sky, stretching out in every direction — thousands upon thousands of tents made from goat hair and animal skins, staked into the dry ground of the plains of Moab.

Joshua son of Nun stood among those tents. He had served Moses. He had walked behind the man for years, had carried messages, had stood at the entrance of the tent of meeting while the pillar of cloud descended and Yahweh spoke with Moses face to face. He had climbed Sinai when summoned. He had gone into the land of Canaan as one of twelve spies and returned carrying a cluster of grapes so heavy it hung from a pole between two men's shoulders. He had spoken to the congregation, and the congregation had threatened to stone him. All of that was behind him now. Moses was dead.

Then Yahweh spoke to Joshua son of Nun, Moses' servant.

The words came after the death of Moses, the servant of Yahweh. And this is what Yahweh said:

"Moses my servant is dead."

The statement was plain. It named what had happened. The ground of Moab was still warm under the sun. The Jordan moved in its channel not far from where the camp stood, its brown water running south toward the Salt Sea, the sound of it audible when the wind shifted — a low, constant rushing over stones and silt.

"Now therefore arise," Yahweh said. "Go across this Jordan, you and all this people, to the land which I am giving to them, even to the children of Israel."

The Jordan was not wide in most places, but it was swollen in certain seasons, and its banks were thick with tamarisk and willow, the branches trailing into the current. On the far side, the land rose — brown hills studded with scrub, the walls of Jericho visible in the distance, pale and solid against the terrain. The land beyond that stretched further than any single pair of eyes could take in from the plains of Moab: hill country and lowland, desert and coast, valleys thick with grass in the rainy months and baked hard in the dry ones.

"Every place that the sole of your foot will tread on, I have given to you, as I spoke to Moses."

Yahweh marked the boundaries with words. The description moved outward from where Joshua stood, as if tracing lines across a vast map laid open under the sky.

"From the wilderness and this Lebanon, even to the great river, the river Euphrates, all the land of the Hittites, and to the great sea toward the going down of the sun, will be your border."

The wilderness to the south was rock and sand and sparse thorn bushes where vipers coiled in the shade of stones. Lebanon to the north was cedar forests — tall, straight trunks rising into mountain air that smelled of resin and cold water. The Euphrates was far to the east, a river broader and deeper than the Jordan, cutting through flat land where cities traded in grain and bronze. The great sea to the west was the edge of everything, water stretching to the horizon, waves breaking on sand and rock, salt spray hanging in the air. These were the borders. From dry wilderness to green mountains, from the great eastern river to the western sea.

Yahweh spoke again.

"No man will be able to stand before you all the days of your life."

The camp around Joshua was full of men who carried swords and spears, men whose sandals were worn from decades of walking, whose skin was darkened and roughened by years of desert sun. They sharpened bronze blades on flat stones. They counted arrows. They oiled bowstrings made of sinew. These were the men who would cross the Jordan, and Yahweh's words addressed the resistance they would meet on the other side — the armies, the fortified cities, the chariots.

"As I was with Moses, so I will be with you. I will not fail you nor forsake you."

The tent of meeting still stood in the camp. The ark of the covenant rested inside it, overlaid with gold, the cherubim on its cover facing each other with wings spread. The poles for carrying it were fitted through the rings at its sides. It had been carried through the wilderness for forty years. It would be carried across the Jordan.

"Be strong and courageous," Yahweh said, "for you will cause this people to inherit the land which I swore to their fathers to give them."

Joshua had walked this ground before. He had seen the land of Canaan with his own eyes — the vineyards, the walled towns, the people who lived there, tall and numerous. He had brought back his report. He had stood beside Caleb and spoken to the whole assembly. Now the commission was given directly to him, not through Moses, not through any intermediary. Yahweh spoke, and Joshua was the one addressed.

"Only be strong and very courageous. Be careful to do according to all the law which Moses my servant commanded you. Don't turn from it to the right hand or to the left, that you may have good success wherever you go."

The scrolls of the law were kept in the camp. The words Moses had spoken and written — the commands, the statutes, the ordinances — were preserved on parchment and carried among the people. They were read aloud at assemblies. They governed how sacrifices were offered on the altar, how disputes between neighbors were settled, how the camp was arranged when it moved, what was clean and what was unclean, how the festivals were observed. The law was not abstract. It was specific. It named animals and grains and fabrics and days. It described the dimensions of the tabernacle down to the cubit. It prescribed what a priest wore, down to the linen undergarments.

"This book of the law shall not depart from your mouth," Yahweh said, "but you shall meditate on it day and night, that you may observe to do according to all that is written in it; for then you shall make your way prosperous, and then you shall have good success."

Day and night. The camp woke before dawn, when the air was still cool and the ground held the cold of the desert night. Men stirred and coughed and rebuilt fires from coals. The smell of smoke and flatbread rose between the tents. At night, the fires burned low, and the sounds of the camp settled to the bleating of goats, the murmur of voices inside tents, the occasional cry of a child. Day and night, the words of the law were to be spoken and considered.

Then Yahweh spoke a final time in this address to Joshua.

"Haven't I commanded you? Be strong and courageous. Don't be afraid. Don't be dismayed, for Yahweh your God is with you wherever you go."

Three times the command to be strong and courageous had been spoken. Three times, each building on the last. The first tied to the inheritance of the land. The second tied to the keeping of the law. The third tied to the presence of Yahweh himself, going with Joshua wherever the road led — across the Jordan, up into the hill country, along the valleys, against the walls of cities not yet named in the campaign to come.

The Jordan continued to move in its channel. The tents of Israel spread across the plains of Moab, smoke rising from cook fires in thin columns. The ark of the covenant sat in the tent of meeting, gold gleaming faintly in the dim interior. The men of war checked their weapons. The cattle and sheep stood in their pens, chewing and shifting their weight. Children ran between tent ropes. Women ground grain between stones, the rhythmic scraping audible from one end of a family cluster to the other.

Joshua son of Nun, the servant of Moses, had received his commission. The words had been spoken. The Jordan lay ahead, brown and moving, and beyond it, the land.
