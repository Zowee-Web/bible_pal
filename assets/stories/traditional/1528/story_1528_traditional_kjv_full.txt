A wind out of the south arose softly along the coast of Crete. It came warm and constant, pressing upon the sails with such gentleness that the mariners looked one upon another and gave the nod. For they had tarried long at Fair Havens, waiting for such a wind as this. And they supposed the hour was come. They drew up the anchor.

The ship went forth westward, keeping close along the shore of Crete. The waters shone blue and green beneath them, and the wind held steady. Their purpose was to reach Phoenix, a harbour farther upon that coast, where they would abide through the winter. The oars were taken in. The sails filled and drew. All things seemed favourable.

It did not hold.

A savage wind fell down from the mountains of Crete and smote the vessel as a man striketh with his fist. The mariners called it Euroclydon — a tempest out of the northeast that came shrieking over the open sea. The ship pitched violently. He that kept the helm strained against the steering oars, yet the vessel could not be brought up into that wind. Her bow turned away. There was no remedy. They gave her over and let her drive.

The storm bore them southward. They passed under the shelter of a small island called Clauda, and in that brief respite the men made haste to draw in the boat that had been towed behind through the swelling seas. It was heavy with water, and sodden, and it took every hand among them to haul it aboard. Brine poured from it upon the deck.

Then they passed great ropes beneath the hull of the ship itself, binding the planks fast together. The timbers groaned under them. And every man aboard knew what lay to the south — the Syrtis, those vast shoals off the coast of Africa where ships are ground to splinters upon the sand. They lowered the gear, whether the sea anchor or the yard, and suffered the vessel to drift before the wind.

And the gale did not relent.

On the second day they began to cast the cargo into the sea. Sacks of grain, and amphoras, and crates — all these they heaved over the side. The waves received them and gave no sound. The ship rode somewhat higher upon the water, yet the wind pressed on.

On the third day the mariners and the passengers together laid hold of the tackle of the ship with their own hands. Spare rigging and heavy cordage and whatsoever could be torn free — all of it they threw over the rail. The deck was stripped bare. And still the wind howled.

Then the days became as one. The heavens remained dark. The clouds sat so thick and so low upon them that neither sun nor stars were seen for many days. There was no means to reckon their place. They knew not where they were. The storm did not ease, neither did it pause. The timbers shifted beneath their feet, and the ropes bound about the hull strained and creaked. Water came over the sides and gathered in the hold.

And all hope that they should be saved was taken from them.

No man ate. Sickness of the sea and fear had emptied their bellies and kept them empty. Men sat where they could brace themselves against the rolling of the ship. Their garments were soaked through. Their hands were raw and bleeding. And the sound of the wind was without ceasing — a low and pressing roar that filled the ears and would not abate.

Then Paul stood up in the midst of them.

He was a prisoner under Roman guard, a man bound in chains, being carried to Rome to stand before the tribunal. He had warned them that they should not have departed from Crete. And he said so now. They ought to have hearkened unto him, and so spared themselves this harm and this loss.

But then his voice turned.

He bade them take heart. Not one soul among them should perish. The ship alone would be lost. Of this he was certain.

And he told them the reason. The angel of God, even the God to whom he belonged and whom he served, had come and stood beside him in the night. In the darkness of the hold, or upon the deck amid the spray, wherever Paul had been — the angel had come and stood there.

And the angel had spoken unto him, saying: Fear not, Paul. Thou shalt be brought before Caesar. And behold, God hath granted thee the lives of all them that sail with thee.

Paul looked upon the men round about him. Two hundred and threescore and sixteen souls were upon that ship. Soldiers and mariners, prisoners and passengers. Their faces were hollow and grey. Their eyes red with salt and weariness. Their hands gripped ropes and rails and the edges of the benches.

And he said unto them: Wherefore be of good courage. I believe God. It shall come to pass even as it was spoken unto me.

The wind pulled at his sodden tunic. The ship pitched hard upon one side, and the rigging above them groaned aloud.

Paul steadied himself and said: Howbeit, we must be cast upon a certain island.
