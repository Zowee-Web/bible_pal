A south wind rose gently along the coast of Crete. It came warm and steady, pressing against the sails with a softness that made the sailors look at one another and nod. They had been waiting for this at Fair Havens. They supposed they had what they needed now. They weighed anchor.

The ship moved west, hugging the Cretan shoreline. The water was blue-green and the wind held. Their destination was Phoenix, a harbor further along the coast where they planned to spend the winter. The oars shipped. The sails drew. Everything seemed right.

It did not hold.

A fierce wind dropped down from the mountains of Crete and struck the ship like a fist. The sailors called it Euroclydon — a tempestuous northeaster that came screaming across the open water. The vessel lurched hard. The helmsman fought the steering oars, but the ship could not bear up into that wind. Her bow swung away. They had no choice. They let her drive.

The storm pushed them south. They ran under the lee of a small island called Clauda, and in that brief shelter the crew scrambled to haul in the ship's boat, which had been dragging behind in the swells. It was waterlogged and heavy. It took every hand to wrestle it aboard. Salt water poured off it onto the deck.

They passed heavy ropes under the hull of the ship itself, binding the planks tight. The timbers groaned beneath them. Every man aboard knew what lay to the south — the Syrtis, those vast sandbars off the North African coast where ships ground themselves to splinters. They lowered the gear, the sea anchor or the yard, and let the vessel drift before the wind.

The gale did not relent.

On the second day they began throwing cargo overboard. Grain sacks, amphoras, crates — all of it went into the sea. The waves swallowed everything without sound. The ship rode a little higher but the wind pressed on.

On the third day the sailors and passengers together seized the ship's tackle with their own hands. Spare rigging, heavy ropes, anything they could tear loose. They threw it all over the rail. The deck cleared. The wind kept howling.

Then the days blurred together. The sky stayed dark. Clouds sat so thick and low that neither sun nor stars appeared for many days. There was no way to fix position. They did not know where they were. The storm did not ease. It did not pause. The timbers shifted and the ropes around the hull strained. Water came over the sides and sloshed in the hold.

All hope that they would be saved was taken away.

No one ate. Seasickness and fear emptied their stomachs and kept them empty. Men sat where they could brace themselves against the rolling. Their clothing was soaked through. Their hands were raw. The sound of the wind was constant — a low, pressing roar that filled the ears and never stopped.

Paul stood up among them.

He was a prisoner under Roman guard, a man in chains being transported to stand trial. He had warned them not to sail from Crete. He said so now. They should have listened to him and spared themselves this damage and loss.

But then his voice shifted.

He told them to take heart. Not a single life among them would be lost. Only the ship. He was certain.

He told them why. The angel of the God he belonged to and the God he served had stood beside him during the night. In the darkness of the hold, or on the deck in the spray, wherever Paul had been — the angel had come and stood there.

The angel had said: "Do not be afraid, Paul. You must be brought before Caesar. And God has given you all those who sail with you."

Paul looked at the men around him. Two hundred and seventy-six people on that ship. Soldiers, sailors, prisoners, passengers. Faces hollow and gray. Eyes red from salt and sleeplessness. Hands gripping ropes and rails and the edges of benches.

He said, "So take courage. I believe God. It will be exactly as it was told to me."

The wind pulled at his soaked tunic. The ship pitched hard to one side and the rigging above them groaned.

Paul steadied himself and said, "But we must be cast upon a certain island."
