A south wind rose gently along the coast of Crete. It came warm and steady from the south, pressing against the sails with a softness that made the sailors look at one another and nod. They had been waiting for this at Fair Havens. They had been waiting many days. The harbor there was not a good one for wintering, and the season was already late. They supposed they had what they needed now. They weighed anchor.

The ship moved west, hugging the Cretan shoreline. The water was blue-green and clear, and the wind held. Gulls drifted above the masts. The shoreline of Crete slid past on their right — rocky points, low cliffs, narrow beaches of pale stone. Their destination was Phoenix, a harbor further along the coast where they planned to spend the winter. The oars shipped. The sails drew. The deck was warm in the sun. Everything seemed right.

It did not hold.

A fierce wind dropped down from the mountains of Crete and struck the ship like a fist. It came without warning. The sailors called it Euroclydon — a tempestuous northeaster that came screaming across the open water. The sky bruised in minutes. The sea darkened beneath them. The vessel lurched hard and the lines snapped taut. The helmsman fought the steering oars, leaning his whole weight against them, but the ship could not bear up into that wind. Her bow swung away. The shoreline of Crete fell back behind them. They had no choice. They let her drive.

The storm pushed them south. The waves rose into long gray ridges streaked with foam. They ran under the lee of a small island called Clauda, and in that brief shelter the crew scrambled to haul in the ship's boat, which had been dragging behind in the swells. It was waterlogged and heavy. Men leaned out over the stern with ropes wrapped around their forearms. It took every hand to wrestle it aboard. Salt water poured off it onto the deck and ran into the scuppers.

Then they passed heavy ropes under the hull of the ship itself, binding the planks tight. They worked fast. The timbers groaned beneath them. Every man aboard knew what lay to the south — the Syrtis, those vast sandbars off the North African coast where ships ground themselves to splinters. They lowered the gear, the sea anchor or the yard, and let the vessel drift before the wind.

The gale did not relent.

On the second day the wind was still on them, and they began throwing cargo overboard. Grain sacks first. Then amphoras. Then crates. Men hauled them up from the hold and pitched them over the rail. The waves swallowed everything without sound. The ship rode a little higher in the water, but the wind pressed on.

On the third day the sailors and passengers together seized the ship's tackle with their own hands. Spare rigging, heavy ropes, planks, anything they could tear loose. They threw it all over the rail. The deck cleared. Salt spray flew sideways across the planking. The wind kept howling.

Then the days blurred together. The sky stayed dark. Clouds sat so thick and low that neither sun nor stars appeared for many days. There was no way to fix position. They did not know where they were. The storm did not ease. It did not pause. The timbers shifted under their feet and the ropes around the hull strained against the planks. Water came over the sides and sloshed in the hold. Below decks the air was sour with bilge and sweat. Above decks the wind tore at every loose thing.

All hope that they would be saved was taken away.

No one ate. Seasickness and fear emptied their stomachs and kept them empty. The food in the lockers stayed in the lockers. Men sat where they could brace themselves against the rolling — wedged against bulwarks, holding onto stanchions, crouched between coils of rope. Their clothing was soaked through and clung to their skin. Their hands were raw from gripping wet line. Their lips were cracked from salt. The sound of the wind was constant — a low, pressing roar that filled the ears and never stopped. Beneath it ran the deeper noise of the hull working in the swell, a slow grinding that a sailor never wants to hear.

Paul stood up among them.

He was a prisoner under Roman guard, a man in chains being transported to stand trial. He had warned them not to sail from Crete. Back in Fair Havens he had told them. He said so now, plainly. They should have listened to him and spared themselves this damage and loss.

But then his voice shifted.

He told them to take heart. Not a single life among them would be lost. Only the ship. He was certain of it.

He told them why. The angel of the God he belonged to and the God he served had stood beside him during the night. In the darkness of the hold, or on the deck in the spray, wherever Paul had been — the angel had come and stood there.

The angel had said: "Do not be afraid, Paul. You must be brought before Caesar. And behold, God has given you all those who sail with you."

Paul looked at the men around him. Two hundred and seventy-six people on that ship. Soldiers in waterlogged tunics. Sailors with the skin peeling from their palms. Other prisoners chained near the mast. Passengers huddled along the bulwarks. Faces hollow and gray. Eyes red from salt and sleeplessness. Hands gripping ropes and rails and the edges of benches. Some of them lifted their heads to look at him. Some did not.

He said, "So take courage. I believe God. It will be exactly as it was told to me."

The wind pulled at his soaked tunic. The ship pitched hard to one side and the rigging above them groaned. A wave struck the bow and water sheeted along the deck and ran past their feet.

Paul steadied himself against the motion and said, "But we must be cast upon a certain island."
