A south wind came up soft along the coast of Crete. The sailors felt it on their faces and took it for a good sign. They weighed anchor at Fair Havens and set course close along the shore, heading for Phoenix where they meant to winter.

The gentle wind did not last.

A violent northeaster — the kind they called Euroclydon — struck down from the island. It hit the ship broadside. The vessel could not hold her heading into that wind, and the crew gave up trying. They let her drive.

Running under the lee of a small island called Clauda, they hauled in the ship's boat that had been towing behind. It took all of them to bring it aboard. They passed ropes under the hull to hold the planks together. They feared the Syrtis sandbanks off the African coast, so they lowered the sea anchor and let the storm push them wherever it would.

The next day the gale had not slowed. They began throwing cargo over the side, crate after crate dropping into gray water. On the third day the sailors took hold of the ship's tackle — rigging, spare gear, anything they could lift — and heaved it overboard with their own hands.

For many days after that, neither sun nor stars appeared. The storm did not ease. It pressed down on them without break. All hope of survival left them.

No one had eaten in a long time. Paul stood among them on the pitching deck. He raised his voice above the wind.

He told them they should have listened to him and not sailed from Crete. But now he urged them to take heart. There would be no loss of life among them. Only the ship would be lost.

He said an angel of the God he belonged to and served had stood beside him in the night. The angel had said, "Do not be afraid, Paul. You must stand before Caesar. And God has given you all those who sail with you."

Paul looked at the men around him — soaked, hollow-faced, gripping whatever they could hold. He said, "So take courage. It will be exactly as I was told."

He paused. The wind tore at the torn sail above them.

"But we must run aground on a certain island."
