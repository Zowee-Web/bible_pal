A south wind arose gently along the coast of Crete. Warm it came, and steady, breathing out of the south, pressing soft against the sails, and the mariners looked one upon another and nodded. For this had they waited at Fair Havens. For this had they waited many days. The harbour was no fit place to winter in, and the season was far spent. They supposed now they had that which they sought. They weighed anchor.

The ship moved westward, keeping close along the shore of Crete. The waters were blue-green and clear, and the wind held. Gulls drifted above the masts. The coastline of Crete slid by upon the right hand — rocky points, low cliffs, narrow beaches of pale stone. Their port was Phoenix, an haven further along that shore, where they purposed to winter. The oars were drawn in. The sails took the wind. The deck was warm beneath the sun. All seemed well.

It did not hold.

A fierce wind came down from the mountains of Crete and smote the ship as with a fist. Without warning it came. The mariners called it Euroclydon — a tempestuous wind out of the northeast, shrieking across the open sea. The sky bruised in moments. The waters darkened beneath them. The vessel lurched hard, and the lines drew taut. The helmsman wrestled the steering oars, casting his whole weight against them, but the ship could not bear up into that wind. Her bow swung away. The shore of Crete fell back behind them. There was nothing to be done. They let her drive.

The storm bare them southward. The waves rose into long grey ridges streaked with foam. They ran under the lee of a small island called Clauda, and in that brief shelter the crew laboured to haul in the ship's boat, which had been dragged behind them in the swells. Heavy it was, and full of water. Men leaned out over the stern with ropes wound about their forearms. It took every hand to wrestle it aboard. Salt water poured off it upon the deck and ran into the scuppers.

Then passed they heavy ropes beneath the hull of the ship herself, and bound the planks tight. Swiftly they wrought. The timbers groaned beneath them. Every man aboard knew what lay to the south — the Syrtis, those great sandbars off the coast of Africa, where ships are ground to splinters. They lowered the gear, the sea anchor or the yard, and suffered the vessel to drift before the wind.

The gale did not relent.

On the second day the wind was yet upon them, and they began to cast the cargo into the sea. The sacks of grain first. Then the amphoras. Then the crates. Men hauled them up out of the hold and pitched them over the rail. The waves swallowed all without a sound. The ship rode a little higher in the water, but the wind pressed on.

On the third day the mariners and the passengers together laid hold of the ship's tackle with their own hands. Spare rigging, heavy ropes, planks, whatsoever they could tear loose. They cast it all over the rail. The deck was made bare. Salt spray flew sideways across the planking. And still the wind howled.

Then the days ran together as one. The sky stayed dark. The clouds sat thick and low upon them, so that neither sun nor stars appeared for many days. There was no fixing of their course. They knew not where they were. The storm ceased not. It paused not. The timbers shifted beneath their feet, and the ropes about the hull strained against the planks. Water came over the sides and washed in the hold. Beneath the deck the air was sour with bilge and sweat. Above the deck the wind tore at every loose thing.

And all hope that they should be saved was taken away.

None did eat. Seasickness and fear had emptied their bellies and kept them empty. The food in the lockers lay in the lockers untouched. Men sat where they could brace themselves against the rolling — wedged against bulwarks, gripping the stanchions, crouched between coils of rope. Their garments were drenched through and clave unto their skin. Their hands were raw from the wet lines. Their lips were cracked with salt. The voice of the wind was constant — a low, pressing roar that filled the ears and ceased not. Beneath it ran the deeper sound of the hull working in the swell, a slow grinding such as a mariner doth never wish to hear.

Then arose Paul among them.

A prisoner he was under Roman guard, a man bound in chains and bound for trial. He had warned them not to sail from Crete. At Fair Havens he had told them so. He told them again, plainly. They ought to have hearkened unto him, and spared themselves this harm and this loss.

But then his voice changed.

He bade them be of good cheer. Not one life among them should be lost. Only the ship. Of this he was certain.

And he told them wherefore. The angel of the God to whom he belonged and whom he served had stood beside him in the night. In the darkness of the hold, or upon the deck amid the spray, wheresoever Paul had been — there had the angel come and stood.

The angel had said unto him: "Fear not, Paul. Thou must stand before Caesar. And behold, God hath given thee all them that sail with thee."

Paul looked upon the men round about him. Two hundred and seventy and six souls upon that ship. Soldiers in tunics soaked through. Mariners with the skin peeling from their palms. Other prisoners chained near the mast. Passengers huddled along the bulwarks. Their faces were hollow and grey. Their eyes were red from the salt and from want of sleep. Their hands gripped ropes and rails and the edges of the benches. Some lifted up their heads to look upon him. Some did not.

He said, "Wherefore, sirs, be of good cheer. I believe God. It shall be even as it was spoken unto me."

The wind pulled at his drenched tunic. The ship pitched hard to one side, and the rigging above them groaned. A wave smote the bow, and the water sheeted along the deck and ran past their feet.

Paul steadied himself against the rolling and said, "Howbeit we must be cast upon a certain island."
