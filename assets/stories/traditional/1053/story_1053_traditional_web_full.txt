Naaman was commander of the army of the king of Syria, a great man with his master and in high favor, because by him the Lord had given victory to Syria. He was a mighty man of valor. But he was a leper.

The disease showed on his skin. Whatever garments he wore, whatever rank he held, whatever victories he had won, the leprosy was there — patches of white, skin thickened and numb, a condition that separated him from the clean world around him. He commanded armies but could not command his own flesh. Under the fine linen of his tunic the patches spread, and he could feel nothing where they touched — not heat, not cold, not the press of fabric against skin.

Now the Syrians on one of their raids had carried away a little girl from the land of Israel. She served Naaman's wife. The girl was young, taken from her home, a captive in a foreign house. Yet she spoke.

"If only my lord were with the prophet who is in Samaria," she said to her mistress. "He would cure him of his leprosy."

Word reached Naaman. He went to the king of Syria, who gave him a letter to the king of Israel. Naaman set out with horses and chariots and a fortune in gifts — ten talents of silver, six thousand shekels of gold, ten sets of clothing. A general's entourage. A display of power meant to match the scale of the request. The chariots rattled over the roads. The horses' hooves threw up dust that coated the riders and the bundles of treasure alike.

He arrived at Elisha's house. The chariots stopped outside. The horses stamped and blew in the dust, their harnesses jingling. Naaman stood at the door with his servants arrayed behind him and his wealth on display — the silver in leather bags, the gold gleaming where the cloth wrappings had loosened on the journey.

Elisha did not come out.

He sent a messenger. "Go and wash in the Jordan seven times, and your flesh shall be restored and you shall be clean."

Naaman was furious. He had traveled from Syria with gifts and letters and an entire retinue. He had expected the prophet to appear in person, to call upon the name of the Lord, to wave his hand over the diseased skin — something dramatic, something visible, something worthy of the journey and the rank of the man who had made it.

Instead, a messenger and a river.

"Behold, I thought that he would surely come out to me and stand and call upon the name of the Lord his God, and wave his hand over the place and cure the leprosy," Naaman said. His voice carried across the yard where the horses shifted and the servants stood still. "Are not Abana and Pharpar, the rivers of Damascus, better than all the waters of Israel? Could I not wash in them and be clean?"

He turned and went away in a rage. The chariots wheeled. The servants followed. The Jordan flowed on without him.

But his servants came near. They spoke carefully, the way you speak to a man whose pride has been wounded and whose hope is fraying.

"My father, if the prophet had commanded you to do some great thing, would you not have done it? How much rather, then, when he says to you, wash and be clean?"

The words were simple. The logic was plain. Naaman stood still on the road, his hands gripping the rail of the chariot, the reins slack.

He went down to the Jordan. The banks were muddy and thick with reeds. The water moved slow and brown between the banks, nothing like the rivers of Damascus that ran cold and clear from the mountains. Insects hovered above the surface. The mud sucked at his sandals as he walked to the edge.

Naaman stepped into the water. He dipped himself once. The water rose to his chest, warm and silty, and he came up. Water streamed from his shoulders. Nothing changed.

Twice. The water ran down and the patches of white were still there.

Three times. Four times. Five. Six.

Each time he went under and came up the same. The servants watched from the bank, their faces tense. The chariots waited on the road above. The general of Syria stood waist-deep in a muddy river in Israel, doing the one thing he had not wanted to do, the brown water clouding around him.

Seven times.

He came up. The water ran down his skin. His servants stared. One of them stepped forward with his mouth open. Where the leprosy had been, the flesh was smooth. Where the skin had been white and thickened and dead, it was now soft and whole and alive, the color returned, the feeling restored. His flesh was restored like the flesh of a little child.

He was clean.
