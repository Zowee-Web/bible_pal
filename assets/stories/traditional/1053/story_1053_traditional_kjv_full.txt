Now Naaman, captain of the host of the king of Syria, was a great man with his master, and honourable, because by him the Lord had given deliverance unto Syria. He was also a mighty man in valour, but he was a leper.

The disease shewed itself upon his skin. Whatever garments he wore, whatever rank he held, whatever victories he had won, the leprosy was there — patches of white upon his flesh, the skin thickened and without feeling, a condition which set him apart from the clean world about him. He commanded armies but could not command his own body. Beneath the fine linen of his garment the patches spread, and he could feel nought where they lay — not heat, not cold, not the press of cloth against his skin.

And the Syrians had gone out by companies, and had brought away captive out of the land of Israel a little maid; and she waited on Naaman's wife. The girl was young, taken from her home, a captive in a strange house. Yet she spake.

"Would God my lord were with the prophet that is in Samaria," she said unto her mistress. "For he would recover him of his leprosy."

Word came unto Naaman. He went unto the king of Syria, who gave him a letter for the king of Israel. And Naaman departed with horses and chariots and with great gifts — ten talents of silver, six thousand pieces of gold, and ten changes of raiment. A general's train. A shewing of power to match the greatness of the asking. The chariots rattled upon the road. The horses' hooves cast up dust that covered the riders and the bundles of treasure alike.

So Naaman came with his horses and with his chariot, and stood at the door of the house of Elisha. The horses stamped and blew in the dust, their harness jingling. His servants stood arrayed behind him, and the wealth of Syria lay on display — the silver in bags of leather, the gold gleaming where the wrappings had come loose upon the journey.

And Elisha sent a messenger unto him, saying, "Go and wash in Jordan seven times, and thy flesh shall come again to thee, and thou shalt be clean."

But Naaman was wroth. He had journeyed from Syria with gifts and letters and a company of men. He had thought surely the prophet would come forth in his own person and stand and call upon the name of the Lord his God, and strike his hand over the place — somewhat worthy of the journey, somewhat befitting the station of the man who had made it.

Instead, a servant and a river.

"Behold, I thought, He will surely come out to me, and stand, and call on the name of the Lord his God, and strike his hand over the place, and recover the leper," Naaman said. His voice carried across the yard where the horses shifted and the servants stood still. "Are not Abana and Pharpar, rivers of Damascus, better than all the waters of Israel? may I not wash in them, and be clean?"

So he turned and went away in a rage. The chariots wheeled about. The servants followed after. The Jordan flowed on without him.

And his servants came near and spake unto him. They spake with care, as men speak unto one whose pride is wounded and whose hope groweth thin.

"My father, if the prophet had bid thee do some great thing, wouldest thou not have done it? how much rather then, when he saith to thee, Wash, and be clean?"

The words were plain. The reasoning was simple. And Naaman stood still upon the road, his hands gripping the rail of the chariot, the reins slack.

Then went he down to Jordan. The banks were soft with mud and thick with reeds. The water moved slow and brown betwixt the banks, nothing to be compared with the rivers of Damascus that ran cold and clear from the mountains. Insects hung above the surface. The mud pulled at his sandals as he walked unto the water's edge.

And Naaman went into the water. He dipped himself once. The water rose unto his breast, warm and full of silt, and he came up. The water streamed from his shoulders. Nothing was changed.

Twice. The water ran from his shoulders and the white places remained.

Thrice. Four times. Five. Six.

Each time he went under and came forth the same. His servants watched from the bank, their faces drawn tight. The chariots waited upon the road above. The captain of the host of Syria stood in the midst of a muddy river in Israel, doing the thing he would not have chosen to do, the brown water clouding round about him.

Seven times.

And he came up. The water ran down upon his skin. His servants looked and stared. One stepped forward with his mouth agape. Where the leprosy had been, the flesh was smooth. Where the skin had been white and thickened and dead, it was now soft and whole and quick with life, the colour returned, the feeling restored. His flesh came again like unto the flesh of a little child.

And he was clean.
