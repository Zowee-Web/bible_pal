The words came through the prophet Isaiah. They were spoken unto a weary people, a people that had waited a long time.

Hast thou not known? Hast thou not heard?

Isaiah stood before them and spake plainly, the way a teacher speaketh unto children who have forgotten somewhat of great worth.

The everlasting God, the Lord, said Isaiah, is the Creator of the ends of the earth.

Every hill that rose in the distance, every valley that dipped betwixt them. The rivers that carved their way through stone over long years. The wide seas that stretched beyond where any man could see. The mountains dusted with snow upon their peaks. The stars that burned in the dark sky — every single one of them, too many to number. He made them all.

He fainteth not, said Isaiah. Neither is he weary.

The sun crossed the sky day after day, and God did not tire. The seasons turned — the dry heat, the early rains, the cold nights — and God did not slack. He did not sit beneath a tree and close his eyes. He did not lean upon a wall to fetch his breath.

His understanding, Isaiah told them, no man can search out. It is deeper than the deepest well. Wider than the widest field.

And Isaiah spake of the young men.

Even the youths shall faint and be weary, he said. And the young men shall utterly fall.

The strongest among them. They that had broad shoulders and swift legs. They that could lift the heaviest stones and run the farthest distances. Even they grew weary. Even they stumbled upon the road. Even they fell down upon the ground, breathing hard, sweat dripping from their brows into the dust.

Their sinews gave out. Their knees bowed. Their feet dragged. Youth was not enough to bear them onward without end.

The people knew this too. They had seen it. A young man running toward the city gates, full of vigour, and then slackening, slackening, his stride shortening, his arms hanging heavy at his sides. Strong legs turning to stone.

But then Isaiah said somewhat else.

They that wait upon the Lord shall renew their strength.

Not strength scraped together from the bottom of an empty vessel. Not the kind of strength a man getteth from a few hours of sleep upon a hard mat. Somewhat new. Somewhat given.

Isaiah's voice lifted.

They shall mount up with wings as eagles.

High above the valleys and the dusty roads, eagles rode upon the wind. Their wide wings stretched open — five cubits, six cubits from tip to tip — brown feathers catching the moving air. They did not flap and strain. They did not beat their wings in alarm. They simply spread them wide, and the wind bare them upward.

Higher and higher. Past the treetops. Past the cliffs. The fields below grew small, a patchwork of green and brown. The rivers seemed as silver threads. And still the eagle climbed, turning in slow wide circles, scarce moving at all, lifted by somewhat he could not see but trusted altogether.

That was the picture Isaiah set before them.

They shall run, and not be weary, he said.

Feet upon solid ground. Arms swinging free. Breath coming steady and even. Running the way a child runneth across an open field toward home — not straining, not gasping, simply moving, moving, moving forward without the heaviness that draweth a body down.

And they shall walk, and not faint.

Step after step after step. Over rocky ground. Through long stretches of dust where the road seemed to go on forever. Past the place where legs begin to burn. Past the place where most men sit down and say they can go no further. Still walking. Still upright. Still putting one foot before the other.

Not falling. Not giving up.

The prophet's words hung in the warm air. The people stood with dust upon their robes and weariness deep in their bones. A dry wind blew across them, lifting the hems of their head coverings. Somewhere above, against the pale wide sky, an eagle turned in a long slow circle, his wings stretched open and perfectly still, rising.
