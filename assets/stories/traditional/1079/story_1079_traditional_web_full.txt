The words came through the prophet Isaiah. They were spoken to a tired people, a people who had waited a long time.

Have you not known? Have you not heard?

Isaiah stood before them and spoke plainly, the way a teacher speaks to children who have forgotten something important.

The Lord is the everlasting God, Isaiah said. He is the Creator of the ends of the earth.

Every hill that rose in the distance, every valley that dropped between them. The rivers that carved their way through stone over long years. The wide seas that stretched beyond where anyone could see. The mountains dusted with snow at their peaks. The stars that burned in the dark sky — every single one of them, too many to count. He made them all.

He does not faint, Isaiah said. He does not grow weary.

The sun crossed the sky day after day, and God did not tire. The seasons turned — the dry heat, the early rains, the cold nights — and God did not slow down. He did not sit beneath a tree and close his eyes. He did not lean against a wall to catch his breath.

His understanding, Isaiah told them, no one can search out. It is deeper than the deepest well. Wider than the widest field.

And Isaiah spoke about the young men.

Even youths shall faint and be weary, he said. Young men shall fall exhausted.

The strongest among them. The ones with broad shoulders and fast legs. The ones who could lift the heaviest stones and run the farthest distances. Even they grew tired. Even they stumbled on the road. Even they dropped to the ground, breathing hard, sweat dripping from their foreheads into the dust.

Their muscles gave out. Their knees buckled. Their feet dragged. Youth was not enough to keep them going forever.

The people knew this too. They had seen it. A young man sprinting toward the city gates, full of energy, and then slowing, slowing, his stride shortening, his arms hanging heavy at his sides. Strong legs turning to stone.

But then Isaiah said something else.

They who wait for the Lord shall renew their strength.

Not strength scraped together from the bottom of an empty jar. Not the kind of strength you get from a few hours of sleep on a hard mat. Something new. Something given.

Isaiah's voice lifted.

They shall mount up with wings like eagles.

High above the valleys and the dusty roads, eagles rode the wind. Their wide wings stretched open — five feet, six feet from tip to tip — brown feathers catching the moving air. They did not flap and strain. They did not beat their wings in a panic. They simply spread them wide, and the wind carried them upward.

Higher and higher. Past the treetops. Past the cliffs. The fields below turned small, a patchwork of green and brown. The rivers looked like silver threads. And still the eagle climbed, circling in slow, wide loops, barely moving at all, lifted by something it could not see but trusted completely.

That was the picture Isaiah set before them.

They shall run and not be weary, he said.

Feet on solid ground. Arms swinging free. Breath coming steady and even. Running the way a child runs across an open field toward home — not straining, not gasping, just moving, moving, moving forward without the heaviness that pulls a body down.

They shall walk and not faint.

Step after step after step. Over rocky ground. Through long stretches of dust where the road seemed to go on forever. Past the place where legs begin to burn. Past the place where most people sit down and say they cannot go any further. Still walking. Still upright. Still putting one foot in front of the other.

Not collapsing. Not giving up.

The prophet's words hung in the warm air. The people stood with dust on their robes and weariness deep in their bones. A dry wind blew across them, lifting the edges of their head coverings. Somewhere above, against the pale wide sky, an eagle turned in a long slow circle, its wings stretched open and perfectly still, rising.
