The words came through the prophet Isaiah. They were spoken unto a weary people — a people that had been waiting a long, long time and whose legs ached from the waiting.

Isaiah stood before them. The sun was high. The air was dry and warm, and the dust beneath their sandals was fine as flour. The people had gathered close, their faces drawn, their shoulders sloped the way shoulders slope when a man hath borne somewhat heavy too many days.

Isaiah opened his mouth and spake.

Hast thou not known? he said. Hast thou not heard?

His voice was clear. It carried over the heads of the people standing nearest unto him and reached them in the back, them leaning against the low stone wall, them who had walked a long way to hear what the prophet had to say.

Hast thou not known? Hast thou not heard?

He asked it again, the way a teacher asketh a question when the answer should be plain. When the children should already know. When the thing hath been told before, many times, and somehow been forgotten.

That the everlasting God, the Lord, said Isaiah, is the Creator of the ends of the earth.

He let those words settle over the multitude the way dust settleth after a cart hath passed upon the road.

The ends of the earth. Every hill that rose up out of the flat ground and every valley that dipped betwixt them. The mountains — tall and cold and ancient, with snow upon their peaks that glinted in the winter sun. The wide seas that stretched out beyond where any man could see, blue and green and deep. The rivers that cut paths through rock, year after year after year, carving their way through stone so slowly that a man could watch his whole life and not perceive the change.

God made them all.

The stars also. Every single one that appeared in the night sky when the sun was gone down. The bright ones and the faint ones. The ones clustered together like spilled grain and the ones that burned alone in the far corners of the dark. Too many to number. He set each one in his place.

And then Isaiah said somewhat of God that the people needed to hear.

He fainteth not.

The prophet paused. He let the silence rest there.

Neither is he weary.

Day after day, the sun crossed the sky — rising, climbing, falling, setting — and God did not tire. The seasons turned. The rains came and the rains ceased. The dry heat pressed down upon the land and the cold nights crept in, and God did not slack. He did not sit down heavily upon a stone and rub his knees. He did not lean against a doorpost and close his eyes. He did not lie down upon a mat and sleep because his body could not bear itself upright any longer.

He fainteth not. Neither is he weary.

His understanding, said Isaiah, is unsearchable.

No man could find the bottom of it. A man could dig and dig and dig, the way a man diggeth a well, deeper and deeper through layers of dry earth and clay and rock, and he would never come unto the end of what God understood. It went further down than any man could go.

The women that bare water pitchers upon their shoulders from the well unto their houses, the clay heavy against their necks, the path up hill, their arms trembling by the time they reached the door. The men that wrought the fields from first light until the sky turned orange and the shadows grew long, swinging their tools until their palms were raw and red. The children that walked long distances beside their parents, their small legs working twice as hard to keep up, their feet dragging through the dust by afternoon.

They all knew weariness. It was as familiar unto them as bread.

And then Isaiah spake of the young men.

Even the youths shall faint and be weary, he said.

The young ones. The strong ones. The lads that had just become men, whose arms were thick and whose legs could bear them far. They that could lift the heaviest stones in the quarry. They that could run from one village unto the next without stopping. They whose vigour seemed to come from some deep well within them that never ran dry.

Even they grew weary.

Even they stumbled upon the road.

And the young men shall utterly fall, said Isaiah.

The people had seen this with their own eyes. A young man, full of strength, running toward the city gates, his feet pounding the packed earth, his arms pumping, his face set with effort. And then — slowly — the stride shortening. The arms dropping. The breath coming in ragged gasps. The knees bending wrong. And at last the young man stopping, bent over, hands upon his thighs, sweat dripping from his brow into the pale dust, unable to take another single step.

Youth was not enough. Sinew was not enough. The strongest legs in the land yet gave out in the end.

Backs ached. Feet blistered. Hands shook. Eyes grew heavy. Bones seemed as wet sand.

The people standing before Isaiah knew every bit of this. Some of them were feeling it right then, right there, in their legs and their shoulders and their heavy eyelids.

But Isaiah was not finished.

He drew a slow breath and spake again.

But they that wait upon the Lord shall renew their strength.

The word came forth steady and clear. Wait. Not rush ahead. Not press the harder. Not grit thy teeth and force thy weary body forward in thine own strength. Wait.

And they that did — they that held fast, they that trusted, they that tarried — they would receive somewhat. Their strength would be made new. Not patched together from scraps. Not borrowed from a neighbour. Not pressed from the last dry corner of an empty wineskin. New. As water from a spring that had not been there the day before, suddenly rising up cool and clean from the ground.

Isaiah lifted up his voice.

They shall mount up with wings as eagles.

The people knew eagles. They had seen them. High above the valleys and the terraced hillsides, above the roads and the rooftops and the olive groves, the great fowls soared. Their wings stretched wide — five cubits, six cubits from tip to tip — brown feathers spread as fingers against the sky. They did not beat their wings in frantic labour. They did not strain and flap and fight the air.

They simply opened their wings.

And the wind — the warm wind that rose up from the valley floor — caught them and bare them up. Higher and higher. Past the treetops with their silver-green leaves trembling. Past the rocky cliff faces where lizards sunned themselves upon warm stone. Past the ridgeline where the shepherds stood with their flocks.

The eagle turned in slow, wide circles. His wings scarce moved. The fields below shrank — green and brown squares stitched together, the paths betwixt them thin as thread. The river became a silver line, glinting. The people upon the ground became as small as seeds.

And still the eagle rose, borne up by somewhat he could not see but leaned into altogether.

That was the picture Isaiah set before the people.

Then he said more.

They shall run, and not be weary.

Feet upon the ground. Hard-packed dirt beneath the soles. Arms swinging loose. Breath coming easy, steady as a heartbeat. Running the way a child runneth across a wide-open field toward home at suppertime — not gasping, not straining, not feeling the pull of heaviness in the legs. Simply moving. Forward. Free.

Mile after mile, and the legs still strong. The breath still full. The heart still beating steady.

And they shall walk, and not faint.

This was the last thing the prophet said. Walking. The simplest thing. The slowest thing. One foot before the other.

Over rocky paths where the stones shifted underfoot. Through long valleys where the road stretched on and on and the horizon never seemed to draw nearer. Past the point where most men sat down upon a boulder and dropped their heads betwixt their knees. Past the place where strong young men fell down in the dust.

Still walking. Still upright. Step after step after step. Not stumbling. Not sinking. Not yielding to the weight that pressed down upon every bone.

The prophet's voice faded into the warm dry air. The people stood still. Dust coated the hems of their robes. The wind moved across them, slow and steady, lifting the loose hems of their head coverings. The sun pressed down upon their shoulders. Somewhere far above, against the wide pale sky, an eagle turned in one long circle, his broad wings stretched open and perfectly still, rising upon the unseen wind.
