The words came through the prophet Isaiah. They were spoken to a tired people — a people who had been waiting a long, long time and whose legs ached from the waiting.

Isaiah stood before them. The sun was high. The air was dry and warm, and the dust beneath their sandals was fine as flour. The people had gathered close, their faces drawn, their shoulders sloped the way shoulders slope when a person has been carrying something heavy for too many days.

Isaiah opened his mouth and spoke.

Have you not known? he said. Have you not heard?

His voice was clear. It carried over the heads of the people standing nearest to him and reached the ones in the back, the ones leaning against the low stone wall, the ones who had walked a long way just to hear what the prophet had to say.

Have you not known? Have you not heard?

He asked it again, the way a teacher asks a question when the answer should be plain. When the children should already know. When the thing has been told before, many times, and somehow been forgotten.

The Lord is the everlasting God, Isaiah said. The Creator of the ends of the earth.

He let those words settle over the crowd the way dust settles after a cart has passed on the road.

The ends of the earth. Every hill that rose up out of the flat ground and every valley that dipped between them. The mountains — tall and cold and ancient, with snow on their peaks that glinted in the winter sun. The wide seas that stretched out beyond where any person could see, blue and green and deep. The rivers that cut paths through rock, year after year after year, carving their way through stone so slowly that a man could watch his whole life and not notice the change.

God made them all.

The stars, too. Every single one that appeared in the night sky when the sun went down. The bright ones and the faint ones. The ones clustered together like spilled grain and the ones that burned alone in the far corners of the dark. Too many to count. He set each one in its place.

And then Isaiah said something about God that the people needed to hear.

He does not faint.

The prophet paused. He let the silence sit there.

He does not grow weary.

Day after day, the sun crossed the sky — rising, climbing, falling, setting — and God did not tire. The seasons turned. The rains came and the rains stopped. The dry heat pressed down on the land and the cold nights crept in, and God did not slow. He did not sit down heavily on a stone and rub his knees. He did not lean against a doorframe and close his eyes. He did not lie down on a mat and sleep because his body could not hold itself upright any longer.

He does not faint. He does not grow weary.

His understanding, Isaiah said, is unsearchable.

No one could find the bottom of it. A man could dig and dig and dig, the way you dig a well, deeper and deeper through layers of dry earth and clay and rock, and he would never reach the end of what God understood. It went further down than anyone could go.

The women who carried water jars on their shoulders from the well to their homes, the clay heavy against their necks, the path uphill, their arms trembling by the time they reached the door. The men who worked the fields from first light until the sky turned orange and the shadows grew long, swinging tools until their palms were raw and red. The children who walked long distances beside their parents, their small legs working twice as hard to keep up, their feet dragging through the dust by afternoon.

They all knew weariness. It was as familiar to them as bread.

And then Isaiah spoke about the young men.

Even youths shall faint and be weary, he said.

The young ones. The strong ones. The boys who had just become men, whose arms were thick and whose legs could carry them far. The ones who could lift the heaviest stones in the quarry. The ones who could run from one village to the next without stopping. The ones whose energy seemed to come from some deep well inside them that never ran dry.

Even they grew tired.

Even they stumbled on the road.

Young men shall fall exhausted, Isaiah said.

The people had seen this with their own eyes. A young man, full of strength, sprinting toward the city gates, his feet pounding the packed earth, his arms pumping, his face set with effort. And then — slowly — the stride shortening. The arms dropping. The breath coming in ragged gasps. The knees bending wrong. And finally the young man stopping, bent over, hands on his thighs, sweat dripping from his forehead into the pale dust, unable to take another single step.

Youth was not enough. Muscle was not enough. The strongest legs in the land still gave out eventually.

Backs ached. Feet blistered. Hands shook. Eyes grew heavy. Bones felt like they were made of wet sand.

The people standing before Isaiah knew every bit of this. Some of them were feeling it right then, right there, in their legs and their shoulders and their heavy eyelids.

But Isaiah was not finished.

He drew a slow breath and spoke again.

But they who wait for the Lord shall renew their strength.

The word came out steady and clear. Wait. Not rush ahead. Not push harder. Not grit your teeth and force your tired body forward on your own. Wait.

And those who did — those who held on, those who trusted, those who stayed — they would receive something. Their strength would be made new. Not patched together from scraps. Not borrowed from a neighbor. Not squeezed from the last dry corner of an empty wineskin. New. Like water from a spring that had not been there the day before, suddenly rising up cool and clean from the ground.

Isaiah lifted his voice.

They shall mount up with wings like eagles.

The people knew eagles. They had seen them. High above the valleys and the terraced hillsides, above the roads and the rooftops and the olive groves, the great birds soared. Their wings stretched wide — five feet, six feet from tip to tip — brown feathers spread like fingers against the sky. They did not beat their wings in frantic effort. They did not strain and flap and fight the air.

They simply opened their wings.

And the wind — the warm wind that rose up from the valley floor — caught them and lifted them. Higher and higher. Past the treetops with their silver-green leaves trembling. Past the rocky cliff faces where lizards sunned themselves on warm stone. Past the ridgeline where the shepherds stood with their flocks.

The eagle turned in slow, wide circles. Its wings barely moved. The fields below shrank — green and brown squares stitched together, the paths between them thin as thread. The river became a silver line, glinting. The people on the ground became small as seeds.

And still the eagle rose, carried by something it could not see but leaned into completely.

That was the picture Isaiah set before the people.

Then he said more.

They shall run and not be weary.

Feet on the ground. Hard-packed dirt beneath the soles. Arms swinging loose. Breath coming easy, steady as a heartbeat. Running the way a child runs across a wide-open field toward home at suppertime — not gasping, not straining, not feeling the pull of heaviness in the legs. Just moving. Forward. Free.

Mile after mile, and the legs still strong. The lungs still full. The heart still beating steady.

They shall walk and not faint.

This was the last thing the prophet said. Walking. The simplest thing. The slowest thing. One foot in front of the other.

Over rocky paths where the stones shifted underfoot. Through long valleys where the road stretched on and on and the horizon never seemed to get closer. Past the point where most people sat down on a boulder and dropped their heads between their knees. Past the place where strong young men collapsed in the dust.

Still walking. Still upright. Step after step after step. Not stumbling. Not sinking. Not giving in to the weight that pressed down on every bone.

The prophet's voice faded into the warm dry air. The people stood still. Dust coated the hems of their robes. The wind moved across them, slow and steady, lifting the loose edges of their head coverings. The sun pressed down on their shoulders. Somewhere far above, against the wide pale sky, an eagle turned in one long circle, its broad wings stretched open and perfectly still, rising on the unseen wind.
