Now about that time Herod the king stretched forth his hands to vex certain of the church. He had slain James the brother of John with the sword. The execution was public. When he saw it pleased the Jews, he took further action. He seized Peter.

These were the days of unleavened bread. The city was full of pilgrims. The streets carried the smell of roasting lamb and bread in the ovens. Herod would not hold trial during the feast. He put Peter in prison and set his guard: four quaternions of soldiers. Sixteen men to keep one fisherman. His intention was to bring Peter forth unto the people when the Passover was ended.

Peter therefore was kept in prison. The days passed. Without the walls, the church gathered in the houses and prayed — without ceasing, through the days and into the nights.

The night came. The last night before Herod would have brought him forth. Within the cell, Peter was sleeping. He lay upon the stone floor between two soldiers. A chain bound his left hand to the wrist of the soldier on his left. Another bound his right hand to the soldier on his right. He could not lift his arms without pulling against the weight of sleeping men. Two keepers stood before the door, facing outward, their spears upright. The cell was dark save for a line of torchlight beneath the door. The air smelled of damp stone and iron.

Peter slept. His breathing was even.

And behold, the angel of the Lord came upon him. A light shined in the prison — sudden, white, exceeding bright, enough to show the cracks between stones and the rust upon the iron. The soldiers stirred not.

The angel smote Peter on his side. A firm blow.

"Arise up quickly," he said.

His chains fell off from his hands. Both at once. The links struck the stone and lay still. The soldiers slept on, their wrists bound to the empty fetters.

Peter sat up. He blinked against the light.

"Gird thyself," the angel said, "and bind on thy sandals."

Peter looked about him. His sandals sat at the edge of the cell. He reached for them and bound them on, his fingers working the leather.

"Cast thy garment about thee, and follow me."

Peter took his cloak from beneath his head and cast it about his shoulders. He rose. The soldiers on either side moved not. Peter stepped over the nearest man's legs and followed the angel toward the door.

The door stood open. The two keepers remained at their posts, one on either side — spears upright, eyes forward. Peter passed between them close enough to touch. They perceived nothing.

He followed the angel down the corridor. Torches burned upon the walls. The passage turned. They came to the first ward — soldiers keeping watch over the inner prison. The guards were there. Their eyes were open. Peter walked before them. None moved.

They came to the second ward. More soldiers, more eyes that beheld nothing. Peter followed the angel past them, his sandals soft upon the stone. He wist not that it was true which was done by the angel. He thought he saw a vision — that this was a dream, and soon he would awake upon the cold floor with chains upon his wrists.

They came unto the iron gate. The last barrier — heavy, forged of dark iron. It stood shut. No hand touched it.

The gate opened to them of his own accord. It moved upon its hinges — slowly, smoothly. It stood wide against the wall. Through it, the street — dark, empty, the paving stones pale in the moonlight.

They went out. The night air came upon Peter's face — cool, clean, carrying the scent of dying fires and a dog barking in the distance. They passed on through one street together. Their sandals fell softly upon the stone. Buildings rose on either side — doors barred, windows shuttered.

And forthwith the angel departed from him. Between one step and the next. There, then gone. The light departed with him. Peter stood alone.

The night was still. He heard his own breath. He looked upon his wrists — the skin raw where the iron had pressed, but the iron gone. He felt the cloak upon his shoulders. He felt the sandals firm beneath him. He was awake. This was not a vision.

"Now I know of a surety," he said, his voice carrying in the empty street, "that the Lord hath sent his angel, and hath delivered me out of the hand of Herod, and from all the expectation of the people of the Jews."

He stood a moment. Then he moved. He went through the narrow streets to the house of Mary the mother of John Mark — past the shuttered market stalls, past the well at the turning. Many were gathered in that house, praying through the night.

Peter came to the gate of the outer court and knocked. The sound was sharp — knuckles upon wood. He waited.

A damsel came to hearken. Her name was Rhoda. She opened not the gate but came near and listened. She heard a voice through the wood.

She knew it. Gladness seized her — so sudden she forgot the gate. She turned and ran back into the house.

"Peter standeth before the gate!" she said, breathless.

They looked upon her — the gathered company, their faces worn with prayer.

"Thou art mad," they said.

She constantly affirmed it.

"It is his angel," one said.

Without, Peter continued knocking — steady, patient, his hand upon the wood. He could hear voices within. No one came.

He knocked again.

At last they came. They unbarred the gate and drew it open. Peter stood in the narrow way — his cloak about his shoulders, his face visible in the lamplight. Alive. Free.

They were astonished. Voices rose — exclamations, hands reaching forth. Peter lifted his hand and beckoned them to hold their peace. They grew still.

He declared unto them how the Lord had brought him forth — the angel, the light, the chains falling, the guards that saw nothing, the gate opening of its own accord, the angel departing. He told it plainly, in order.

"Go show these things unto James, and to the brethren," he said. Then he departed and went into another place.

As soon as it was day and the sun rose upon the prison walls, there was no small stir among the soldiers. The cell was empty. The chains lay upon the floor. The keepers stood at their posts and could give no account. Peter was gone.
