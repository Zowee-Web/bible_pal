King Herod stretched out his hand against the church. He had already killed James — the brother of John, one of the inner circle — put to death with the sword. The execution was public. When Herod saw it pleased the Jewish leaders, he moved again. He arrested Peter.

This was during the Feast of Unleavened Bread. The city was full of pilgrims. The streets smelled of roasting lamb and bread baking in clay ovens. Herod would not hold the trial during the feast — that would cause unrest. He put Peter in prison and set the guard: four squads of four soldiers each. Sixteen men to keep one fisherman. His intention was clear: after the Passover, he would bring Peter out before the people.

Peter was kept in prison. The days passed. Outside the walls, the church gathered and prayed — earnestly, without ceasing, through the days and into the nights.

The night came. The last night before Herod intended to bring Peter out. Inside the cell, Peter slept. He lay on the stone floor between two soldiers. A chain ran from his left wrist to the wrist of the soldier on his left. Another chain ran from his right wrist to the soldier on his right. He could not move his arms without pulling against sleeping men. Two more sentries stood at the cell door, facing outward into the corridor. The cell was dark except for a faint line of torchlight beneath the door. The air smelled of damp stone and iron.

Peter was asleep. His breathing was even and slow.

Then an angel of the Lord stood beside him. Light filled the cell — sudden, white, bright enough to show the cracks between the stones and the rust on the chains. The soldiers did not stir. Their breathing continued unchanged.

The angel struck Peter on the side. A firm blow, enough to wake a man from deep sleep.

"Get up quickly," the angel said.

The chains fell from Peter's wrists. Both at once. The links hit the stone floor and lay still. The soldiers slept on, their wrists bound to the empty chains.

Peter sat up. He blinked in the light, his eyes adjusting.

"Dress yourself," the angel said. "Put on your sandals."

Peter looked around. His sandals sat at the edge of the floor where he had set them. He reached for them and strapped them on, his fingers working the leather.

"Wrap your cloak around you and follow me."

Peter pulled his cloak from where it lay folded beneath his head and wrapped it around his shoulders. He stood. The soldiers on either side did not move. Their chests rose and fell. Peter stepped over the nearest one's legs and followed the angel toward the cell door.

The door was open. The two sentries stood at their posts on either side, spears upright, eyes forward. They did not turn. Peter passed between them close enough to touch.

He followed the angel down the corridor. Torches burned steadily on the walls. The passage turned. They came to the first guard post — soldiers stationed to watch the inner prison. The guards were there. Their eyes were open. Peter walked past them. None moved or spoke.

They came to the second guard post. More soldiers, more open eyes that saw nothing. Peter followed the angel through, his sandals quiet on the stone. He still did not believe this was real. He thought he was seeing a vision — that soon he would wake on the cold floor with chains on his wrists.

They came to the iron gate. The final barrier — heavy, dark metal, separating prison from city. It stood closed.

The gate swung open. It moved on its hinges slowly, smoothly, with no hand touching it. It stood wide against the wall. Through it Peter could see the street — dark, empty, paving stones reflecting faint moonlight.

They stepped through. The night air hit Peter's face — cool, clean, carrying the smell of dying cook fires and the sound of a dog barking far off. They walked together down the street, sandals soft on the stone. One block. The buildings rose on either side — closed doors, shuttered windows, no lamps burning.

Then the angel was gone. Between one step and the next. There, and then not. The light went with him. Peter stood alone in the dark street.

The night was quiet. He could hear his own breathing. He looked down at his wrists — the skin raw where the chains had been, but the chains gone. He touched his cloak. He felt his sandals on the ground. He was awake. This was not a vision.

"Now I truly know," he said aloud, his voice carrying in the empty street, "that the Lord has sent his angel and rescued me from the hand of Herod and from all that the Jewish people were expecting."

He stood for a moment, thinking. Then he moved. He went to the house of Mary, the mother of John Mark. He knew the way — through the narrow streets, past the closed market stalls, past the well at the corner. Many people were gathered in that house, praying through the night for him.

Peter reached the outer gate of the courtyard. He knocked. The sound was sharp — knuckles against wood. He waited.

A servant girl came to answer. Her name was Rhoda. She did not open the gate. She came close and listened through the wood.

She recognized his voice. Joy seized her — so sudden she forgot the gate entirely. She turned and ran back into the house without opening it.

"Peter is at the gate!" she said, breathless, her face lit.

They stared at her — the gathered company, their faces worn with hours of prayer.

"You are out of your mind," they said.

She kept insisting. She said it again.

"It is his angel," someone said.

Outside, Peter kept knocking. Steady, patient, knuckles against the wood. He could hear voices inside, muffled through the wall. No one was opening.

He knocked again.

Finally they came. They unbarred the gate and pulled it open. Peter stood in the narrow alley, his cloak around his shoulders, his face visible in the faint light from the doorway. Alive. Free.

They were astonished. Voices rose — questions, exclamations, hands reaching toward him. Peter raised his hand and motioned for silence. They grew quiet.

He told them. The angel in the cell. The light. The chains falling. The guards who did not see. The iron gate opening on its own. The street, and the angel departing. He told it simply, in order, his voice steady.

When he finished, he said, "Tell these things to James and to the brothers." Then he left the house and went to another place.

When morning came and the sun rose over the prison walls, there was no small disturbance among the soldiers. The cell was empty. The chains lay on the floor. The sentries stood at their posts, unable to explain. Peter was gone.
