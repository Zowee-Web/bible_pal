The room was small, and it stood at the hinder part of a fuller's shop upon a narrow street in Rome. The stone floor had been swept clean that evening. Oil lamps burned upon a ledge beside the single window, and their light fell upon the plastered walls yet reached not unto the corners. A bench of wood ran along the one side. Mats of woven reed were laid upon the remainder of the floor. Seven or eight souls sat gathered close together — men and women, some bearing hands roughened by labour, and one woman advanced in years who had drawn about her a shawl of brown wool against the chill of the evening.

A man called Lucius stood nigh unto the doorway. He held a scroll opened between both hands, gripping it with care at the wooden rods upon either end. The letter was from Paul, written in a far country, borne by hand along the roads and over the sea. The ink was of a dark brown, the script even and steady, the labour of a trained scribe setting down what had been spoken aloud unto him. And Lucius had been reading unto them for some time.

He had read through weighty arguments and close reasonings. Words concerning the law and concerning grace. Words concerning sin and what the Spirit doth accomplish. Words of suffering, of groaning, of hope not yet made visible. And the people who sat in that room had hearkened without ceasing, leaning forward upon their places or shifting upon their mats as the words passed through them.

Now Lucius came unto the end of the letter's eighth portion, and his voice was changed. It lifted, as though the words upon the scroll had taken another form.

"What then shall we say unto these things?" he read.

He lifted his eyes from the scroll. The room was still. Somewhere beyond the window a cart wheel ground against the stone of the alley. No one within the room stirred.

"If God be for us," Lucius read, "who then shall stand against us?"

The question hung in the lamplight. A man who sat near the wall, broad of shoulder, with a short dark beard, leaned forward and set his hands flat upon his knees. His mouth was partly opened.

Lucius read on. "He that withheld not his own Son, but gave him up for the sake of us all — shall he not also with him bestow upon us freely all things?"

A young woman near the bench drew in her breath slowly. The elder woman in the brown shawl had become very still, her fingers gripping the edge of the wool at her throat.

"Who shall bring a charge against those whom God hath chosen?" Lucius read. His voice was steady and clear. "It is God himself who declareth them righteous."

He paused the space of half a breath. The lamplight moved upon the wall behind him.

"Who is he that shall condemn?"

Another pause. Then the answer came forth from the scroll.

"It is Christ who died — yea, and more than this, who is risen from the dead, who sitteth at the right hand of God, who even now maketh appeal on our behalf."

The broad-shouldered man near the wall pressed his lips together and shut his eyes. His hands remained flat upon his knees, and he was very still.

Lucius drew breath. He was come now unto a new manner of questions. The cadence within the words was building, and those who sat in that room perceived it, and felt it in the way each phrase struck harder than the one before.

"Who shall sever us from the love of Christ?" Lucius read. He steadied the scroll between both hands.

"Shall tribulation?" he read.

"Or distress?"

"Or persecution?"

"Or famine?"

"Or nakedness?"

"Or peril?"

"Or the sword?"

Each word fell separate and distinct into that small room. The young woman by the bench had pressed both her hands together in her lap. The eyes of the elder woman were wet in the lamplight. No one uttered a sound.

"As it is written," Lucius continued: "For thy sake we are given over unto death the whole day long. We are reckoned as sheep appointed for the slaughter."

He paused. The oil lamps flickered. A draught moved through the doorway behind him, and the flames bent sideways and then were made straight again.

Then Lucius read the word that followed, and his voice rose.

"Nay."

He lifted his chin. His fingers tightened upon the rods of the scroll.

"In all these things we are more than conquerors, through him who hath loved us."

The broad-shouldered man opened his eyes. The young woman near the bench set one hand to her mouth. Someone in the hinder part of the room — it was not clear who it was — let forth a sharp breath, as one who had held it long within.

Lucius turned the scroll nearer unto the lamplight. The final lines were there upon the sheet, the scribe's lettering firm and unhurried.

"For I am assured of this," he read, and his voice filled the room now, reaching unto every corner, every face turned toward him, "that neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor rulers, nor powers, nor things which are present, nor things which are yet to come, nor the height above, nor the depth beneath, nor any created thing whatsoever —"

He drew one breath more.

"— shall be able to sever us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord."

Lucius lowered the scroll. The wooden rods met softly one against the other. The oil lamps burned upon the stone ledge. And the room was full of breathing, and the faces in the lamplight were wet, and no one spake.
