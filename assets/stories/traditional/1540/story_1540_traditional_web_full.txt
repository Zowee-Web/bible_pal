The room was small and sat at the back of a fuller's shop on a narrow street in Rome. The stone floor had been swept clean that evening. Oil lamps burned on a ledge near the single window, and their light reached the plaster walls but did not touch the corners. A wooden bench ran along one side. Mats of woven reed covered the rest of the floor. Seven or eight people sat crowded together — men and women, some with rough hands from labor, one older woman wrapped in a brown wool shawl against the evening chill.

A man named Lucius stood near the doorway. He held a scroll open between both hands, gripping it carefully at the wooden rods on either end. The letter was from Paul, written far away, carried by hand along the roads and across the sea. The ink was dark brown, the script even and careful, the work of a trained scribe writing at dictation. Lucius had been reading aloud for some time now.

He had read through dense arguments and explanations. Talk of the law and of grace. Talk of sin and of what the Spirit does. Talk of suffering, of groaning, of hope not yet seen. The people in the room had listened without interruption, leaning forward or shifting on their mats as the words moved through them.

Now Lucius came to the end of the letter's eighth section, and his voice changed. It lifted slightly, as though the words on the scroll had changed shape.

"What shall we then say to these things?" he read.

He looked up from the scroll. The room was quiet. Somewhere outside the window, a cart wheel scraped against stone in the alley. No one in the room moved.

"If God is for us," Lucius read, "who can be against us?"

The question hung in the lamplight. A man sitting near the wall, broad across the shoulders, with a short dark beard, leaned forward and put his hands flat on his knees. His mouth was slightly open.

Lucius read on. "He who did not spare his own Son, but delivered him up for us all — how shall he not with him also freely give us all things?"

A young woman near the bench drew a slow breath. The older woman in the brown shawl had gone very still, her fingers gripping the edge of the wool at her throat.

"Who shall lay anything to the charge of God's elect?" Lucius read. His voice was steady and clear. "It is God who justifies."

He paused half a breath. The lamplight moved on the wall behind him.

"Who is he that condemns?"

Another pause. Then the answer came from the scroll.

"It is Christ who died — yes, rather, who is risen again, who is even at the right hand of God, who also makes intercession for us."

The broad-shouldered man near the wall pressed his lips together and closed his eyes. His hands stayed flat on his knees, very still.

Lucius drew breath. He was coming to a new set of questions. The cadence in the words was building, and those in the room could hear it, could feel it in the way each phrase landed harder than the one before.

"Who shall separate us from the love of Christ?" Lucius read. He steadied the scroll in both hands.

"Shall tribulation?" he read.

"Or distress?"

"Or persecution?"

"Or famine?"

"Or nakedness?"

"Or peril?"

"Or sword?"

Each word fell separate and distinct into the small room. The young woman by the bench had both hands pressed together in her lap. The older woman's eyes were wet in the lamplight. No one made a sound.

"As it is written," Lucius continued: "For your sake we are killed all the day long. We are accounted as sheep for the slaughter."

He paused. The oil lamps flickered. A draft moved through the doorway behind him, and the flames bent sideways and then straightened.

Then Lucius read the next word, and his voice rose.

"No."

He lifted his chin. His fingers tightened on the rods of the scroll.

"In all these things we are more than conquerors through him who loved us."

The broad-shouldered man opened his eyes. The young woman near the bench put one hand to her mouth. Someone in the back of the room — it was not clear who — exhaled sharply, as though they had been holding their breath.

Lucius tilted the scroll closer to the lamplight. The final lines were there on the sheet, the scribe's lettering firm and unhurried.

"For I am persuaded," he read, and his voice filled the room now, reaching every corner, every face turned toward him, "that neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor principalities, nor powers, nor things present, nor things to come, nor height, nor depth, nor any other creature —"

He drew one more breath.

"— shall be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord."

Lucius lowered the scroll. The wooden rods clicked softly as the edges met. The oil lamps burned on the stone ledge. The room was full of breathing, and the faces in the lamplight were wet, and no one spoke.
