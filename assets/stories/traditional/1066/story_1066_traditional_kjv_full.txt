The hillside rose gently above the road, and the grass was thick and green from the latter rains. People had gathered along the slope — men and women and children, sitting upon the ground or standing at the edges, their sandals dusty from the journey. The sky was clear and wide. Below the hill, the fields stretched toward the lake where the water lay flat and silver under the sun.

Jesus sat among them, higher upon the slope, and the people were quiet. His voice carried in the still air.

"Take no thought for your life," he saith, "what ye shall eat, or what ye shall drink." He looked across the multitude. "Neither for your body, what ye shall put on."

A child shifted in his mother's lap. A man near the front rested his elbows upon his knees.

"Is not the life more than meat," Jesus saith, "and the body than raiment?"

He paused. Then he lifted his hand toward the open sky above them. Birds moved there — small dark shapes gliding and turning against the blue. Some rose upon the wind. Some dropped low over the grass.

"Behold the fowls of the air," he saith. "They sow not. Neither do they reap. Nor gather into barns." His hand stayed raised for a moment, tracing the path of one bird as it swept across the hillside. "And yet your heavenly Father feedeth them."

The multitude followed his gaze. The birds circled once more and disappeared beyond the ridge.

"Are ye not much better than they?" Jesus asked.

No man spake. The wind pressed gently against the grass.

He went on. "Which of you by taking thought can add one cubit unto his stature?"

The question hung in the air. A woman near the back adjusted the cloth over her head. A young man stared at his own hands.

Jesus shifted where he sat. "And why take ye thought for raiment?" he saith.

He turned and gestured toward the edge of the path that wound up the hillside. Wildflowers grew there in clusters — white and violet and deep gold, their thin stems rising above the grass. The petals caught the light and held it.

"Consider the lilies of the field," he saith, "how they grow." His voice was even, unhurried. "They toil not, neither do they spin."

The flowers swayed in the breeze. Their colours were bright against the green slope.

"And yet I say unto you," Jesus saith, and his voice rose slightly, "that even Solomon in all his glory was not arrayed like one of these."

A murmur passed through the multitude. Some turned to look upon the flowers. Others kept their eyes upon Jesus.

He continued. "Wherefore, if God so clothe the grass of the field, which to day is, and to morrow is cast into the oven, shall he not much more clothe you?"

He looked across the faces before him — the weathered fishermen, the young mothers, the old men with their staves resting across their laps.

"O ye of little faith."

The words were not sharp. They were steady. The multitude was still.

"Therefore take no thought," he saith, "saying, What shall we eat? or, What shall we drink? or, Wherewithal shall we be clothed?" He paused between each question. The syllables fell clearly in the open air. "For after all these things do the Gentiles seek."

A dry leaf tumbled across the path at the base of the hill. The sun had moved higher and the shadows beneath the few scattered trees had shortened.

"And your heavenly Father knoweth that ye have need of all these things," Jesus saith.

He leaned forward slightly. "But seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness; and all these things shall be added unto you."

The hillside was quiet. Somewhere below, near the road, an ass brayed and fell silent. The surface of the lake in the distance caught the light and sent it back in a single white line.

Jesus spake again. "Take therefore no thought for the morrow: for the morrow shall take thought for the things of itself."

He paused once more. The wind moved through the wildflowers along the path.

"Sufficient unto the day," he saith, "is the evil thereof."

The multitude sat upon the green slope. The lilies held their bright faces open to the sun. The birds had returned above the ridge, turning slowly in the warm air, and the hillside was still.
