The road that wound up from the lake passed through low fields where grain was beginning to stand in rows, green and thin, the stalks barely a hand's width above the soil. Beyond the fields the hillside rose — not steep, but broad and open, and the grass on it was thick from the late spring rains. Wildflowers grew along the edges of the path in clusters of white and violet and deep gold, their petals trembling faintly in the wind that came up from the water.

People were already gathered on the slope when Jesus arrived. They had come from the villages around the lake — fishermen still smelling of salt, women carrying small children on their hips, old men leaning on staffs, young men standing in groups of two or three at the edges of the crowd. Some had spread cloaks on the ground to sit on. Others simply sat in the grass with their sandals beside them. The sun was high and warm but not yet hot, and the sky above the ridge was clean and pale.

Jesus climbed the hillside and sat down among them, higher on the slope where the ground leveled slightly. The crowd settled. Conversations dropped away. A child called out once and was hushed.

He had been teaching them already — about prayer, about fasting, about treasure. Now he went on.

"Do not be anxious about your life," he said, "what you will eat or what you will drink."

His voice was clear in the open air. It carried down the slope to the people at the edges, past the path, past the wildflowers growing there.

"Do not be anxious about your body, what you will put on."

A man near the front shifted where he sat. His tunic was patched at the shoulder. Beside him a woman held bread wrapped in cloth on her lap — the midday meal, brought from home.

"Is not life more than food," Jesus said, "and the body more than clothing?"

He paused. The crowd waited. Then he lifted his hand and pointed upward.

Birds moved above the hillside. Small brown birds, common as dust, turning and dipping in the air above the ridge. They came and went against the blue sky in quick, purposeful lines. One dropped low over the grass, rose again, and vanished behind the trees at the crest of the hill.

"Look at the birds of the air," Jesus said. His hand followed one as it crossed overhead. "They do not sow."

The bird turned in a wide circle.

"They do not reap."

It dropped again toward the field below.

"They gather nothing into barns."

He lowered his hand. "And yet your heavenly Father feeds them."

The crowd was quiet. Some watched the sky where the birds still moved. Others watched Jesus.

"Are you not of more value than they?" he asked.

No one answered.

The wind came again, gently, pressing the grass flat in a slow wave across the slope. A child reached for a flower near her mother's knee. The mother pulled her hand back softly.

Jesus continued. "And which of you by being anxious can add a single hour to his span of life?"

The words were measured. He let them settle.

An old man near the middle of the crowd rubbed his knees with the heels of his hands. The sun was on his face and his eyes were narrowed against it. A young fisherman beside him sat with his arms around his legs, his chin on his knees, listening.

Jesus spoke again. "And why are you anxious about clothing?"

He turned on the slope and stretched his hand toward the edge of the path below, where the wildflowers grew thick. The lilies were bright there — some white as wool, some golden, their centers marked with lines of deeper color. They grew without order, pushing up between stones and grass, their stems thin but their petals wide open to the light.

"Consider the lilies of the field," he said, "how they grow."

The crowd looked where he pointed. The flowers moved slightly in the breeze. Their stems bent and straightened.

"They neither toil nor spin."

Some of the women in the crowd had spindles in their bags — they carried them everywhere, working wool into thread while they walked or waited. One woman touched the spindle at her belt, almost without thinking, and let her hand fall.

"Yet I tell you," Jesus said, and his voice rose, "even Solomon in all his glory was not arrayed like one of these."

A murmur ran through the crowd the way a ripple runs through water. Heads turned. People looked again at the flowers growing wild at the edge of the path — the same flowers they had walked past every day without stopping.

The petals caught the light. The gold ones shone. The white ones held their brightness in the shade of their own leaves.

Jesus let the silence last. Then he spoke.

"But if God so clothes the grass of the field, which today is alive and tomorrow is thrown into the oven —"

He paused. The people knew what he meant. Dried grass and stalks were bundled for fuel. Women gathered them from the hillsides after the rains stopped and the green things yellowed and curled. The ovens behind their houses burned hot with it.

"— will he not much more clothe you?"

He looked across the faces on the slope. The patched tunics. The worn sandals. The dusty hems. The cloth wrapped around the bread to keep it clean.

"O you of little faith."

The words were not loud. They were level and steady and spoken into the silence. A bird called from the ridge above. No one moved.

"Therefore do not be anxious," Jesus said. "Do not say, 'What shall we eat?'"

He let the question hang.

"'Or what shall we drink?'"

He waited again.

"'Or what shall we wear?'"

The three questions stood in the air, simple and familiar. These were the questions spoken in every household, at every table, in every field when the rain did not come. The people on the hillside had asked them all.

"For the Gentiles seek after all these things," Jesus said. "And your heavenly Father knows that you need them all."

A dry leaf blew across the path at the base of the hill. Down by the lake, the water was still and bright. A fishing boat sat motionless near the shore, its sail furled, its hull rocking gently in the shallows. The sun had shifted, and the shadows of the few scattered trees had grown shorter on the grass.

Jesus leaned forward on the slope. "But seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness," he said, "and all these things will be added to you."

The crowd sat still. A woman adjusted her shawl against the breeze. A man rested his hand on the head of the child beside him. The grain in the field below the road stood straight in its rows.

"Therefore do not be anxious about tomorrow," Jesus said, "for tomorrow will be anxious for itself."

The wind passed low over the hillside, bending the wildflowers along the path. The lilies held their bright faces open, white and gold, their petals thin enough to see light through.

"Sufficient for the day," Jesus said, "is its own trouble."

The birds turned above the ridge in slow circles. The crowd sat on the green slope with the sun on their shoulders, and the lilies along the path swayed once more in the wind and were still.
