The road that wound up from the lake passed through low fields where grain was beginning to stand in rows, green and thin, the stalks scarce a hand's breadth above the soil. Beyond the fields the hillside rose — not steep, but broad and open, and the grass upon it was thick from the latter spring rains. Wildflowers grew along the edges of the path in clusters of white and violet and deep gold, their petals trembling faintly in the wind that came up from the water.

People were already gathered upon the slope when Jesus arrived. They had come from the villages round about the lake — fishermen yet smelling of salt, women carrying small children upon their hips, old men leaning upon staves, young men standing in groups of two or three at the edges of the multitude. Some had spread their cloaks upon the ground to sit upon. Others simply sat in the grass with their sandals beside them. The sun was high and warm but not yet hot, and the sky above the ridge was clean and pale.

Jesus went up the hillside and sat down among them, higher upon the slope where the ground was made level somewhat. The multitude settled. Their speech fell away. A child cried out once and was hushed.

He had been teaching them already — of prayer, of fasting, of treasure. Now he went on.

"Be not anxious for your life," he saith, "what ye shall eat, or what ye shall drink."

His voice was clear in the open air. It carried down the slope unto the people at the edges, past the path, past the wildflowers growing there.

"Be not anxious for your body, what ye shall put on."

A man near the front shifted where he sat. His tunic was patched at the shoulder. Beside him a woman held bread wrapped in cloth upon her lap — the midday meal, brought from home.

"Is not the life more than meat," Jesus saith, "and the body than raiment?"

He paused. The multitude waited. Then he lifted his hand and pointed upward.

Birds moved above the hillside. Small brown birds, common as dust, turning and dipping in the air above the ridge. They came and went against the blue sky in quick, purposeful lines. One dropped low over the grass, rose again, and vanished behind the trees at the crest of the hill.

"Behold the fowls of the air," Jesus saith. His hand followed one as it crossed overhead. "They sow not."

The bird turned in a wide circle.

"Neither do they reap."

It dropped again toward the field below.

"Nor gather into barns."

He lowered his hand. "And yet your heavenly Father feedeth them."

The multitude was quiet. Some watched the sky where the birds yet moved. Others watched Jesus.

"Are ye not much better than they?" he asked.

No one answered.

The wind came again, gently, pressing the grass flat in a slow wave across the slope. A child reached for a flower near her mother's knee. The mother drew back her hand softly.

Jesus continued. "Which of you by taking thought can add one cubit unto his stature?"

The words were measured. He let them settle.

An old man near the midst of the multitude rubbed his knees with the heels of his hands. The sun was upon his face and his eyes were narrowed against it. A young fisherman beside him sat with his arms about his legs, his chin upon his knees, hearkening.

Jesus spake again. "And why take ye thought for raiment?"

He turned upon the slope and stretched forth his hand toward the edge of the path below, where the wildflowers grew thick. The lilies were bright there — some white as wool, some golden, their centres marked with lines of deeper colour. They grew without order, pushing up between stones and grass, their stems thin but their petals wide open unto the light.

"Consider the lilies of the field," he saith, "how they grow."

The multitude looked where he pointed. The flowers moved slightly in the breeze. Their stems bent and were made straight again.

"They toil not, neither do they spin."

Some of the women in the multitude had spindles in their bags — they carried them everywhere, working wool into thread while they walked or waited. One woman touched the spindle at her girdle, almost without thought, and let her hand fall.

"And yet I say unto you," Jesus saith, and his voice rose, "that even Solomon in all his glory was not arrayed like one of these."

A murmur ran through the multitude the way a ripple runneth through water. Heads turned. People looked again at the flowers growing wild at the edge of the path — the same flowers they had walked past every day without tarrying.

The petals caught the light. The gold ones shone. The white ones held their brightness in the shadow of their own leaves.

Jesus let the silence last. Then he spake.

"Wherefore, if God so clothe the grass of the field, which to day is, and to morrow is cast into the oven —"

He paused. The people knew his meaning. Dried grass and stalks were bundled for fuel. Women gathered them from the hillsides after the rains ceased and the green things yellowed and curled. The ovens behind their houses burned hot with it.

"— shall he not much more clothe you?"

He looked across the faces upon the slope. The patched tunics. The worn sandals. The dusty hems. The cloth wrapped about the bread to keep it clean.

"O ye of little faith."

The words were not loud. They were level and steady and spoken into the silence. A bird called from the ridge above. No one moved.

"Therefore take no thought," Jesus saith, "saying, What shall we eat?"

He let the question hang.

"Or, What shall we drink?"

He waited again.

"Or, Wherewithal shall we be clothed?"

The three questions stood in the air, simple and familiar. These were the questions spoken in every household, at every table, in every field when the rain came not. The people upon the hillside had asked them all.

"For after all these things do the Gentiles seek," Jesus saith. "And your heavenly Father knoweth that ye have need of all these things."

A dry leaf blew across the path at the foot of the hill. Down by the lake, the water was still and bright. A fishing vessel sat motionless near the shore, its sail furled, its hull rocking gently in the shallows. The sun had shifted, and the shadows of the few scattered trees had grown shorter upon the grass.

Jesus leaned forward upon the slope. "But seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness," he saith, "and all these things shall be added unto you."

The multitude sat still. A woman set her shawl aright against the breeze. A man rested his hand upon the head of the child beside him. The grain in the field below the road stood straight in its rows.

"Take therefore no thought for the morrow," Jesus saith, "for the morrow shall take thought for the things of itself."

The wind passed low over the hillside, bending the wildflowers along the path. The lilies held their bright faces open, white and gold, their petals thin enough to see light through.

"Sufficient unto the day," Jesus saith, "is the evil thereof."

The birds turned above the ridge in slow circles. The multitude sat upon the green slope with the sun upon their shoulders, and the lilies along the path swayed once more in the wind and were still.
