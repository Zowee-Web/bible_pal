The room lay beneath the street. Seven steps of stone led down unto the door. Within, the ceiling hung low, and the lamps were set in hollows along the walls — small vessels of clay filled with oil, their wicks floating and burning with thin threads of smoke that rose and did not scatter. The air was close and warm. It bore the smell of bodies pressed together, and of damp plaster, and the sharp bitterness of olive oil set alight.

Thirty souls were gathered there, or forty. They sat upon wooden benches set hard against the walls, or upon the floor itself with their backs to the cold stone. Some bowed their heads. Some gazed before them and saw nothing. A woman near the door held a sleeping child upon her shoulder, and the face of the child was turned into the fold of her mantle. An elder man sat in the corner with his hands laid open upon his knees, the palms turned upward — not as one who prayeth, but as one who hath ceased to hold fast to any thing at all.

They were weary. It could be seen in the manner of their sitting — the heaviness that lay upon their arms, the bowing of their backs. Dust covered their sandals. Their cloaks were mended and mended again. Whatever road had brought them to this room in the evening hour had not given them rest along the way.

A man stood near the far wall. He was not young. His tunic was without adornment, and his beard was turning grey at the edges thereof. Between both hands he held a parchment unrolled, and the weight of the remaining scroll hung below. He had been reading aloud for some time. His voice carried the careful measure of a man who desired that every word should find its mark.

He looked down upon the parchment, found again his place, and continued.

"Therefore—"

He stopped. He let the word abide alone. A few among them raised their eyes.

"Therefore, seeing we also are compassed about with so vast a company of those who have borne witness—"

He spake it slowly. The word *company* moved through the room as a thing passing among them. A young man near the middle of the left bench lifted his head. The elder in the corner drew his fingers inward, slowly, as one who catcheth at a thing nearly fallen from his grasp. The woman bearing the child shifted the weight upon her shoulder but turned not her gaze from the reader.

The reader waited. The lamps along the wall cast his shadow up behind him, large and unmoving.

"—let us set down every burden that presseth upon us."

His voice was level. It was not loud. The room was of such smallness that he had no need of loudness. He spake as a man speaketh when he layeth a heavy thing upon a table before thee.

"And the sin which doth so readily wind itself about our feet."

He let that word also rest among them. Somewhere above, beyond the walls, upon the street, the wheel of a cart ground across the paving stones. The sound passed away. The room was still again.

"And let us run with endurance the course which hath been appointed unto us."

A man upon the floor near the front drew up his knees and clasped his arms about them. A woman two benches behind pressed her knuckles hard against her mouth. The hands of the reader were steady upon the parchment, his thumbs set along the edges, holding it open.

He read the line which followed more slowly still.

"Fixing our eyes upon Jesus, who is both the beginning and the completion of our faith."

The lamps burned. The smoke curled upward and spread flat against the low ceiling. The reader drew breath. The parchment trembled once within his grip and then was still.

"Who, for the sake of the joy appointed before him, bore the suffering of the cross—"

His voice did not rise. Neither did it fall. It held.

"—counting the shame thereof as nothing—"

The sleeping child stirred, and the woman set her hand upon the child's back. The elder in the corner leaned forward by the breadth of an inch. The young man upon the left bench laid both hands flat upon the wood beside him, pressing down.

"—and hath taken his seat at the right hand of the throne of God."

The reader paused. He looked out upon them. Their faces were lined with weariness, their eyes reddened in the lamplight. Some among them had not moved. Some were leaning forward now, and the burden of their tiredness was yet upon their shoulders, but their heads were lifted, and they watched him, and they waited.

He looked down upon the parchment one final time. When he spake again his voice came lower and more even, measured out word by word, as though he were setting each one down before each soul in the room as bread is set upon a table.

"Consider him who endured such opposition from sinful men against his own person—"

He looked up.

"—that ye grow not weary, nor faint within your souls."

The last word settled in the room. The reader held the parchment open between his hands. The lamps burned in their niches. The smoke lay flat against the ceiling. The thirty or the forty sat in the low yellow light, their cloaks mended, their hands still, their faces turned toward him.
