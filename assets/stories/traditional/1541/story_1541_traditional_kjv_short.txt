The chamber was small, and the lamps burned low. Thirty souls, or near unto forty, sat upon benches along the walls, some leaning forward with their arms upon their knees, and some with their heads resting back against the stone. Their faces were drawn thin. Their hands lay still. The air was heavy with the smell of lamp oil and of sweat and of wool yet damp from the road.

A man stood near the far wall, and in his hands he held a letter. The parchment was unrolled unto the final portion. He had been reading for some good while. His voice was steady, yet not loud. He read as one who knoweth that those before him are weary.

He found his place, and he spake.

"Wherefore, seeing that we are compassed about with so great a company of those who have borne witness—"

He stayed his voice. A woman near the door shifted upon the bench. A child lay sleeping against her side. The man's eyes passed over the room, over the wearied faces, and returned again unto the parchment.

"—let us cast off every burden that doth press upon us, and the sin that windeth itself so readily about our feet."

Some among them sat straighter. One man loosed his arms from across his chest. The lamp nearest the reader gave a tremor, and the shadow upon the wall behind him stirred.

"And let us run with endurance the course that is appointed unto us."

His voice carried through the chamber as a thing passed from hand unto hand. Some looked upon the floor. Some looked upon him. One elder woman near the back pressed her lips together and gave a single nod, scarcely to be seen.

"Looking unto Jesus, who is both the beginning of our faith and the one who bringeth it unto completion."

He drew breath. The parchment trembled in his hands.

"Who, for the joy that was laid before him, endured the cross and counted its shame as nothing, and is set down at the right hand of the throne of God."

The room was still. None coughed. None stirred. The child against the woman's side breathed softly.

The reader lifted his eyes from the letter one last time. His voice came lower now, and slower, as though he were setting each word down upon the bench beside each soul.

"Consider him who endured such opposition from sinners against his own self, that ye grow not weary, nor faint in your hearts."

He held the parchment still. The oil lamps burned. The thirty or forty faces looked back at him in the low and yellow light, their shoulders heavy, their eyes yet open.
