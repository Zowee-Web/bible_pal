The room was below street level. You went down seven stone steps to reach the door. Inside, the ceiling was low and the lamps were set in niches along the walls, small clay dishes of oil with floating wicks that gave off thin lines of smoke. The air was warm and close. It smelled of bodies and damp plaster and the sharp edge of burning olive oil.

Thirty or forty people filled the room. They sat on wooden benches pushed against the walls, or on the floor with their backs against the stone. Some had their heads bowed. Some stared ahead at nothing. A woman near the door held a sleeping child against her shoulder, the child's face turned into the fold of her cloak. An older man in the corner had his hands open on his knees, palms up, not in prayer but in the way of someone who has simply stopped holding on to anything.

They were tired. You could see it in the way they sat — the heaviness in their arms, the slope of their backs. Their sandals were dusty. Their cloaks were patched. Whatever had brought them to this room tonight had not left them rested.

A man stood near the far wall. He was not young. His tunic was plain and his beard was going grey at the edges. He held a parchment unrolled between both hands, the scroll weighted at the bottom where the rest of the letter hung. He had been reading aloud for some time. His voice had the careful pace of a man who wanted every word to land.

He looked down at the parchment, found his place, and continued.

"Therefore—"

He stopped. He let the word sit. A few of them looked up.

"Therefore, seeing we also are surrounded by so great a cloud of witnesses—"

He said it slowly. The word "cloud" moved through the room. A young man near the middle of the left bench lifted his head. The older man in the corner closed his fingers slightly, as if catching something he had almost dropped. The woman with the child adjusted the weight against her shoulder but did not take her eyes off the reader.

The reader waited a moment. The lamps along the wall threw his shadow up behind him, large and steady.

"—let us lay aside every weight."

His voice was level. Not loud. The room was small enough that he did not need to be loud. He spoke the way a man speaks when he is placing something heavy on a table in front of you.

"And the sin which so easily entangles us."

He let that sit too. Somewhere above them, outside on the street, a cart wheel scraped across paving stones. The sound faded. The room was quiet again.

"And let us run with patience the race that is set before us."

A man on the floor near the front pulled his knees up and wrapped his arms around them. A woman two benches back pressed her knuckles against her mouth. The reader's hands were steady on the parchment, his thumbs along the edges, holding it open.

He read the next line more slowly still.

"Looking to Jesus, the author and finisher of our faith."

The lamps burned. Smoke curled upward and flattened against the low ceiling. The reader drew a breath. The parchment trembled once in his grip and then was still.

"Who for the joy that was set before him endured the cross—"

His voice did not rise. It did not fall. It held.

"—despising the shame—"

The sleeping child stirred and the woman shifted her hand to the child's back. The older man in the corner leaned forward an inch. The young man on the left bench had both hands flat on the wood beside him, pressing down.

"—and is seated at the right hand of the throne of God."

The reader paused. He looked out at them. Their faces were lined, their eyes red-rimmed in the lamplight. Some of them had not moved. Some of them were leaning forward now, the weariness still on their shoulders but their heads lifted, watching him, waiting.

He looked down at the parchment one final time. When he spoke again his voice came lower and more even, measured out word by word, as though he were setting each one down in front of each person in the room like bread on a table.

"For consider him who endured such hostility from sinners against himself—"

He looked up.

"—so that you do not grow weary and faint in your minds."

The last word settled in the room. The reader held the parchment open between his hands. The lamps burned in their niches. The smoke lay flat against the ceiling. The thirty or forty people sat in the low yellow light, their cloaks patched, their hands still, their faces turned toward him.
