The banquet hall of Belshazzar was loud at the high hour of the night. A thousand lords reclined along the tables, their robes heavy with embroidery, their cups always full. Music carried from the corners of the hall, the horn and the lyre and the harp, and the wine went around among them without pause. The king sat at the head of his lords on a raised seat. He lifted his cup. He drank wine before them all, and his lords drank with him.

While he tasted the wine, a thought came into him. He gave the command. Servants ran from the hall and down the long stair into the treasury beneath the palace, and they returned bearing the gold and silver vessels of the house of God, which Nebuchadnezzar his father had taken out of the temple in Jerusalem. They came back into the hall slowly, their arms full, and the lords turned to watch them come. The vessels were set upon the king's table. The lamplight caught them. The cups of the sanctuary stood among the cups of the feast.

The king lifted one of the holy vessels. His lords lifted theirs. They drank wine from them. And as they drank they praised the gods of gold and of silver, of bronze, of iron, of wood, and of stone. The names of the dead gods went up into the painted ceiling. The music kept time beneath them.

In the same hour the fingers of a man's hand came forth.

They came out beside the lampstand, against the plaster of the wall of the king's palace. There was no arm behind them. There was no body. There were only the fingers, moving in the light, and they began to write.

The king saw the part of the hand that wrote. The cup stopped at his lip. Across the wall the fingers moved, deliberate, unhurried, forming letters in the soft plaster as a scribe might form them in wax. One letter. Then the next. The lamp beside them did not flicker. The hand cast no shadow.

His face was changed in him. The flush of the wine drained away. His thoughts troubled him. The joints of his hips were loosed. His knees struck one against another beneath his robe, and the sound of it could not be heard above the music, but the king heard it in his own body.

He cried aloud. His voice cracked across the hall and the music broke off. The harpists set down their harps. The pipers lowered their pipes. The room fell into a quiet that was not yet silence — the cups still on the tables, the lords still half-reclined, the wine still in the holy vessels — and into that quiet the king's voice went again. "Bring in the wise men. Bring in the enchanters and the astrologers and the soothsayers. Bring them now."

Servants ran. The lords sat with their cups still in their hands and their eyes on the wall. None of them spoke. The fingers had finished their writing and were no longer there. Only the letters remained.

The wise men of Babylon came in. They came in from their houses and from their watch-rooms, robed and stumbling, blinking against the lamplight of the hall. The king rose from his seat to meet them, and his voice was loud and unsteady. "Whoever shall read this writing, and show me its interpretation, shall be clothed with purple, and have a chain of gold around his neck. He shall be the third ruler in the kingdom."

The wise men gathered before the wall. They lifted their hands toward the letters. They turned to one another. They turned back. They studied the shapes. They could not read them. They could not make the interpretation known to the king. One after another they stepped away from the wall and stood with their heads bowed. The king was greatly troubled, and his face was changed in him, and his lords were perplexed. The wine in the holy vessels was untouched now. No one would lift them.

Then the queen came into the banquet house.

She had heard the cry from her own rooms, and she crossed the long hall between the tables of the lords. Her step was even. She did not look at the vessels on the table. She did not look at the wine. She came up before the king and her voice was steady against the trembling of his.

"O king, live forever. Do not let your thoughts trouble you, nor let your face be changed. There is a man in your kingdom in whom is the spirit of the holy gods. In the days of your father, light and understanding and wisdom were found in him. The king, Nebuchadnezzar your father, made him master of the magicians and the enchanters, because an excellent spirit was found in him. Now let Daniel be called. He will show the interpretation."

The king sent for him.

He came up through the hall, an old man brought through the wine-stained lords until he stood before the young king. He was unhurried. He looked at the vessels upon the table. He looked at the lampstand and the wall beside it. Then he looked at the king.

The king spoke first. "Are you that Daniel of the children of the captivity of Judah, whom the king my father brought out of Judah? I have heard of you, that the spirit of the gods is in you, and light and understanding and excellent wisdom. If you can read the writing and make known to me its interpretation, you shall be clothed with purple, and have a chain of gold around your neck, and shall be the third ruler in the kingdom."

Daniel did not move toward the gifts. He answered before the king, and his voice did not rise. "Let your gifts be to yourself, and give your rewards to another. Yet I will read the writing to the king, and make known to him the interpretation."

He turned and looked at the vessels on the table.

"The Most High God gave Nebuchadnezzar your father the kingdom, and greatness, and glory, and majesty. But when his heart was lifted up, he was deposed from his kingly throne, and they took his glory from him. He was driven from the sons of men, until he knew that the Most High God rules in the kingdom of men."

He turned back to the king.

"You, his son, Belshazzar, have not humbled your heart, though you knew all this. You have lifted yourself up against the Lord of heaven. They have brought the vessels of his house before you, and you and your lords have drunk wine from them. You have praised the gods of silver and gold, of bronze, iron, wood, and stone, which do not see, nor hear, nor know. The God in whose hand your breath is, and whose are all your ways, you have not glorified. Therefore the part of the hand was sent from before him, and this writing was inscribed."

He stepped toward the wall.

The lamplight fell across the letters. Daniel read them aloud, one by one, into the hush of the hall. "Mene. Mene. Tekel. Upharsin."

He turned back to the king. The lords leaned forward in their seats.

"This is the interpretation of the thing. Mene: God has numbered your kingdom, and brought it to an end. Tekel: you are weighed in the balances, and are found wanting. Peres: your kingdom is divided, and given to the Medes and Persians."

The hall was quiet. The king did not speak for a long moment. Then he gave the command. They clothed Daniel with purple. They put a chain of gold around his neck. The proclamation was made concerning him, that he should be the third ruler in the kingdom. Daniel received the purple and the chain. He did not put on the look of a ruler.

In that night the kingdom passed from the hand of Belshazzar. Darius the Mede received it, being about sixty-two years old. The lamps of the banquet hall were left burning a long while after the lords had gone, and the holy vessels still stood upon the table, and the letters were still on the wall beside the lampstand when the morning came in over the broken city walls and the long quiet of Babylon settled in.
