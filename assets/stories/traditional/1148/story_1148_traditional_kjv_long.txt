The banquet hall of Belshazzar was loud at the high hour of the night. A thousand lords reclined along the tables, their robes heavy with embroidery, their cups ever full. Music carried from the corners of the hall, the horn and the lyre and the harp, and the wine went round about among them without pause. The king sat at the head of his lords upon a raised seat. He lifted his cup. He drank wine before them all, and his lords drank with him.

While he tasted the wine, a thought came into him. He gave the command. Servants ran from the hall and down the long stair into the treasury beneath the palace, and they returned bearing the golden and silver vessels of the house of God, which Nebuchadnezzar his father had taken out of the temple in Jerusalem. They came back into the hall slowly, their arms full, and the lords turned to behold them as they came. The vessels were set upon the king's table. The lamplight caught them. The cups of the sanctuary stood among the cups of the feast.

The king lifted one of the holy vessels. His lords lifted theirs. They drank wine therein. And as they drank they praised the gods of gold, and of silver, of brass, of iron, of wood, and of stone. The names of the dead gods went up into the painted ceiling. The music kept time beneath them.

In the same hour came forth fingers of a man's hand.

They came out beside the candlestick, against the plaister of the wall of the king's palace. There was no arm behind them. There was no body. There were only the fingers, moving in the light, and they began to write.

The king saw the part of the hand that wrote. The cup stopped at his lip. Across the wall the fingers moved, deliberate, unhurried, forming letters in the soft plaister as a scribe might form them in wax. One letter. Then the next. The lamp beside them flickered not. The hand cast no shadow.

His countenance was changed in him. The flush of the wine drained away. His thoughts troubled him. The joints of his loins were loosed. His knees smote one against another beneath his robe, and the sound thereof could not be heard above the music, yet the king heard it in his own body.

He cried aloud. His voice cracked across the hall and the music brake off. The harpers laid down their harps. The pipers lowered their pipes. The chamber fell into a quiet that was not yet silence — the cups yet upon the tables, the lords yet half-reclined, the wine yet in the holy vessels — and into that quiet the king's voice went forth again. "Bring in the wise men. Bring in the enchanters and the astrologers and the soothsayers. Bring them now."

Servants ran. The lords sat with their cups yet in their hands and their eyes upon the wall. None of them spake. The fingers had finished their writing and were no more there. Only the letters remained.

The wise men of Babylon came in. They came in from their houses and from their watch-chambers, robed and stumbling, blinking against the lamplight of the hall. The king rose up from his seat to meet them, and his voice was loud and unsteady. "Whosoever shall read this writing, and shew me the interpretation thereof, shall be clothed with scarlet, and have a chain of gold about his neck. He shall be the third ruler in the kingdom."

The wise men gathered before the wall. They lifted up their hands toward the letters. They turned one to another. They turned back again. They studied the shapes thereof. They could not read them. They could not make known the interpretation unto the king. One after another they stepped away from the wall and stood with their heads bowed. The king was greatly troubled, and his countenance was changed in him, and his lords were astonied. The wine in the holy vessels was untouched now. No man would lift them.

Then came the queen into the banquet house.

She had heard the cry from her own chambers, and she crossed the long hall between the tables of the lords. Her step was even. She looked not upon the vessels on the table. She looked not upon the wine. She came up before the king and her voice was steady against the trembling of his.

"O king, live for ever. Let not thy thoughts trouble thee, neither let thy countenance be changed. There is a man in thy kingdom in whom is the spirit of the holy gods. In the days of thy father, light and understanding and wisdom were found in him. The king, Nebuchadnezzar thy father, made him master of the magicians and the enchanters, because an excellent spirit was found in him. Now let Daniel be called. He will shew the interpretation."

The king sent for him.

He came up through the hall, an old man brought through the wine-stained lords until he stood before the young king. He was unhurried. He looked upon the vessels upon the table. He looked upon the candlestick and the wall beside it. Then he looked upon the king.

The king spake first. "Art thou that Daniel of the children of the captivity of Judah, whom the king my father brought out of Judah? I have heard of thee, that the spirit of the gods is in thee, and light and understanding and excellent wisdom. If thou canst read the writing, and make known unto me the interpretation thereof, thou shalt be clothed with scarlet, and have a chain of gold about thy neck, and shalt be the third ruler in the kingdom."

Daniel moved not toward the gifts. He answered before the king, and his voice rose not. "Let thy gifts be to thyself, and give thy rewards to another. Yet I will read the writing unto the king, and make known unto him the interpretation."

He turned and looked upon the vessels on the table.

"The most high God gave Nebuchadnezzar thy father the kingdom, and greatness, and glory, and majesty. But when his heart was lifted up, he was deposed from his kingly throne, and they took his glory from him. He was driven from the sons of men, until he knew that the most high God ruleth in the kingdom of men."

He turned back unto the king.

"And thou, his son, Belshazzar, hast not humbled thine heart, though thou knewest all this. Thou hast lifted up thyself against the Lord of heaven. They have brought the vessels of his house before thee, and thou and thy lords have drunk wine therein. Thou hast praised the gods of silver and gold, of brass, iron, wood, and stone, which see not, nor hear, nor know. And the God in whose hand thy breath is, and whose are all thy ways, hast thou not glorified. Then was the part of the hand sent from before him, and this writing was inscribed."

He stepped toward the wall.

The lamplight fell across the letters. Daniel read them aloud, one by one, into the hush of the hall. "Mene. Mene. Tekel. Upharsin."

He turned again unto the king. The lords leaned forward in their seats.

"This is the interpretation of the thing. Mene: God hath numbered thy kingdom, and finished it. Tekel: thou art weighed in the balances, and art found wanting. Peres: thy kingdom is divided, and given to the Medes and Persians."

The hall was quiet. The king spake not for a long moment. Then he gave the command. They clothed Daniel with scarlet. They put a chain of gold about his neck. The proclamation was made concerning him, that he should be the third ruler in the kingdom. Daniel received the scarlet and the chain. He put not on the look of a ruler.

In that night the kingdom passed from the hand of Belshazzar. Darius the Median received it, being about threescore and two years old. The lamps of the banquet hall were left burning a long while after the lords had gone, and the holy vessels yet stood upon the table, and the letters were yet upon the wall beside the candlestick when the morning came in over the broken city walls and the long quiet of Babylon settled in.
