Noah was a righteous man. That means he tried every day to do what was right and good. Among all the people living on the earth at that time, Noah stood out. He was blameless. Not perfect — but honest and faithful, the kind of person who kept his word and treated others fairly. And the most important thing about Noah was this. He walked with God.

Walking with God did not mean they went on hikes together through the fields. It meant Noah talked to God and listened to God. He paid attention to what God wanted. His life moved in step with God the way two friends walk side by side on a long road.

Noah had three sons. Their names were Shem, Ham, and Japheth. They lived together with their families.

But outside Noah's family, the world had become a very dark place.

The earth was corrupt in God's sight. That word — corrupt — means broken and rotten, like a piece of fruit that has gone bad all the way through. Violence filled the land. People hurt each other without thinking twice. They took what was not theirs. They were cruel when they could have been kind. Every thought in every heart seemed bent toward doing wrong.

God looked down at the earth. He saw everything — every act of cruelty, every broken promise, every person who had turned away from goodness. And it made God deeply sorry that he had created human beings. His heart was filled with pain.

So God made a decision.

He spoke to Noah. Just Noah. Out of everyone on the whole earth, God chose this one man to hear what was coming.

"The end of all flesh has come before me," God said. His voice was serious and sad. "The earth is filled with violence because of them. I will destroy them along with the earth."

Noah listened. The words were heavy, like stones dropping into still water.

But God did not leave Noah without hope. God had a plan, and Noah was part of it.

"Make yourself a ship of gopher wood," God said.

A ship. Not a small fishing boat or a raft for crossing a river. A real ship — the biggest thing anyone had ever been asked to build.

"Make rooms in the ship," God continued. "Cover it inside and outside with pitch." Pitch was a thick, dark, sticky substance. It filled up cracks and sealed seams so that no water could get through. Every surface of the ship — walls, floor, ceiling — would need to be covered with it.

Then God gave the measurements. "The length of the ship shall be three hundred cubits. Its width shall be fifty cubits. Its height shall be thirty cubits."

Three hundred cubits long. That was enormous. Longer than a whole row of houses standing in a line. Fifty cubits wide — broad enough to hold rooms on both sides of a center walkway. Thirty cubits tall — high enough for three full levels stacked on top of each other.

"You shall make a roof for the ship," God said. "Finish it to a cubit from the top." A cubit was about the length of a grown man's forearm. The roof would have a small opening near the top for air and light. "Set the door of the ship in its side. Make it with lower, second, and third levels."

Three decks. Lower, middle, and upper. Each one filled with rooms and spaces. One door in the side. One roof on top. Noah listened carefully to every measurement, every instruction.

Then God told Noah why.

"I am bringing a flood of waters on this earth," God said, "to destroy all flesh that has the breath of life from under the sky. Everything that is in the earth will die."

A flood. Not a river overflowing its banks or a heavy rain that fills the ditches. A flood that would cover everything — every valley, every hill, every path and village and field. Water rising higher and higher until nothing on land could survive.

Noah must have felt his heart beating hard. Everything? Everyone?

But God spoke again, and this time the words were different. They were a promise.

"I will establish my covenant with you."

A covenant. That was the strongest kind of promise there was. Unbreakable. God was making a deal with Noah, and God never breaks his word.

"You shall come into the ship," God said. "You, your sons, your wife, and your sons' wives with you."

Eight people. Noah and his wife. Shem, Ham, and Japheth and their three wives. They would all be safe inside the ship when the water came.

But it was not just the people.

"Of every living thing of all flesh," God said, "you shall bring two of every sort into the ship to keep them alive with you. They shall be male and female."

Two of every kind of bird — the eagles and the sparrows, the doves and the ravens. Two of every kind of animal that walks on the ground — the oxen, the deer, the lions, the rabbits. Two of every creature that creeps and crawls — the lizards and the beetles and the snakes. Male and female of each kind. All of them would come to Noah, and he would bring them into the ship.

"Take with you every kind of food that is eaten," God said, "and store it up. It shall be food for you and for them."

Food for eight people and two of every animal on earth. Grain and dried fruit and hay and seeds. Enough to last until the floodwaters went away. Nobody knew how long that would be.

Think about the size of this job. Noah had to cut down trees and shape them into beams. He had to build a frame three hundred cubits long. He had to divide the inside into three levels with rooms big enough for animals of every size. He had to seal every surface with pitch. He had to store mountains of food in the right places. And he had to do all of this on dry land, before a single drop of rain had fallen.

The people around him must have watched and wondered. What was this man doing? Why was he building an enormous ship where there was no water? Some of them probably laughed. Some probably shook their heads and walked away.

But Noah did not stop. He did not change his mind. He did not cut corners or skip steps.

The Bible says it simply. Noah did this. He did everything that God commanded him.

Every beam cut to the right length. Every room built to the right size. Every seam sealed with pitch. Every measurement followed exactly as God had spoken. The ship rose slowly on the dry ground, plank by plank, day by day.

Noah trusted God. He believed what God had said, even though the sky was clear and the ground was dry and everything around him looked perfectly normal. He picked up his tools each morning and went back to work. Because God had asked him to. And Noah walked with God.
