They laid hold upon one Simon, a Cyrenian, coming out of the country. He was walking into the city — perhaps for the feast, perhaps upon an errand. He had not been part of the procession. He had not been at the trial. He had not cried out in the courtyard. The soldiers drew him from the side of the road, laid the crossbeam upon his back, and compelled him to bear it after Jesus.

The wood was rough and heavy. Simon's shoulders bent beneath it. He walked behind the condemned man, step by step, through streets he may not have known well. The multitude pressed in from both sides — people standing against the walls, leaning from upper windows, gathered upon the steps. The procession moved slowly. Soldiers walked before and behind.

A great company of people followed. Among them were women which bewailed and lamented him. The sound rose above the murmur of the crowd and the tramp of the soldiers' sandals upon the stone.

Jesus turned unto them. His face was bloodied. His back was torn. His voice was steady.

"Daughters of Jerusalem, weep not for me, but weep for yourselves, and for your children. For, behold, the days are coming, in the which they shall say, Blessed are the barren, and the wombs that never bare, and the paps which never gave suck. Then shall they begin to say to the mountains, Fall on us; and to the hills, Cover us. For if they do these things in a green tree, what shall be done in the dry?"

Two others were led with him — malefactors, condemned to die. The three of them passed through the gate and out of the city. The road ascended toward the hill.

They came unto the place which is called Calvary. The ground was bare and rocky. A few sparse plants grew between the stones. The sky was open above them — wide and pale. The wind blew across the exposed hilltop.

There they crucified him. The soldiers laid him upon the wood. They drove the nails through his wrists and feet — the hammer blows echoing off the rock. They raised the cross and set it in the hole. His body jerked against the nails. Blood ran down the wood.

The two malefactors were crucified beside him — one on the right hand, the other on the left.

Jesus spake. His voice came through laboured breathing. "Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do."

The soldiers settled in beneath the crosses. They sat upon the ground and parted his raiment among themselves, casting lots — tossing a small bone or stone to determine who should receive each piece. His outer cloak, his coat, his girdle. They handled the cloth without looking up.

The people stood beholding. Some had followed from the city. Some had come from the roads without. They stood in companies — some near, some at a distance.

The rulers derided him. They stood among the multitude with their arms folded. "He saved others; let him save himself, if he be Christ, the chosen of God."

The soldiers also mocked him. They came up unto him with vinegar upon a sponge. "If thou be the king of the Jews, save thyself."

A superscription was set above his head upon the cross. The letters were written in Greek, Latin, and Hebrew: THIS IS THE KING OF THE JEWS.

One of the malefactors which were hanged turned his head and railed on him. His own body was wracked with pain, his chest heaving. "If thou be Christ, save thyself and us."

The other malefactor spake. His voice was strained. "Dost not thou fear God, seeing thou art in the same condemnation? And we indeed justly; for we receive the due reward of our deeds: but this man hath done nothing amiss."

He turned his face toward Jesus. The effort cost him. His arms were stretched out. His breath came in gasps.

"Lord, remember me when thou comest into thy kingdom."

Jesus turned unto him. "Verily I say unto thee, today shalt thou be with me in paradise."

The hill was quiet after that. The mocking faded. The multitude shifted and murmured but the words from the crosses ceased.

About the sixth hour — noon — darkness fell over all the earth. It came not as clouds rolling in but as a dimming, a draining of light. The sky darkened. The sun was darkened. The land went grey and then nearly black. It continued until the ninth hour — three hours of darkness in the midst of the day.

The multitude grew still. The soldiers sat in the dark. The women stood without moving. The only sounds were breathing and the wind.

The veil of the temple was rent in the midst — from the top to the bottom. The heavy curtain that divided the holy place from the most holy place split down the centre. No hand of man touched it.

Then Jesus cried with a loud voice. The sound carried across the hill and down toward the city.

"Father, into thy hands I commend my spirit."

Having said thus, he gave up the ghost. His head dropped forward. His chest ceased to move. His body hung still upon the wood.

The centurion stood at the foot of the cross. He had overseen the execution from the beginning — the nails, the lifting, the superscription. He had heard the words spoken from the cross. He had watched the darkness come and go.

He glorified God. He said, "Certainly this was a righteous man."

The people that had come together to that sight began to turn away. They walked down the hill in small companies, smiting their breasts. The road back into the city filled with people moving in silence. The spectacle was ended. The hill was emptying.

But not all departed.

All his acquaintance stood afar off. The women that followed him from Galilee were there — standing together upon the slope beneath the cross. They had journeyed with him through the towns and villages. They had been at the meals and the teachings and the healings. They had walked behind him on the road to Jerusalem.

They stood and beheld. They departed not. The hill grew quiet about them. The crosses stood against the darkening sky. The women remained.
