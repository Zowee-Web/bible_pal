They seized a man named Simon, a Cyrenian, coming out of the countryside. He was walking into the city — perhaps for the festival, perhaps on an errand. He had not been part of the procession. He had not been at the trial. He had not shouted in the courtyard. The soldiers pulled him from the side of the road, laid the crossbeam on his back, and made him carry it behind Jesus.

The wood was rough and heavy. Simon's shoulders bent under it. He walked behind the condemned man, step by step, through streets he may not have known well. The crowd pressed in from both sides — people standing against the walls, leaning from upper windows, gathered on steps. The procession moved slowly. Soldiers walked ahead and behind.

A great multitude of people followed. Among them were women who beat their chests and wailed. The sound rose above the murmur of the crowd and the tramp of the soldiers' sandals on the stone.

Jesus turned to them. His face was bloodied. His back was torn. His voice was steady.

"Daughters of Jerusalem, don't weep for me, but weep for yourselves and for your children. For behold, the days are coming in which they will say, 'Blessed are the barren, the wombs that never bore, and the breasts that never nursed.' Then they will begin to tell the mountains, 'Fall on us!' and tell the hills, 'Cover us!' For if they do these things in the green tree, what will be done in the dry?"

Two others were led with him — criminals, condemned to die. The three of them moved through the gate and out of the city. The road rose toward the hill.

They came to the place called The Skull. The ground was bare and rocky. A few sparse plants grew between the stones. The sky was open above them — wide and pale. The wind blew across the exposed hilltop.

There they crucified him. The soldiers laid him on the wood. They drove the nails through his wrists and feet — the hammer blows echoing off the rock. They raised the cross and dropped it into the hole. His body jerked against the nails. Blood ran down the wood.

The two criminals were crucified beside him — one on his right, one on his left.

Jesus spoke. His voice came through labored breathing. "Father, forgive them, for they don't know what they are doing."

The soldiers settled in beneath the crosses. They sat on the ground and divided his garments among themselves, casting lots — tossing a small bone or stone to determine who received each piece. His outer cloak, his tunic, his belt. They handled the fabric without looking up.

The people stood watching. Some had followed from the city. Some had come from the roads outside. They stood in clusters — some close, some at a distance.

The rulers scoffed. They stood among the crowd with their arms folded. "He saved others. Let him save himself, if this is the Christ of God, his chosen one."

The soldiers joined in. They walked up to him with sour wine on a sponge. "If you are the King of the Jews, save yourself."

A sign was placed above his head on the cross. The letters were written in Greek, Latin, and Hebrew: THIS IS THE KING OF THE JEWS.

One of the criminals hanging beside him turned his head and hurled insults. His own body was wracked with pain, his chest heaving. "Aren't you the Christ? Save yourself and us."

The other criminal spoke. His voice was strained. "Don't you even fear God, seeing you are under the same condemnation? We indeed justly, for we receive the due reward for our deeds, but this man has done nothing wrong."

He turned his face toward Jesus. The effort cost him. His arms were stretched. His breath came in gasps.

"Jesus, remember me when you come into your Kingdom."

Jesus turned to him. "Assuredly I tell you, today you will be with me in Paradise."

The hill was quiet after that. The mocking faded. The crowd shifted and murmured but the words from the crosses stopped.

About the sixth hour — noon — darkness fell over the whole land. It came not as clouds rolling in but as a dimming, a draining of light. The sky darkened. The sun failed. The land went gray and then nearly black. It lasted until the ninth hour — three hours of darkness in the middle of the day.

The crowd grew still. The soldiers sat in the dark. The women stood motionless. The only sounds were breathing and the wind.

The veil of the temple was torn in two — from top to bottom. The heavy curtain that separated the Holy Place from the Most Holy Place split down the center. No human hand touched it.

Then Jesus cried out with a loud voice. The sound carried across the hill and down toward the city.

"Father, into your hands I commit my spirit."

Having said this, he breathed his last. His head dropped forward. His chest stopped moving. His body hung still on the wood.

The centurion was standing at the foot of the cross. He had overseen the execution from the beginning — the nails, the lifting, the sign. He had heard the words spoken from the cross. He had watched the darkness come and go.

He glorified God. He said, "Certainly this was a righteous man."

The crowds who had come to watch began to turn away. They walked down the hill in small groups, beating their breasts. The road back into the city filled with people moving in silence. The spectacle was over. The hill was emptying.

But not everyone left.

All his acquaintances stood at a distance. The women who had followed him from Galilee were there — standing together on the slope below the cross. They had traveled with him through the towns and villages. They had been at the meals and the teachings and the healings. They had walked behind him on the road to Jerusalem.

They stood and watched. They did not leave. The hill grew quiet around them. The crosses stood against the darkening sky. The women remained.
