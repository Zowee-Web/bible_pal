A south wind blew softly from the south, warm against their faces. The sailors looked one to another. They had tarried for this — a fair wind to carry them along Crete toward the haven at Phenice. They weighed anchor. The chain came up dripping. The ship eased forward, her sails filling. They sailed close by the shore, the brown cliffs of Crete visible to starboard, the water blue and flat beneath them.

The change came swiftly. A tempestuous wind struck from the northeast — violent, cold. The sailors called it Euroclydon. It smote the ship broadside. The sails snapped taut. The mast groaned. The bow swung hard. The helmsman fought the tiller but the ship would not answer. She could not bear up into the wind. They let her drive — away from the coast, into the open sea.

They ran under the lee of a certain island called Clauda. The sea was somewhat calmer there — enough for the crew to labour. The boat had been towing behind upon a rope, half swamped. The men had much work to come by it, waves lifting and dropping it against the hull. They drew it aboard, water pouring from its sides. Then they used helps, passing heavy cables under the ship herself — about the hull from rail to rail — undergirding her to keep the planks from splitting. They strake sail, fearing the quicksands of Syrtis to the south. They let the ship drive, held back only by the drag.

The next day the tempest was worse. The ship rose upon waves that ran from horizon to horizon. Water poured over the rails. The men lightened the ship — sacks of grain dragged across the wet deck and heaved overboard. On the third day they went further. They cast out with their own hands the tackling of the ship — ropes, spare spars, wooden blocks. They strained at the weight, the deck pitching beneath them.

After that, days passed without change. Neither sun nor stars in many days appeared. The sky was flat grey — the same above and below, the horizon invisible. No small tempest lay upon them. The waves continued day and night, lifting the ship and dropping her. The men ceased to eat. They ceased to speak among themselves. They sat upon the heaving deck, holding fast to what they could.

All hope that they should be saved was taken away.

Paul stood forth. He braced himself against the mast and spake above the wind.

"Sirs, ye should have hearkened unto me, and not have loosed from Crete, and to have gained this harm and loss. And now I exhort you to be of good cheer: for there shall be no loss of any man's life among you, but of the ship. For there stood by me this night the angel of God, whose I am, and whom I serve, saying, 'Fear not, Paul; thou must be brought before Caesar: and, lo, God hath given thee all them that sail with thee.' Wherefore, sirs, be of good cheer: for I believe God, that it shall be even as it was told me. Howbeit we must be cast upon a certain island."

The men hearkened. The wind continued.

On the fourteenth night — fourteen days without sight of sun or stars — the ship was driven up and down in the Adriatic Sea. About midnight the shipmen deemed that they drew near to some country. Perhaps the sound of the waves changed, striking against something ahead. Perhaps they smelled earth upon the wind. They deemed that land drew nigh.

They sounded. The line ran out, then went taut. Twenty fathoms. They tarried, drifted further, and sounded again. Fifteen fathoms. The bottom was rising fast.

Fear took hold of them. If they fell upon rocks in the darkness, the ship would break asunder. They cast four anchors out of the stern — one after another, the heavy iron splashing into the black water. The ropes ran out and went taut. The ship swung, her bow pointing toward the unseen shore. They wished for the day.

Certain of the sailors devised a plan. They let down the boat into the sea, under colour as though they would cast anchors out of the foreship. Paul perceived it. He said unto the centurion and unto the soldiers, "Except these abide in the ship, ye cannot be saved."

The soldiers cut off the ropes of the boat. She fell away, struck the water, and was carried off into the darkness. Gone.

The hours crept toward dawn. The anchors held. The waves beat upon the hull.

As the sky began to lighten, Paul stood forth again. Two hundred threescore and sixteen souls were aboard.

"This day is the fourteenth day that ye have tarried and continued fasting, having taken nothing," he said. "Wherefore I pray you to take some meat: for this is for your health: for there shall not an hair fall from the head of any of you."

He took bread. He gave thanks to God in presence of them all — standing upon the wet deck of a breaking ship, anchored off an unknown shore. He brake the bread and did eat.

The men watched. Something changed among them. They reached for food. All took meat.

When they had eaten enough, they lightened the ship, casting out the wheat into the sea. The ship rose higher in the water.

When it was day, light spread across the waves. They knew not the land. But they discovered a certain creek with a shore. The shore was there.

The crew set to work. They cast off the anchors, letting the iron fall into the sea. They loosed the rudder bands. They hoisted up the mainsail to the wind. The ship began to move toward shore.

She struck. The forepart fell into a place where two seas met and drove hard fast. She remained unmoveable. The hinder part, still in deep water, was broken with the violence of the waves. The timbers cracked. Planks split. The hull came asunder piece by piece.

The soldiers drew their swords. Their counsel was clear — if any prisoner should swim out and escape, the guard responsible paid with his life. They purposed to kill the prisoners.

The centurion stayed them. He was willing to save Paul. He gave his commands: they which could swim should cast themselves first into the sea and get to land. The rest should follow — some on boards, some on broken pieces of the ship.

They went. Swimmers struck the cold water and made for the shore. Others laid hold upon boards and wreckage, the waves bearing them shoreward. Men gasped and held fast. One by one they reached the shallows. One by one they drew themselves upon the wet sand.

All of them escaped safe to land. Two hundred threescore and sixteen souls. Every one alive.
