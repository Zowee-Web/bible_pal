A south wind blew gently, and they thought their opportunity had come. They weighed anchor and sailed close along the coast of Crete. But soon a violent wind struck from the northeast — the kind the sailors called Euroclydon. The ship was caught. It could not face the wind. They gave way and were driven.

Running under a small island called Cauda, they managed to secure the ship's boat. They used cables to undergird the hull. They lowered the sea anchor and were driven along.

The next day they began throwing cargo overboard. On the third day, they threw the ship's tackle over the side with their own hands.

Neither sun nor stars appeared for many days. No small storm pressed on them. All hope of being saved was taken away.

Paul stood among them. "Men, I urge you to take courage. There will be no loss of life — only the ship. For this night an angel of God stood by me and said, 'Do not be afraid, Paul. You must stand before Caesar. God has granted you all those who sail with you.' So take courage. I believe God."

On the fourteenth night, drifting in the Adriatic, the sailors sensed land. They took soundings — twenty fathoms. A little farther — fifteen. Fearing rocks, they cast four anchors from the stern and prayed for daylight.

Some sailors tried to escape in the ship's boat. Paul warned the centurion. The soldiers cut the ropes and let it fall.

As dawn approached, Paul urged everyone to eat. He took bread, gave thanks before them all, and broke it. They were encouraged. All two hundred and seventy-six ate.

When day came, they saw a bay with a beach. They cut the anchors, loosened the rudders, hoisted the foresail, and made for shore.

The ship struck a reef. The bow jammed fast. The stern broke apart in the surf.

The soldiers planned to kill the prisoners. The centurion stopped them, wanting to save Paul. He ordered those who could swim to jump first. The rest followed on planks and broken pieces.

All two hundred and seventy-six reached land safely.
