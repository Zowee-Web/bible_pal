A south wind rose gently from the south, warm against their faces. The sailors looked at one another. They had been waiting for this — a fair wind to carry them along Crete toward the harbor at Phoenix. They weighed anchor. The chain came up dripping. The ship eased forward, sails filling. They sailed close along the shore, the brown cliffs of Crete visible to starboard, the water blue and flat beneath them.

The change came fast. A wind struck from the northeast — violent, cold. The sailors called it Euroclydon. It hit the ship broadside. The sails snapped taut. The mast groaned. The bow swung hard. The helmsman fought the tiller but the ship would not answer. It could not face the wind. They gave way and were driven — out from the coast, into the open sea.

They ran under the lee of a small island called Cauda. The sea was slightly calmer there — enough for the crew to work. The ship's boat had been towing behind on a rope, half swamped. The men hauled it in with difficulty, waves lifting and dropping it against the hull. They got it aboard, water pouring from its sides. Then they passed heavy cables under the ship itself — around the hull from rail to rail — undergirding it to keep the planks from splitting. They lowered the sea anchor to slow their drift. To the south lay the Syrtis — the great sandbars where ships ran aground and broke apart. They let the ship drift, held back only by the drag.

The next day the storm was worse. The ship rose on waves that ran from horizon to horizon. Water poured over the rails. The men threw cargo overboard — sacks of grain dragged across the wet deck and heaved over. On the third day they went further. They threw the ship's tackle over the side with their own hands — ropes, spare spars, wooden blocks. They strained at the weight, the deck pitching beneath them.

After that, days passed without change. Neither sun nor stars appeared. The sky was flat gray — the same above and below, the horizon invisible. The storm did not relent. The waves continued day and night, lifting the ship and dropping it. The men stopped eating. They stopped talking. They sat on the heaving deck, holding what they could.

All hope of being saved was taken away.

Paul stood. He braced himself against the mast and spoke over the wind.

"Men, you should have listened to me and not set sail from Crete. But now I urge you to take courage. Not one life will be lost — only the ship. This night an angel of the God whose I am and whom I serve stood beside me. He said, 'Do not be afraid, Paul. You must stand before Caesar. God has granted you all those who sail with you.' So take courage. I believe God — it will be exactly as I was told. But we must run aground on some island."

The men listened. The wind continued.

On the fourteenth night — fourteen days without sight of sun or stars — the ship was being driven in the Adriatic Sea. About midnight the sailors sensed something. Perhaps the sound of the waves changed, striking against something ahead. Perhaps they smelled earth on the wind. They sensed land was near.

They dropped a sounding line. It ran out, then went taut. Twenty fathoms. They waited, drifted further, and dropped it again. Fifteen fathoms. The bottom was rising fast.

Fear gripped them. If they struck rocks in the dark, the ship would break apart. They cast four anchors from the stern — one after another, the heavy iron splashing into the black water. The ropes ran out and went taut. The ship swung, bow pointing toward the unseen shore. They prayed for daylight.

Some sailors made a plan. They lowered the ship's boat over the side, pretending to lay anchors from the bow. Paul saw it. He said to the centurion and the soldiers, "Unless these men stay in the ship, you cannot be saved."

The soldiers cut the ropes. The boat fell away, hit the water, and was carried off into the dark. Gone.

The hours crawled toward dawn. The anchors held. The waves beat the hull.

As the sky began to lighten, Paul stood again. Two hundred and seventy-six people were aboard.

"This is the fourteenth day you have waited without food," he said. "Take some. It will give you strength. Not a hair will fall from your heads."

He took bread. He gave thanks to God before them all — standing on the wet deck of a breaking ship, anchored off an unknown shore. He broke the bread and ate.

The men watched. Something shifted. They reached for food. All ate.

When they finished, they threw the remaining wheat into the sea. The ship rose higher in the water.

Day came. Light spread across the waves. They saw land — a bay with a sandy beach. They did not recognize it. But the beach was there.

The crew moved. They cut the anchor ropes, letting the iron fall. They untied the lashings on the rudders and let them drop into the water. They hoisted the foresail and let the wind take it. The ship began to move toward the beach.

It struck. The bow drove hard into a sandbar and jammed fast. The stern, still in deep water, was exposed to the full violence of the waves. The timbers cracked. Planks split. The hull came apart piece by piece.

The soldiers drew swords. Their orders were clear — if any prisoner escaped, the responsible guard paid with his life. They planned to kill the prisoners.

The centurion stopped them. He wanted to save Paul. He gave his orders: those who could swim, jump overboard and make for shore. The rest — grab planks, broken timbers, anything that floated.

They went. Swimmers hit the cold water and stroked for the beach. Others clutched at boards and wreckage, the waves pushing them shoreward. Men gasped and held on. One by one they reached the shallows. One by one they dragged themselves onto the wet sand.

All of them made it. Two hundred and seventy-six people. Every one alive.
