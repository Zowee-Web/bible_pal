A south wind blew softly, and the sailors deemed their moment had come. They weighed anchor and sailed close by the shore of Crete. The water was calm. The wind was warm upon their faces.

Then the weather turned. A tempestuous wind struck from the northeast — Euroclydon. It came without warning. The ship was caught broadside. The sails snapped taut. The bow swung hard. They could not bear up into the wind. They let her drive, and were carried into the open sea.

Running under a certain island called Clauda, the crew laboured to come by the boat — straining at the ropes as the sea lifted it against the hull. They hoisted it aboard. Then they used helps, undergirding the ship to hold her together. They strake sail, fearing lest they should fall into the quicksands of Syrtis to the south. So they were driven.

The next day the tempest pressed harder. Waves lifted the ship and dropped her. Water poured over the rails. The men lightened the ship — sacks and crates heaved into the grey water. On the third day they cast out with their own hands the tackling of the ship.

Days passed. Neither sun nor stars in many days appeared. No small tempest lay upon them. The men ceased to eat. All hope that they should be saved was taken away.

Paul stood forth among them. He spake above the wind.

"Sirs, ye should have hearkened unto me, and not have loosed from Crete. But now I exhort you to be of good cheer: for there shall be no loss of any man's life among you, but of the ship. For there stood by me this night the angel of God, whose I am, and whom I serve, saying, 'Fear not, Paul; thou must be brought before Caesar: and, lo, God hath given thee all them that sail with thee.' Wherefore, sirs, be of good cheer: for I believe God, that it shall be even as it was told me. Howbeit we must be cast upon a certain island."

On the fourteenth night, as they were driven up and down in the Adriatic Sea, about midnight the shipmen deemed that they drew near to some country. They sounded — twenty fathoms. They went a little further and sounded again — fifteen fathoms. The bottom was rising.

They cast four anchors out of the stern and wished for the day. The ship swung upon her ropes in the darkness.

Certain of the sailors sought to flee out of the ship, having let down the boat under colour of casting anchors out of the foreship. Paul said unto the centurion, "Except these abide in the ship, ye cannot be saved." Then the soldiers cut off the ropes of the boat and let her fall off into the dark water. She was gone.

While the day was coming on, Paul besought them all to take meat. "This is the fourteenth day that ye have tarried and continued fasting, having taken nothing. Wherefore I pray you to take some meat: for this is for your health: for there shall not an hair fall from the head of any of you." He took bread, gave thanks to God in presence of them all, and when he had broken it, he began to eat. They were all of good cheer, and they also took some meat.

They lightened the ship further, casting out the wheat into the sea.

When it was day, they knew not the land. But they discovered a certain creek with a shore, into the which they were minded to thrust in the ship. They cast off the anchors. They loosed the rudder bands. They hoisted up the mainsail to the wind and made toward shore.

The ship fell into a place where two seas met. The forepart stuck fast and remained unmoveable. The hinder part was broken with the violence of the waves — planks splitting, timbers cracking.

The soldiers' counsel was to kill the prisoners, lest any of them should swim out and escape. But the centurion, willing to save Paul, kept them from their purpose. He commanded that they which could swim should cast themselves first into the sea and get to land. The rest he bade follow — some on boards, some on broken pieces of the ship.

So they all escaped safe to land. Two hundred threescore and sixteen souls. Every one alive.
