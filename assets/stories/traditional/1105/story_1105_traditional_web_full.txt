A south wind rose gently, and the sailors believed their moment had come. They weighed anchor and sailed close along the coast of Crete. The water was calm. The wind was warm.

Then the weather changed. A tempestuous wind struck from the northeast — Euroclydon. It came without warning. The ship was caught broadside. The sails snapped. The bow swung. They gave way and were driven into open water.

Running under the lee of a small island called Cauda, the crew managed to secure the ship's dinghy — straining at the ropes as the sea lifted it against the hull. They hoisted it aboard. Then they passed cables under the ship, undergirding the hull to hold it together. They lowered the sea anchor, fearing the sandbars of the Syrtis to the south. They drifted.

The next day the storm pressed harder. Waves lifted the ship and dropped it. Water poured over the rails. The men threw cargo overboard — sacks and crates heaved into the gray water. On the third day they threw the ship's tackle over with their own hands.

Days passed. Neither sun nor stars appeared. The sky was flat gray, indistinguishable from the sea. The men stopped eating. All hope of being saved was gone.

Paul stood among them. He spoke over the wind.

"Men, you should have listened to me and not set sail from Crete. But now I urge you to take courage. Not one life will be lost — only the ship. This night an angel of the God whose I am and whom I serve stood beside me. He said, 'Do not be afraid, Paul. You must stand before Caesar. God has granted you all those who sail with you.' So take courage. I believe God. But we must run aground on some island."

On the fourteenth night, drifting in the Adriatic Sea, the sailors sensed land approaching. They dropped a sounding line — twenty fathoms. A little farther — fifteen. The bottom was rising.

They cast four anchors from the stern and prayed for daylight. The ship swung on its ropes in the dark.

Some sailors tried to escape. They lowered the boat under pretense of laying anchors from the bow. Paul said to the centurion, "Unless these men stay, you cannot be saved." The soldiers cut the ropes. The boat fell into the dark water and was gone.

As dawn approached, Paul urged all two hundred and seventy-six to eat. "This is the fourteenth day you have gone without food. Take some — not a hair will fall from your heads." He took bread, gave thanks before them all, broke it, and ate. They were encouraged. All ate.

They lightened the ship further, throwing the remaining wheat into the sea.

Day came. They saw a bay with a sandy beach. They cut the anchors loose. They untied the rudder ropes. They hoisted the foresail and made for shore.

The ship struck a shoal. The bow drove in and stuck fast. The stern was exposed to the waves. It began to break apart — planks splitting, timbers cracking.

The soldiers drew swords, planning to kill the prisoners. The centurion stopped them. He wanted to save Paul. He ordered: swimmers jump first. The rest — hold onto planks and broken pieces.

They went. Swimmers stroked for the beach. Others clutched at wreckage. The waves pushed them shoreward.

All two hundred and seventy-six made it to land. Every one of them alive.
