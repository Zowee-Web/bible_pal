Early in the morning, Hagar stood at the doorway, the light just brushing the edges of the tents. Her son, Ishmael, waited beside her, quiet and watchful. Abraham rose up early, and took bread, and a bottle of water. He placed the provisions in her hand and looked upon her, saying no more. The camp stood silent, its people afar off. With heavy steps, Hagar departed into the wild land. Ishmael followed after her.

The sand beneath their feet was pale and hot, already warming as the sun rose higher. The wilderness of Beersheba sprawled out before them — bare, without end, and empty. They walked beneath an open sky. The only sound was the padding of feet and the shifting of the bottle upon Hagar's shoulder.

As the hours passed, the sun pressed down, fierce and unrelenting. The bread was eaten, morsel by morsel, the water sipped in a thin hope that it might endure. Each time Hagar lifted the bottle, it felt the lighter.

The day burned. The air shimmered. Ishmael's steps slowed, his countenance marked with thirst and weariness. Sweat ran down his cheeks, dust clave unto his skin. Yet there was no shade, no sound of water, not even the whisper of a breath of wind among the stones.

Hagar uncovered the bottle of water, and tipped it. Not a drop remained. She weighed it in her hand — it was empty, even as empty as the wilderness round about her. She looked upon her son, and saw that he could go no farther.

A clump of shrubs appeared — a small patch of thorns and scant shade in a world of glare. Hagar led Ishmael unto it, and cast the child under one of the shrubs. She spread what little shelter the shrub afforded above him. His eyes were closed, his lips parched.

She went away. Hagar could not bear to behold the death of her child. She went, and sat her down over against him a good way off, as it were a bowshot, her heart tight and sore. The sound of the lad's crying came unto her under that burning sky.

Sitting apart, Hagar lifted up her voice. Her sorrow brake loose, a sound caught betwixt despair and weariness. Yet the wilderness gave no answer, only the distant crying of her son.

And then, in the silence, the Lord heard the voice of the lad.

The angel of God called unto Hagar out of heaven, breaking open the loneliness of the wilderness. "What aileth thee, Hagar? fear not; for God hath heard the voice of the lad where he is. Arise, lift up the lad, and hold him in thine hand; for I will make him a great nation."

Hagar's breath was caught. The ache in her body faded for a moment as new hope kindled in her breast. She arose, her strength returning. She turned unto her son and reached forth her hand, trembling.

And God opened her eyes, and she saw a well of water — a glint of sunlight upon water. There, not far from where the lad lay, a well stood, hidden until that very moment. Hagar made haste, her steps quickened by relief. She filled the bottle with fresh water.

Returning unto Ishmael, she knelt and lifted up his head. She gave him to drink, drop by precious drop. His lips parted, and he drank, his strength restored with every swallow, his breath steadying in the dry air.

The wilderness held them, but it did not destroy them. The water from the well sustained them. The glare of the day lessened as hope reclaimed a place in Hagar's heart.

The lad grew strong once again, and arose, and took his place beside his mother. Days of wandering became days of living, and the wilderness was no longer only a place of loss, but of survival and of promise. Ishmael went on, abiding in the wilderness, learning its ways.

And God was with the lad as he grew. Ishmael waxed strong in the wilderness, finding his place in the dry lands. In process of time, he became an archer, skilled with the bow.

He dwelt in the wilderness of Paran. And his mother took him a wife out of the land of Egypt.
