The road unto the city of Sychar was quiet, as the sun stood high above, bright and warm. The sky held not so much as a single cloud, and the ground shewed its pale, dry dust, warming beneath the heat. There, without the borders of the city, rose an old and mighty well that the people called Jacob's well. Smooth stones builded the sides thereof, and cool water waited far below. The land round about stood still, silent save for the small sounds of dry grass moving in the gentle wind.

At midday, when most folk hid them in the shade, a woman of Sychar came forth bearing her waterpot, walking alone. The sun pressed down round about her, making the air to shimmer, yet she went on. The roads were empty; other women came in the morning or the evening, when it was not so hot. But this woman chose the lonely hour, her feet making quiet prints in the dust as she came toward the well. Her eyes were cast down, and her steps were slow. The world felt wide and empty, and she looked not to meet any man at Jacob's well.

But on this day, one was there. A man rested by the well, sitting wearied in the full sunlight. His robe bare the dust of many miles. This man was Jesus, and he came from Judaea, passing through Samaria on his way. He had journeyed far with his friends, but now he was there alone, for his disciples were gone into the city to buy meat. Jesus seemed wearied, yet his eyes were kind. As the woman drew near, the silence between them grew. The sound of water being lifted from the well seemed even louder.

She set down her pot and began to draw forth some water, taking heed not to look upon this stranger. But Jesus spake, breaking the stillness. "Give me to drink," he said. His voice was gentle but steady. The woman lifted up her eyes, and marvelled. She perceived by his garments and his manner that he was a Jew. The Jews have no dealings with the Samaritans, and a Jewish man almost never spake unto a woman of Samaria. She wondered wherefore he should ask drink of her.

She said, "How is it that thou, being a Jew, askest drink of me, which am a woman of Samaria?" The moment felt strange and new, quiet save for the echo of the well. Though she had come alone to hide herself away, now she could not but answer, caught by the kindness of Jesus' eyes.

Jesus answered her, "If thou knewest the gift of God, and who it is that saith to thee, Give me to drink; thou wouldest have asked of him, and he would have given thee living water." His words were quiet, yet they held a promise she did not understand. The woman looked again upon the stone well, and thought of the cool water below.

She said, "Sir, thou hast nothing to draw with, and the well is deep: from whence then hast thou that living water? Art thou greater than our father Jacob, which gave us the well, and drank thereof himself, and his children, and his cattle?" She thought upon the old stories of Jacob's well, and wondered how this wearied stranger could give aught better than that which was already there.

Jesus spake again, his words soft but strong. "Whosoever drinketh of this water shall thirst again: but whosoever drinketh of the water that I shall give him shall never thirst; but the water that I shall give him shall be in him a well of water springing up into everlasting life." The bright sunlight seemed not so harsh now, as she hearkened. His words held a wonder deep within them, somewhat she had need of, but could not name.

"Sir, give me this water," the woman said, "that I thirst not, neither come hither to draw." Her heart desired that which he offered—a rest from coming every day to the well in the full heat, and a thirst that should come no more. She knew not yet all that he meant, but her voice held a true longing.

Jesus said unto her, "Go, call thy husband, and come hither." The silence deepened again. The woman's eyes were cast down. She answered, "I have no husband." Her voice was quiet, almost a whisper. She told no more.

Jesus said unto her, "Thou hast well said, I have no husband: for thou hast had five husbands; and he whom thou now hast is not thy husband: in that saidst thou truly." His voice was not harsh nor judging. Rather, it was calm and accepting, as one that truly saw her. At once the woman knew that this stranger knew all things concerning her—her hurt, her lonely journeys, her secrets that others whispered of. Yet still he spake gently, with care and truth.

Marvelling and unsure, the woman said, "Sir, I perceive that thou art a prophet. Our fathers worshipped in this mountain; and ye say, that in Jerusalem is the place where men ought to worship." She desired to speak of somewhat besides her past, somewhat great and weighty. She pointed unto the high hills, where her people prayed, and unto Jerusalem afar off, whither the Jews went to worship God.

But Jesus said unto her, "Woman, believe me, the hour cometh, when ye shall neither in this mountain, nor yet at Jerusalem, worship the Father. Ye worship ye know not what: we know what we worship: for salvation is of the Jews. But the hour cometh, and now is, when the true worshippers shall worship the Father in spirit and in truth: for the Father seeketh such to worship him. God is a Spirit: and they that worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth."

The woman hearkened, her heart full of wonder and many questions. She tried to call to remembrance the stories of a Saviour that was to come—one that should tell them all things and help them to understand. Her voice trembled a little as she said, "I know that Messias cometh, which is called Christ: when he is come, he will tell us all things."

Jesus looked upon her and spake the truth she had longed to hear. "I that speak unto thee am he," he said. In that moment the world grew very still. The bright sunlight touched them both, the dust clave unto their feet, but she felt wrapped in somewhat gentle and true. Jesus had told her all things that ever she did, yet he had not turned away. Instead, he offered her living water and spake words of hope.

The road without the city yet was quiet, but the woman's heart was no longer lonely. She had met the Messias at Jacob's well, near Sychar, in the brightness of midday.

Just then, the sound of voices drifted up the road—his disciples returning, marvelling to see Jesus speaking with her. The woman set down her pot, her cares forgotten. Hope flickered within her, brighter than before. Leaving her waterpot, she hasted back toward the city, dust rising from her sandals, eager to declare that which she had found. As she ran, the air seemed lighter. She whispered unto herself, "Is not this the Christ?"—and for the first time, she believed it might be so.
