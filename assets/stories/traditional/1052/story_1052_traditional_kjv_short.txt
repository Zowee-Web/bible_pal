And it came to pass, when David had made an end of speaking unto Saul, that the soul of Jonathan was knit with the soul of David, and Jonathan loved him as his own soul.

It came to pass that selfsame day. Not over weeks or months of acquaintance. Something in David's words unto the king — or in the manner of his standing, yet bearing the dust of the valley and the blood of the giant upon him — reached Jonathan in a place that could not be told by custom or courtesy.

And Saul took David that day, and would let him go no more home to his father's house. David entered the king's service. He stood where soldiers stood, ate where the court did eat, slept within the walls of the palace.

And Jonathan and David made a covenant, because he loved him as his own soul.

Then Jonathan did that which none in the court had seen a prince do before. He stripped himself of the robe that was upon him — the robe which marked him as the king's son, woven and dyed, the garment of the heir — and gave it to David.

He gave him his garments, even to his sword, and to his bow, and to his girdle.

The armour that had been fitted unto his body. The sword that hung at his side, balanced for his grip. The bow wherewith he had trained since he was a lad. The girdle that held all things together.

A prince, standing in the court of his father the king, giving his very self piece by piece unto a shepherd boy who yet bore the smell of sheep and open fields. The robe, the armour, the sword, the bow, the girdle — all that said who Jonathan was — given freely, without condition, unto him whose soul was knit with his own.

David stood in Jonathan's garments. The robe hung different upon his frame — broader in the shoulders, shorter in its length. The sword belt drew tighter about his waist. Jonathan stood in what remained, stripped bare of every outward mark of rank. Neither of them moved to take anything back.
