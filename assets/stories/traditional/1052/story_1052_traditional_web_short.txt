When David had finished speaking to Saul, the soul of Jonathan was knit to the soul of David. Jonathan loved him as his own soul.

It happened that day. Not over weeks or months or years of acquaintance. Something in David's words to the king — or perhaps in the way he stood, still carrying the dust of the valley and the blood of the giant — reached Jonathan in a place that could not be explained by custom or courtesy.

Saul took David that day and would not let him return to his father's house. David entered the king's service. He stood where soldiers stood, ate where the court ate, slept within the walls of the palace.

And Jonathan made a covenant with David, because he loved him as his own soul.

Then Jonathan did something no one in the court had seen a prince do before. He stripped off the robe he was wearing — the robe that marked him as the king's son, woven and dyed, the garment of the heir — and gave it to David.

He gave him his armor. The leather and bronze that had been fitted to his own body.

He gave him his sword. The blade that hung at his side, balanced for his grip.

He gave him his bow. The weapon he had trained with since he was a boy.

He gave him his belt. The last piece. The thing that held everything together.

A prince, standing in the court of his father the king, handing his identity piece by piece to a shepherd boy who still smelled of sheep and open fields. The robe, the armor, the sword, the bow, the belt — everything that said who Jonathan was — given freely, without condition, to the man whose soul was knit to his.

David stood in Jonathan's garments. The robe hung different on his frame — broader in the shoulders, shorter in length. The sword belt cinched tighter around his waist. Jonathan stood in whatever remained, stripped bare of every outward sign of rank. Neither of them moved to take anything back.
