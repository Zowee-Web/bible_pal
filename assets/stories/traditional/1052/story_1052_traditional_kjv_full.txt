And it came to pass, when David had made an end of speaking unto Saul after the slaying of the Philistine, that the soul of Jonathan was knit with the soul of David, and Jonathan loved him as his own soul.

David yet stood before the king. His garment was stained with grass and the sweat of the valley floor. The sling hung loose from his girdle. His sandals were thick with dust. His hands were rough and brown — the hands of a shepherd — and they hung at his sides whilst the court looked on from the edges of the chamber. The light from the high windows fell upon the stone floor.

And Saul took him that day, and would let him go no more home to his father's house. The lad who had kept sheep in the hills of Bethlehem entered into the king's service. He stood where the men of war stood. He ate where the court did eat. He walked passages that smelled of cedar oil and the smoke of lamps and polished stone, far from the open pastures where the wind blew clean over the grass.

And Jonathan and David made a covenant, because he loved him as his own soul.

Then Jonathan reached up and unfastened the robe that was upon him. The clasp of bronze at his shoulder opened with a sharp sound. He drew the robe off — purple cloth, woven close, cut to his frame. The weight of it passed through his hands. He held it forth unto David.

And David took it. The cloth was heavier than aught he had worn before. It smelled of cedar and the smoke of the palace lamps. He held it against his breast with both arms.

Then Jonathan put off his armour. The breastpiece of leather had been fitted unto his chest through years of use. The straps had worn smooth grooves against his shoulders. The guard of bronze at his side bore a dent from a blow he had taken in battle and never hammered flat. He loosed each strap — one at the shoulder, one at the ribs, one at the waist. He lifted the pieces free. The leather was dark with his sweat. He set the armour in David's arms upon the robe.

He loosed his sword from his side. The blade hung upon his hip in a scabbard of brown leather, worn pale where it had rested against his thigh through years of marching. He drew the sword and held it flat across both palms. The edge caught the light. The grip was smooth from his hand. He laid it in David's hands.

He unstrung his bow. The wood was dark from years of oil and handling, bent to the shape his arm had drawn it a thousand times. The string went slack. He held the bow forth. David took it, setting it across the pile of armour and cloth already in his arms.

He removed his girdle. The wide strap of leather which held sword and quiver in their place. The buckle was warm from his body. He drew it free, wound it once, and laid it upon the rest.

Jonathan stepped back. The air was cool upon his arms where the armour had been. His tunic — the plain linen beneath — hung loose without the girdle to hold it. His hands were empty. He stood in the court of his father's palace with nothing upon him that marked his station.

David stood before him. The robe hung over one arm. The breastpiece and guard sat against his chest. The sword lay across the armour. The bow leaned against his shoulder. The girdle was coiled atop. He held all of it and set none of it down. The purple cloth shewed bright against his dust-stained garment.

The court was still. The stone floor stretched between them. Somewhere beyond the high windows a bird called twice and was silent. The light shifted as a cloud passed over. Jonathan's hands hung at his sides. David's arms were full. Neither of them moved.
