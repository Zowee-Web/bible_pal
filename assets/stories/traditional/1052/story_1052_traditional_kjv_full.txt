And it came to pass, when David had made an end of speaking unto Saul after the slaying of the Philistine, that the soul of Jonathan was knit with the soul of David, and Jonathan loved him as his own soul.

It came to pass that selfsame day. Not over weeks of acquaintance nor months of shared bread. Not through the slow gathering of trust that cometh of labouring side by side. Something moved betwixt them at once — something in David's voice as he spake unto the king, or in the manner of his standing there, yet covered with the dust of the valley floor, yet bearing the weight of what he had done. His garment was stained with grass and sweat. The sling hung loose from his girdle. Jonathan, the king's son, the prince of Israel, the warrior who had himself smitten the Philistines and prevailed, looked upon this shepherd lad and knew him.

And Saul took him that day, and would let him go no more home to his father's house. The boy who had kept sheep in the hills of Bethlehem entered into the king's service. He stood where the men of war stood. He ate where the court did eat. He walked the passages of the palace that smelled of cedar oil and the smoke of lamps and polished stone, far from the open pastures where the wind blew clean over the grass.

And Jonathan and David made a covenant, because he loved him as his own soul.

Then Jonathan did that which none in the court had beheld before. He took the robe that was upon him and stripped it off — the robe which marked him as the king's son. The cloth was dyed a deep purple, woven close and cut to his frame, the garment that said unto every eye in every chamber: this is the heir. The bronze clasp at his shoulder opened with a sharp sound. He drew the robe from his shoulders, the weight of it passing through his hands, and held it forth unto David.

And David took it. The cloth was heavier than aught he had worn. It smelled of cedar and the smoke of the palace.

Then Jonathan put off his armour. The breastpiece of leather fitted unto his chest, the guard of bronze at his side — the protection he bore into battle, shaped to his body through years of war, the leather darkened with his sweat. He loosed the straps one by one and lifted the pieces free. He gave them unto David.

He loosed his sword from his side. The blade that hung upon his hip, balanced for his hand, the edge he had kept whetted since he was old enough to draw it forth. The sheath was worn smooth where it had rested upon his thigh through years of marching. He laid it in David's hands.

He unstrung his bow. The weapon wherewith he had trained since he was a youth, the curve of it as familiar as his own arm, the grip polished smooth by his palm. He gave it unto David.

He removed his girdle. The wide strap of leather which held all in its place — sword, quiver, the instruments of a prince and a soldier. The buckle was warm from his body. The last piece. He gave it unto David.

And Jonathan stood in the court of his father's palace, stripped of every outward token of who he was. The robe, the armour, the sword, the bow, the girdle — the whole identity of the king's son, the warrior, the heir — laid across the arms of a shepherd boy from Bethlehem who yet bore the smell of sheep and lanolin and wind.

David stood in Jonathan's garments. The robe hung different upon his frame — broader in the shoulders yet shorter in its length, the hem falling above his ankles where it had reached to Jonathan's shins. The sword belt drew tighter about his narrower waist. The armour sat otherwise upon his chest, the leather stiff against unfamiliar sinew. He wore another man's life, and that man had given it freely.

No priest stood between them. No altar was built. No witnesses were summoned to seal the terms. No scribe set down the words upon a scroll. Jonathan stood with empty hands in the court where he had once worn every mark of his station, the cool air upon his arms where the armour had been. David stood bearing the weight of all of it — robe, blade, bow, girdle. The light from the high windows fell across them both, the prince stripped bare and the shepherd newly clothed, and neither moved to undo what had been done.
