When David had finished speaking to Saul after the killing of the Philistine, the soul of Jonathan was knit to the soul of David. Jonathan loved him as his own soul.

It happened that day. Not over weeks of acquaintance or months of shared meals. Not through the slow accumulation of trust that comes from working side by side. Something immediate moved between them — something in David's voice as he spoke to the king, or in the way he stood there still covered in the dust of the valley floor, still carrying the weight of what he had done. His tunic was stained with grass and sweat. The sling hung loose from his belt. Jonathan, the king's son, the prince of Israel, the warrior who had himself fought Philistines and won, looked at this shepherd boy and knew him.

Saul took David that day and would not let him return to his father's house. The boy who had tended sheep in the hills of Bethlehem entered the king's service. He stood where the soldiers stood. He ate where the court ate. He walked the corridors of the palace that smelled of cedar oil and lamp smoke and polished stone, a long way from the open pastures where the wind blew clean over the grass.

And Jonathan made a covenant with David, because he loved him as his own soul.

Then Jonathan did something that no one in the court had seen before. He reached up and unfastened the robe he was wearing — the robe that marked him as the king's son. The fabric was dyed deep purple, woven tight and cut to fit his frame, the garment that said to every eye in every room: this is the heir. The bronze clasp at the shoulder clicked open. He drew the robe off his shoulders, the weight of it sliding through his hands, and held it out to David.

David took it. The fabric was heavier than anything he had worn. It smelled of cedar and palace smoke.

Then Jonathan removed his armor. The leather breastpiece fitted to his chest, the bronze guard at his side — the protection he wore into battle, shaped to his body over years of use, the leather darkened with his sweat. He unbuckled the straps one by one and lifted the pieces free. He gave them to David.

He unbuckled his sword. The blade that hung at his hip, balanced for his hand, the edge he had kept sharp since he was old enough to draw it. The scabbard was worn smooth where it had rested against his thigh through years of marching. He placed it in David's hands.

He unstrung his bow. The weapon he had trained with since boyhood, the curve of it familiar as his own arm, the grip polished smooth by his palm. He gave it to David.

He removed his belt. The wide leather strap that held everything in place — sword, quiver, the tools of a prince and a soldier. The buckle was warm from his body. The last piece. He gave it to David.

Jonathan stood in the court of his father's palace, stripped of every outward sign of who he was. The robe, the armor, the sword, the bow, the belt — the complete identity of the king's son, the warrior, the heir — laid across the arms of a shepherd boy from Bethlehem who still smelled of sheep and lanolin and wind.

David stood in Jonathan's garments. The robe hung different on his frame — broader in the shoulders but shorter in the length, the hem falling above his ankles where it had fallen to Jonathan's shins. The sword belt cinched tighter around his narrower waist. The armor sat differently on his chest, the leather stiff against unfamiliar muscle. He wore another man's life, and that man had given it freely.

No priest presided. No altar was built. No witnesses were called to seal the terms. No scribe recorded the words on a scroll. Jonathan stood with empty hands in the court where he had once worn every mark of his station, the cool air on his arms where the armor had been. David stood bearing the weight of all of it — robe, blade, bow, belt. The light from the high windows fell across them both, the prince stripped bare and the shepherd newly clothed, and neither moved to undo what had been done.
