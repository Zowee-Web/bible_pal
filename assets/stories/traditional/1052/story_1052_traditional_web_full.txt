When David had finished speaking to Saul after the killing of the Philistine, the soul of Jonathan was knit to the soul of David. Jonathan loved him as his own soul.

David still stood before the king. His tunic was stained with grass and sweat from the valley floor. The sling hung loose from his belt. His sandals were caked with dust. His hands were rough and brown — shepherd's hands — and they hung at his sides while the court watched from the edges of the room. The light from the high windows fell across the stone floor.

Saul took David that day and would not let him return to his father's house. The boy who had tended sheep in the hills of Bethlehem entered the king's service. He stood where the soldiers stood. He ate where the court ate. He walked corridors that smelled of cedar oil and lamp smoke and polished stone, a long way from the open pastures where the wind blew clean over the grass.

And Jonathan made a covenant with David, because he loved him as his own soul.

Then Jonathan reached up and unfastened the robe he was wearing. The bronze clasp at his shoulder clicked open. He drew the robe off — purple cloth, woven tight, cut to his frame. The weight of it slid through his hands. He held it out to David.

David took it. The fabric was heavier than anything he had worn before. It smelled of cedar and the smoke of the palace lamps. He held it against his chest with both arms.

Then Jonathan removed his armor. The leather breastpiece had been fitted to his chest over years of use. The straps had worn smooth grooves against his shoulders. The bronze guard at his side carried a dent from a blow he had taken in battle and never hammered flat. He unbuckled each strap — one at the shoulder, one at the ribs, one at the waist. He lifted the pieces free. The leather was dark with his sweat. He set the armor in David's arms on top of the robe.

He unbuckled his sword. The blade hung at his hip in a scabbard of brown leather, worn pale where it had rested against his thigh through years of marching. He drew the sword and held it flat across both palms. The edge caught the light. The grip was polished smooth from his hand. He laid it in David's hands.

He unstrung his bow. The wood was dark from years of oil and handling, curved to the shape his arm had drawn it a thousand times. The string went slack. He held the bow out. David took it, balancing it across the pile of armor and cloth already in his arms.

He removed his belt. The wide leather strap that held sword and quiver in place. The buckle was warm from his body. He pulled it free, coiled it once, and placed it on top of the rest.

Jonathan stepped back. The air was cool on his arms where the armor had been. His tunic — the plain linen underneath — hung loose without the belt to hold it. His hands were empty. He stood in the court of his father's palace with nothing on him that marked his rank.

David stood across from him. The robe draped over one arm. The breastpiece and guard sat against his chest. The sword lay across the armor. The bow leaned against his shoulder. The belt was coiled on top. He held all of it and did not set any of it down. The purple cloth was bright against his dust-stained tunic.

The court was quiet. The stone floor stretched between them. Somewhere outside the high windows a bird called twice and stopped. The light shifted as a cloud moved. Jonathan's hands hung at his sides. David's arms were full. Neither of them moved.
