The twelve spies had been gone forty days. The children of Israel waited at Kadesh in the wilderness of Paran, dwelling in their tents with nought to do but watch the horizon toward the north and wonder. The heat pressed down upon the camp. Cookfires sent thin columns of smoke into the still air. Dogs lay panting in the shade of the tent walls. Children chased lizards betwixt the tent ropes and grew weary and quarrelled.

Then they returned.

Two men appeared first, walking slowly beneath the burden they bare. Betwixt them they held upon a staff a single cluster of grapes so heavy it bowed the wood. The grapes were dark and round and covered with the bloom of a land the people had never beheld. Their skins were tight, near to bursting, and some few had split upon the journey, leaving trails of juice along the staff. Behind the two came the rest of the twelve, and some of them bare pomegranates, their rinds smooth and deep red, and figs, soft and heavy in their hands, the stems yet green where they had been cut from the trees.

The congregation gathered round about them. Moses stood with Aaron at the fore. The people pressed nigh, shoulder against shoulder, rising upon their toes to see over one another. They could smell the fruit — the sweetness of the grapes, the green savour of the pomegranate rind — and the smell was unlike aught in the desert, rich and wet and full of life.

The spies gave their report.

"We came unto the land whither thou sentest us, and surely it floweth with milk and honey; and this is the fruit of it." They laid the grapes and the pomegranates and the figs before the assembly upon a spread cloth. The cluster of grapes alone was enough to feed a dozen families. Flies found the split fruit nigh at once, circling above the juice.

For a moment the camp was still with wonder. A woman near the front covered her mouth with her hand.

Then the selfsame men who held up the fruit opened their mouths again, and their words changed all.

"Nevertheless." The word fell as a stone into still water. "The people be strong that dwell in the land, and the cities are walled, and very great." Voices in the multitude drew sharp breath. A child began to weep. "And moreover we saw the children of Anak there."

The Amalekites dwell in the land of the south. The Hittites and the Jebusites and the Amorites dwell in the mountains. The Canaanites dwell by the sea and by the coast of Jordan.

The murmuring began low — a sound as of bees in a wall — and then it grew. It moved through the multitude from front to back, building as it went. Men shifted upon their feet and crossed their arms. Women drew their children close and looked one upon another with set mouths. Faces that had been bright with wonder grew pale and drawn. An old man nigh unto Moses sat down in the dust and put his head in his hands.

And Caleb pressed forward through the throng and stilled the people before Moses. He was not a man of many words, but his voice carried over the host as the sound of a trumpet.

"Let us go up at once and possess it; for we are well able to overcome it."

The crowd held still. For the space of a breath it might have gone either way. Caleb stood alone in the open place before the people, his jaw set fast, his hands at his sides.

But the men that went up with him said, "We be not able to go up against the people; for they are stronger than we." Ten voices against one. The weight of the greater number pressed down as a millstone.

And they brought up an evil report of the land which they had searched unto the children of Israel, saying, "The land, through which we have gone to search it, is a land that eateth up the inhabitants thereof; and all the people that we saw in it are men of a great stature. And there we saw the giants, the sons of Anak, which come of the giants: and we were in our own sight as grasshoppers, and so we were in their sight."

Grasshoppers. The word went through the camp as a sickness. It passed from mouth to mouth, from tent to tent. The men who had borne proof of abundance now bore only fear, and it spread faster than any tidings of honey or milk.

The grapes hung yet from the staff, their weight bowing the wood. The pomegranates sat untouched in the dust, their dark rinds gathering a film of sand. The figs softened in the heat, their skins darkening and splitting open. The proof of the promise lay upon the ground before the whole assembly, the flies circling above, and none reached for it.
