The twelve spies returned after forty days. They came back to Moses and Aaron and the whole congregation at Kadesh in the wilderness of Paran. Two men walked at the front bearing a single cluster of grapes on a pole between them. The cluster hung heavy, its fruit dark and swollen, the skins tight with juice. Behind them came others carrying pomegranates and figs, the rinds smooth, the figs soft in their hands.

The congregation gathered close. Dust rose under hundreds of sandaled feet. The spies gave their report.

"We came to the land where you sent us. It flows with milk and honey, and this is its fruit." They held the grapes high. The people pressed forward, eyes wide. Children pushed between their parents' legs to see. The scent of ripe fruit carried on the dry desert air, sweet against the familiar smell of sand and goatskin.

Then the voices changed.

"However, the people who dwell in the land are strong. The cities are fortified and very large. And we saw the descendants of Anak there." The Amalekites in the south. The Hittites and Jebusites and Amorites in the hill country. The Canaanites by the sea and along the Jordan.

The murmuring started low and spread through the crowd like wind through dry grass. Men shifted on their feet. Women pulled children close. Hands that had been reaching toward the fruit dropped back to their sides.

Caleb quieted the people before Moses. He stepped forward and stood where they could see him, his sandals planted in the dust. He spoke over the murmuring. "Let us go up at once and occupy it, for we are well able to overcome it."

But the men who had gone with him said, "We are not able to go up against the people, for they are stronger than we are."

They brought a bad report to the people of Israel. "The land through which we have gone to spy it out is a land that devours its inhabitants. All the people that we saw in it are of great height. And there we saw the Nephilim, the sons of Anak, who come from the Nephilim. We seemed to ourselves like grasshoppers, and so we seemed to them."

The grapes still hung from the pole, bending the wood with their weight. The pomegranates lay in the dust where they had been set down. The figs softened in the heat, their skins splitting. No one reached for them.
