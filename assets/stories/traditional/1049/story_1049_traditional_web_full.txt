The twelve spies had been gone for forty days. The people of Israel waited at Kadesh in the wilderness of Paran, camped in their tents with nothing to do but watch the northern horizon and wonder. The heat pressed down on the camp. Cookfires sent thin columns of smoke into the still air. Dogs panted in the shade of the tent walls. Children chased lizards between the guy ropes and grew bored and fought.

Then they came back.

Two men appeared first, walking slowly under the weight of what they carried. Between them they held a pole, and hanging from the pole was a single cluster of grapes so heavy it sagged the wood. The grapes were dark and round and dusted with the bloom of a land the people had never seen. Their skins were tight, nearly bursting, and a few had split open on the journey, leaving trails of sticky juice down the pole. Behind the two men came the rest of the twelve, and some of them carried pomegranates, their rinds smooth and dark red, and figs, soft and heavy in their hands, the stems still green where they had been cut from the trees.

The congregation gathered around them. Moses stood with Aaron at the front. The people pressed close, shoulder to shoulder, standing on their toes to see over one another's heads. They could smell the fruit — the sweetness of the grapes, the green scent of the pomegranate skin — and the smell was different from anything in the desert, rich and wet and alive.

The spies gave their report.

"We came to the land to which you sent us. It flows with milk and honey, and this is its fruit." They laid the grapes and the pomegranates and the figs before the assembly on a spread cloth. The cluster of grapes alone was enough to feed a dozen families. Flies found the split fruit almost immediately, buzzing in small circles around the juice.

For a moment the camp was quiet with wonder. A woman near the front covered her mouth with her hand.

Then the same men who held up the fruit opened their mouths again, and their words changed everything.

"However." The word fell like a stone into still water. "The people who dwell in the land are strong. The cities are fortified and very large." Voices in the crowd drew sharp breaths. A child began to cry. "And besides, we saw the descendants of Anak there."

The Amalekites dwell in the land of the Negeb. The Hittites, the Jebusites, and the Amorites dwell in the hill country. The Canaanites dwell by the sea and along the Jordan.

The murmuring started low — a sound like bees in a wall — and then it grew. It moved through the crowd from the front to the back, building as it traveled. Men shifted on their feet and crossed their arms. Women pulled children closer and looked at one another with tight mouths. Faces that had been bright with wonder turned pale and drawn. An old man near Moses sat down in the dust and put his head in his hands.

Caleb stepped forward. He pushed through the press of bodies and quieted the people before Moses. He was not a man of many words, but his voice carried across the assembly like a trumpet.

"Let us go up at once and occupy it, for we are well able to overcome it."

The crowd held still. For a breath it could have gone either way. Caleb stood alone in the open space before the people, his jaw set, his hands at his sides.

But the men who had gone up with him said, "We are not able to go up against the people, for they are stronger than we are." Ten voices against one. The weight of the majority pressed down like a millstone.

And they brought to the people of Israel a bad report of the land they had spied out. "The land through which we have gone to spy it out is a land that devours its inhabitants, and all the people that we saw in it are of great height. And there we saw the Nephilim — the sons of Anak, who come from the Nephilim — and we seemed to ourselves like grasshoppers, and so we seemed to them."

Grasshoppers. The word went through the camp like a sickness. It passed from mouth to mouth, tent to tent. The men who had carried proof of abundance now carried only fear, and it spread faster than any news of honey or milk.

The grapes still hung from the pole, their weight bending the wood. The pomegranates sat untouched in the dust, their dark rinds gathering a film of sand. The figs softened in the heat, their skins darkening and splitting open. The proof of the promise lay on the ground before the whole assembly, the flies circling above it, and no one reached for it.
