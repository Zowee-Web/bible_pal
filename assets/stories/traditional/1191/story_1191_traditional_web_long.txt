The wall stood whole around the city.

For weeks the workers had carried stone and timber up the slopes, and the masons had set block to block, and the watchmen had stood at half-built towers with weapons within reach. Now the wall ran the length of Jerusalem. It met itself at every corner. No breach was left in it. The doors of the gates had not yet been hung in their frames, but the long line of stone was joined and unbroken, and a man could walk it from the Fish Gate around to the Valley Gate and find no opening on his way.

Nehemiah walked the top of it in the late light. The sun was low over the western country. Below him the streets were quieting. Workers had set down their tools and gone to their houses. The smoke of evening fires rose between the roofs. He laid his hand on the stone at the parapet and felt it warm from the day.

Word of the finished wall went out across the province. It came to Sanballat in Samaria. It came to Tobiah the Ammonite. It came to Geshem the Arabian. And what they heard grieved them. They had spoken against the work from its first day. They had laughed at the rubble. They had sent threats by night. Now the rubble was gone, and the wall was up.

A messenger came to Nehemiah at the gate the next morning. He carried a letter from Sanballat and Geshem. Nehemiah broke the seal and read.

"Come, let us meet together in some one of the villages in the plain of Ono."

He folded the letter slowly. The plain of Ono lay a day's hard ride to the northwest. He understood what the letter meant. A man who travelled to such a meeting might not travel back. He had not laboured fifty days to walk away from the work for the sake of a quiet conference in a distant village. He gave his answer to the messenger.

"I am doing a great work, so that I cannot come down. Why should the work cease, whilst I leave it, and come down to you?"

The messenger went. Nehemiah watched the dust of the road close behind him and turned back to the gate.

A second letter came. It said the same thing. He answered it the same way. A third letter came. He answered again. The fourth time, when the messenger spread the parchment open, the words inside were almost word for word what had come before, and the answer Nehemiah gave was almost word for word what he had given. He did not raise his voice. He did not vary the form. "I am doing a great work, so that I cannot come down."

Then Sanballat sent a fifth time, by his own servant, with an open letter in the man's hand. The letter was open so that any who passed in the street might read it as the servant carried it through the city. Nehemiah received it in the open ground near the gate. Workers paused on the wall above to watch. The servant unrolled the letter in front of him and held it up.

"It is reported among the heathen, and Gashmu saith it, that thou and the Jews think to rebel: for which cause thou buildest the wall, that thou mayest be their king, according to these words. And thou hast also appointed prophets to preach of thee at Jerusalem, saying, There is a king in Judah: and now shall it be reported to the king according to these words. Come now therefore, and let us take counsel together."

Nehemiah read the letter standing. He did not lower his head. He did not draw the servant aside. If the letter was open, his answer would be open also.

"There are no such things done as thou sayest, but thou feignest them out of thine own heart."

The servant rolled the letter and went. The workers above shifted on the wall and went back to their tools. Nehemiah stood a moment longer in the open ground. He knew what they wanted. They did not want a meeting. They wanted hands to fall from the stone. They had said it among themselves, and the saying of it had reached his ear: their hands shall be weakened from the work, that it be not done. He stood in the open ground and spoke under his breath, as a man speaks who has only one place to bring such a prayer.

"Now therefore, O God, strengthen my hands."

That afternoon he went to the house of Shemaiah the son of Delaiah the son of Mehetabeel, who was shut up within his own walls. The door was barred. A servant opened it after Nehemiah knocked. Shemaiah was in the inner room. He did not rise when Nehemiah came in. He spoke quickly, with the manner of a man delivering an urgent and holy word.

"Let us meet together in the house of God, within the temple, and let us shut the doors of the temple: for they will come to slay thee; yea, in the night will they come to slay thee."

Nehemiah stood in the small room. He looked at the man who would not come out into the light to speak. The lamp on the table cast Shemaiah's face in unsteady shadow. He weighed the words against the day's work, and against the kind of man he had been called to be. A governor of Judah hiding in the temple. The doors of the house of God shut against the night. A story to be carried out into every street by morning.

He gave his answer.

"Should such a man as I flee? And who is there, that, being as I am, would go into the temple to save his life? I will not go in."

He turned and went out. The door closed behind him. The street outside was bright after the room. He walked back through the city with the sun in his eyes.

As he walked he understood. God had not sent Shemaiah. The man had pronounced his prophecy against him, not for him. Tobiah and Sanballat had hired him. The errand was the same errand that had come in the letters, only changed in shape. They had tried to draw him away to the plain of Ono. They had tried to draw him into the temple in the dark. They wanted him to be afraid, and to do something out of fear, and to sin in the doing, that they might have matter for an evil report and carry his name down to the king.

He brought the matter once more before God, in the same quiet voice he had used in the open ground.

"My God, think thou upon Tobiah and Sanballat according to these their works, and on the prophetess Noadiah, and the rest of the prophets, that would have put me in fear."

The next morning the hammers began again. The doors of the gates were lifted into their frames and the hinges set. The watchmen took their stations at the towers. The masons finished the last courses where they were still to be finished. The carriers brought timber from the king's forest. The wall held its line.

So the wall was finished in the twenty and fifth day of the month of Elul, in fifty and two days.

When the enemies of the Jews heard of it, and all the heathen round about saw the wall standing whole around Jerusalem, they were much cast down in their own eyes. The laughter that had carried across the province went silent. The letters stopped. The plain of Ono went on without its meeting.

For they perceived that this work was wrought of our God.

Nehemiah stood at the gate in the morning of the fifty-second day and looked along the length of the wall. The watchmen were at the towers. The doors hung in their places. The streets were waking inside the line of stone. The sun climbed over Jerusalem, and the wall stood, and the work that had been begun in fasting and prayer was standing now in stone, and the city of David was guarded again.
