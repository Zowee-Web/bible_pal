The council chamber held its breath. Stephen stood at the center of the half-circle of stone benches, his hands loose at his sides, the dust of the street still on the hem of his robe. The elders watched him from the curved tiers above. Lamps burned in iron brackets along the walls. The high priest sat at the head, robed in white linen, his fingers laid flat along the arm of his seat.

The witnesses against Stephen had finished their accusations. The room was silent. Somewhere outside, a cart rolled past in the street, and the sound came in faintly and went out again.

The high priest spoke. "Are these things so?"

Stephen lifted his face. He did not look hurried. He did not look afraid. He drew a breath. The room waited.

"Brothers and fathers, listen. The God of glory appeared to our father Abraham, when he was in Mesopotamia, before he lived in Haran. He said to him, 'Get out of your land, and from your relatives, and come into a land which I will show you.'"

The eldest on the bench gave a slow nod. The story they all knew. The story of the beginning. A scribe near the wall, who had bent forward to record what was said, lowered his stylus and listened.

Stephen carried Abraham forward. The promise of a land for a people not yet born. The covenant of circumcision. Isaac. Jacob. The twelve patriarchs. He let the names fall in order, the way a man counts the rooms of his father's house. The councilmen nodded again. Familiar ground. The high priest's shoulders eased.

Then he turned to Joseph.

"The patriarchs, moved with jealousy against Joseph, sold him into Egypt. God was with him."

The word jealousy moved through the room. A man in the second row shifted on his bench. Stephen carried it onward. The famine that came over the land. The brothers brought low before the brother they had cast out. The robe of fine linen on the one they had stripped of his coat. Jacob and his household going down into Egypt. The bones brought back to lie in the tomb at Shechem.

The shift in the room was small. A loosening of one face. A tightening of another.

He moved to Moses. He spoke of the new king who did not know Joseph, of the infants taken from their mothers and cast into the river. He spoke of the child set adrift in a basket of reeds, drawn from the water by Pharaoh's daughter, raised in the house of the very king who had wanted him dead. He spoke of the day Moses, forty years old, went out to his brothers and saw one of them being struck. He spoke of how Moses struck back, and killed the Egyptian, and supposed that his brothers would understand that God was giving deliverance by his hand. He spoke of how they did not understand.

"Who made you a ruler and a judge over us?"

He let the question sit in the air a moment. The eldest blinked slowly. A councilman near the back drew his robe more tightly around his shoulders.

Then Midian. The forty silent years. The flock on the back side of the wilderness. The bush that burned and was not consumed. The voice that called from the flame.

"'Take off your sandals, for the place where you stand is holy ground.'"

Stephen's voice softened around the words. The scribe had forgotten his tablet altogether.

"This Moses whom they refused," Stephen said, "saying, 'Who made you a ruler and a judge?' — God has sent him as both a ruler and a deliverer by the hand of the angel who appeared to him in the bush."

The eldest leaned forward. The cart in the street outside had passed. The lamps burned on without flicker.

Stephen spoke of the wonders in Egypt, and the Red Sea, and the forty years in the wilderness. He spoke of the assembly on Mount Sinai, of the living revelations given to be handed down. Then his voice changed.

"To whom our fathers would not be obedient, but rejected him and turned back in their hearts to Egypt, saying to Aaron, 'Make us gods that will go before us, for as for this Moses who led us out of the land of Egypt, we do not know what has become of him.'"

A councilman in the front row laid his palm flat on his knee.

Stephen spoke of the calf they made in those days, and the sacrifice they offered to the work of their own hands, and how they rejoiced in it. He spoke of God turning away. He spoke of the prophets who had named it for what it was, and the words of God spoken through their mouths about Moloch and Rephan and the carrying away beyond Babylon.

The high priest's fingers closed on the arm of his seat.

Stephen spoke of the tabernacle in the wilderness, made according to the pattern Moses had been shown. He spoke of Joshua bringing it into the land. He spoke of David finding favor and asking to build a house for the God of Jacob. He spoke of Solomon, who built it.

Then his voice settled lower, and the room settled with it.

"However, the Most High does not dwell in temples made with hands."

A murmur ran along the back tier. It died quickly. The elders waited for what was coming, because they had begun to feel where the story was bending. The high priest's mouth was a line. The eldest no longer nodded.

Stephen lifted his eyes from the floor and let them travel along the curved benches. He looked at each face. The young men at the doors. The scribes against the wall. The high priest on his seat.

"Brothers and fathers" was gone. What came next came at them.

"You stiff-necked and uncircumcised in heart and ears, you always resist the Holy Spirit! As your fathers did, so do you! Which of the prophets did your fathers not persecute? They killed those who foretold the coming of the Righteous One, of whom you have now become betrayers and murderers. You received the law as it was ordained by angels, and did not keep it."

The room broke.

Men were on their feet. Robes swung. Voices rose from every tier at once. The eldest pressed his palms against his ears. The high priest was rising. A councilman in the front row shouted something no one heard. Teeth showed. Hands shook. The murmur became a roar. They were cut to the heart. They gnashed at him with their teeth.

And in the middle of it, Stephen, full of the Holy Spirit, looked up.

He looked up steadfastly into heaven. His face changed. It grew bright. It became as the face of an angel. The councilman nearest him stepped back without knowing he had stepped back. The lamps could not account for the light in his face.

Stephen spoke once more, and his voice rode over the noise of the room.

"Behold, I see the heavens opened, and the Son of Man standing at the right hand of God."

They covered their ears. They cried out with a loud voice. They rushed upon him with one accord. Hands took hold of his robe and his arms and the back of his neck. The benches emptied. They drove him toward the doors. They cast him out of the city.

Outside the wall, the stones lay ready. The witnesses laid down their cloaks at the feet of a young man named Saul, who stood watching, and they took up the stones.

Stephen knelt. The first blow drove him forward, and he caught himself on his hands. The second came. The third. Blood ran into the dust beneath him.

He called out, and his voice was clear above them.

"Lord Jesus, receive my spirit."

Another stone struck. He knelt lower. He cried with a loud voice, "Lord, do not hold this sin against them."

And when he had said this, he fell asleep. The stones lay where they had fallen. The young man named Saul gathered the cloaks against his arm. The morning held still.
