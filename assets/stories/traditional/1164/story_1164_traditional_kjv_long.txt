The council chamber held its breath. Stephen stood at the centre of the half-circle of stone benches, his hands loose at his sides, the dust of the street still on the hem of his robe. The elders watched him from the curved tiers above. Lamps burned in iron brackets along the walls. The high priest sat at the head, robed in white linen, his fingers laid flat along the arm of his seat.

The witnesses against Stephen had finished their accusations. The room was silent. Somewhere without, a cart rolled past in the street, and the sound came in faintly and went out again.

The high priest spake. "Are these things so?"

Stephen lifted his face. He did not look hurried. He did not look afraid. He drew a breath. The room waited.

"Men, brethren, and fathers, hearken. The God of glory appeared unto our father Abraham, when he was in Mesopotamia, before he dwelt in Charran. He said unto him, 'Get thee out of thy country, and from thy kindred, and come into the land which I shall shew thee.'"

The eldest on the bench gave a slow nod. The story they all knew. The story of the beginning. A scribe near the wall, who had bent forward to record what was said, lowered his stylus and listened.

Stephen carried Abraham forward. The promise of a land for a people not yet born. The covenant of circumcision. Isaac. Jacob. The twelve patriarchs. He let the names fall in order, the way a man counteth the rooms of his father's house. The councilmen nodded again. Familiar ground. The high priest's shoulders eased.

Then he turned unto Joseph.

"The patriarchs, moved with envy, sold Joseph into Egypt. But God was with him."

The word envy moved through the room. A man in the second row shifted on his bench. Stephen carried it onward. The famine that came over the land. The brethren brought low before the brother they had cast out. The robe of fine linen upon the one they had stripped of his coat. Jacob and his household going down into Egypt. The bones brought back to lie in the tomb at Sychem.

The shift in the room was small. A loosening of one face. A tightening of another.

He moved unto Moses. He spake of the new king which knew not Joseph, of the infants taken from their mothers and cast into the river. He spake of the child set adrift in a basket of reeds, drawn from the water by Pharaoh's daughter, raised in the house of the very king who had wanted him dead. He spake of the day Moses, forty years old, went out unto his brethren and saw one of them being smitten. He spake of how Moses smote back, and slew the Egyptian, and supposed that his brethren would have understood that God by his hand would deliver them. He spake of how they understood not.

"Who made thee a ruler and a judge over us?"

He let the question sit in the air a moment. The eldest blinked slowly. A councilman near the back drew his robe more tightly about his shoulders.

Then Midian. The forty silent years. The flock on the backside of the wilderness. The bush that burned and was not consumed. The voice that called from the flame.

"'Put off thy shoes from thy feet, for the place where thou standest is holy ground.'"

Stephen's voice softened around the words. The scribe had forgotten his tablet altogether.

"This Moses whom they refused," Stephen said, "saying, 'Who made thee a ruler and a judge?' — the same did God send to be a ruler and a deliverer by the hand of the angel which appeared to him in the bush."

The eldest leaned forward. The cart in the street without had passed. The lamps burned on without flicker.

Stephen spake of the wonders in Egypt, and the Red sea, and the forty years in the wilderness. He spake of the assembly on mount Sinai, of the lively oracles given to be handed down. Then his voice changed.

"To whom our fathers would not obey, but thrust him from them, and in their hearts turned back again into Egypt, saying unto Aaron, 'Make us gods to go before us, for as for this Moses, which brought us out of the land of Egypt, we wot not what is become of him.'"

A councilman in the front row laid his palm flat upon his knee.

Stephen spake of the calf they made in those days, and the sacrifice they offered unto the work of their own hands, and how they rejoiced in it. He spake of God turning away. He spake of the prophets who had named it for what it was, and the words of God spoken through their mouths concerning Moloch and Remphan and the carrying away beyond Babylon.

The high priest's fingers closed on the arm of his seat.

Stephen spake of the tabernacle of witness in the wilderness, made according to the fashion that Moses had seen. He spake of Joshua bringing it into the land. He spake of David finding favour and desiring to build an house for the God of Jacob. He spake of Solomon, who built it.

Then his voice settled lower, and the room settled with it.

"Howbeit the most High dwelleth not in temples made with hands."

A murmur ran along the back tier. It died quickly. The elders waited for what was coming, because they had begun to feel where the story was bending. The high priest's mouth was a line. The eldest no longer nodded.

Stephen lifted his eyes from the floor and let them travel along the curved benches. He looked at each face. The young men at the doors. The scribes against the wall. The high priest on his seat.

"Men, brethren, and fathers" was gone. What came next came at them.

"Ye stiffnecked and uncircumcised in heart and ears, ye do always resist the Holy Ghost! As your fathers did, so do ye! Which of the prophets have not your fathers persecuted? They have slain them which shewed before of the coming of the Just One, of whom ye have been now the betrayers and murderers. Ye who have received the law by the disposition of angels, and have not kept it."

The room broke.

Men were on their feet. Robes swung. Voices rose from every tier at once. The eldest pressed his palms against his ears. The high priest was rising. A councilman in the front row shouted something no one heard. Teeth shewed. Hands shook. The murmur became a roar. They were cut to the heart. They gnashed on him with their teeth.

And in the midst of it, Stephen, full of the Holy Ghost, looked up.

He looked up stedfastly into heaven. His face changed. It grew bright. It became as the face of an angel. The councilman nearest him stepped back without knowing he had stepped back. The lamps could not account for the light in his face.

Stephen spake once more, and his voice rode over the noise of the room.

"Behold, I see the heavens opened, and the Son of man standing on the right hand of God."

They stopped their ears. They cried out with a loud voice. They ran upon him with one accord. Hands took hold of his robe and his arms and the back of his neck. The benches emptied. They drave him toward the doors. They cast him out of the city.

Without the wall, the stones lay ready. The witnesses laid down their cloaks at the feet of a young man named Saul, who stood watching, and they took up the stones.

Stephen kneeled down. The first blow drave him forward, and he caught himself on his hands. The second came. The third. Blood ran into the dust beneath him.

He called out, and his voice was clear above them.

"Lord Jesus, receive my spirit."

Another stone struck. He kneeled lower. He cried with a loud voice, "Lord, lay not this sin to their charge."

And when he had said this, he fell asleep. The stones lay where they had fallen. The young man named Saul gathered the cloaks against his arm. The morning held still.
