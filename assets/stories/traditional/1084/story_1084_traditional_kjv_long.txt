The afternoon sun hung low over the country where Jesus taught. A great company had gathered about him in an open place beneath a few scattered trees. Men and women from the towns roundabout sat upon the dry ground or leaned against the trunks. The light came through the branches in shifting patterns. The air was warm and still. Some had brought bread and dried fruit wrapped in cloth. Others sat with empty hands, their faces turned toward the man who spake.

Among the company was a certain lawyer — a man learned in the writings of Moses, who had spent many years in the study of the scriptures and the traditions of the elders. He was skilled at asking questions which might expose a teacher's weakness or force him into a contradiction. He rose up from his place with measured bearing.

"Master," said he, "what shall I do to inherit eternal life?"

The crowd turned to look upon him. Jesus answered not the question directly, but turned it back.

"What is written in the law? How readest thou?"

The lawyer answered without hesitation, reciting from memory the words he had learned in his youth and studied all his life. "Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy strength, and with all thy mind; and thy neighbour as thyself."

Jesus said unto him, "Thou hast answered right: this do, and thou shalt live."

The crowd settled. But the lawyer was not content. He was willing to justify himself — to draw a line about his obligation and know where precisely his duty ended. Who was his neighbour, and just as greatly, who was not.

"And who is my neighbour?" he asked.

Jesus looked upon him. The crowd leaned forward. Then Jesus began to speak.

"A certain man went down from Jerusalem to Jericho."

All knew the road. It was a journey of seventeen miles which descended steeply from the high ground about Jerusalem into the valley of the Jordan — a descent of more than three thousand feet. The road wound through barren, rocky hills and deep ravines where shadows lay thick even at midday. Loose stones shifted underfoot. In places the path narrowed between high walls of sun-bleached stone, scarce wide enough for a man and his beast to pass. Thieves were known to wait among the rocks for those who walked alone.

"And he fell among thieves," Jesus continued, "which stripped him of his raiment, and wounded him, and departed, leaving him half dead."

The man lay in the dust at the side of the way. His body was broken. His raiment was taken. His blood soaked into the dry earth beneath him. The sun moved overhead, and none came. The silence was broken only by the wind moving among the rocks and the distant cry of a bird wheeling in the hot sky.

"And by chance there came down a certain priest that way."

The crowd hearkened carefully. A priest — a man who served in the temple at Jerusalem, who handled the sacred things, who knew the commandments as well as any man alive.

"When he saw him, he passed by on the other side."

He saw him. He looked upon him. And then he crossed unto the far side of the road and went his way. His sandals scraped upon the stone. His robe swayed about his ankles. He looked not back.

"And likewise a Levite, when he was at the place, came and looked on him."

Another man of holy duty — one who sang in the temple, who assisted in the sacrifices, who knew the law of Moses by heart.

"And he also passed by on the other side."

He had slowed as he drew near. He had looked down upon the body — the blood, the bruises, the stillness. Then he too crossed the road and went on, his footsteps fading about the next turning of the path.

"But a certain Samaritan, as he journeyed, came where he was."

The word fell upon the crowd. A Samaritan — a man of a people whom the Jews despised, whose worship was deemed unclean, whose company was shunned. And yet this was the one who stopped.

"And when he saw him, he had compassion on him."

The Samaritan came down from his beast. He knelt in the dust beside the wounded man. He opened his provision bag and took out oil and wine. With great care he poured oil upon the open wounds, then wine, washing away the dirt and dried blood. He tore strips of cloth and wound them about the man's arms, his chest, and his head, binding each wound tightly. His hands moved with tenderness, as though the man were one of his own household.

Then he lifted the man — slowly, with great care — and set him upon his own beast. He steadied him with one hand and began to walk beside the animal, leading it by the reins down the long road toward the nearest inn. The way was hot. The stones were rough beneath his feet. The man swayed upon the beast's back, barely sensible, his head fallen forward against the animal's neck.

At the inn, the Samaritan brought him within. He laid him upon a bed. He abode beside him through the evening, looking to the bandages, offering water, watching his breathing as the lamps burned low. The sounds of the road died away. The night grew quiet without the walls.

In the morning, the Samaritan rose early. He took out two pence and gave them unto the host. "Take care of him," said he, "and whatsoever thou spendest more, when I come again, I will repay thee."

Then he gathered his things and went forth into the pale morning light, continuing upon his journey alone.

Jesus ceased speaking. The crowd was still. The lawyer stood where he had risen, his arms at his sides.

"Which now of these three, thinkest thou, was neighbour unto him that fell among the thieves?"

The lawyer looked upon the ground. When he spake, his voice was low.

"He that shewed mercy on him."

Jesus held his gaze.

"Go," he said, "and do thou likewise."

The crowd sat in the stillness that followed. The breeze stirred the branches above them, and the leaves turned, casting small patterns of light and shade upon the ground. No man spake. The lawyer remained standing, the words settling upon him as dust from the road.
