The afternoon sun hung low over the countryside where Jesus was teaching. A crowd had gathered around him in an open space beneath a few scattered trees — men and women from the towns nearby, sitting on the dry ground or leaning against the trunks. The light came through the branches in shifting patterns. The air was warm and still. Some had brought bread and dried fruit wrapped in cloth. Others sat with empty hands, their eyes fixed on the man speaking.

Among the crowd was a certain lawyer — a man trained in the writings of Moses, who had spent years studying the scriptures and the traditions of the elders. He was skilled at asking the kind of question that could expose a teacher's weakness or force him into a contradiction. He rose from his seat with a careful, measured posture.

"Teacher," he said, "what must I do to inherit eternal life?"

The crowd turned to look at him. Jesus did not answer the question directly. Instead, he turned it back.

"What is written in the law?" he said. "How do you read it?"

The lawyer answered without hesitation, reciting from memory the words he had learned as a child and studied all his life. "You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, with all your strength, and with all your mind — and your neighbor as yourself."

Jesus nodded. "You have answered correctly," he said. "Do this, and you will live."

The crowd settled. But the lawyer was not finished. He wanted to justify himself — to narrow the definition, to draw a clear line around his obligation and know exactly where it ended. To know who his neighbor was, and just as importantly, who was not.

"And who," he asked, "is my neighbor?"

Jesus looked at him. The crowd leaned forward slightly. Then Jesus began to tell a story.

"A certain man was going down from Jerusalem to Jericho."

Everyone knew the road. It was a seventeen-mile stretch that dropped steeply from the high ground around Jerusalem down into the Jordan Valley — a descent of more than three thousand feet. The road wound through barren, rocky hills and deep ravines where shadows lay thick even at midday. Loose stones shifted underfoot. In places, the path narrowed between high walls of sun-bleached limestone, barely wide enough for a man and a donkey to pass side by side. Robbers were known to hide among the rocks, waiting for travelers walking alone.

"On his way," Jesus continued, "the man fell among robbers. They stripped him of his clothes. They beat him. And they went away, leaving him half dead."

The man lay in the dust at the side of the road. His body was broken. His clothes were gone. Blood soaked into the dry ground beneath him. The sun moved overhead, and no one came. The silence was broken only by the wind moving through the rocks and the distant cry of a bird circling in the hot sky.

"By chance," Jesus said, "a certain priest was going down that same road."

The crowd listened carefully. A priest — a man who served in the temple in Jerusalem, who handled the sacred things, who knew the commandments as well as anyone alive.

"When he saw the wounded man," Jesus said, "he passed by on the other side."

He saw him. He looked. And then he moved to the far side of the road and kept walking. His sandals scraped against the stone. His robe swayed at his ankles. He did not look back.

"In the same way," Jesus said, "a Levite came to the place."

Another man of religious duty. A man who sang in the temple, who assisted in the sacrifices, who knew the law of Moses by heart.

"When he saw the man," Jesus continued, "he also passed by on the other side."

He had slowed as he approached. He had looked down at the body — the blood, the bruises, the stillness. Then he, too, crossed the road and walked on, his footsteps fading around the next bend in the path.

"But a Samaritan," Jesus said, "as he traveled, came where the man was."

The word settled over the crowd. A Samaritan. A man from a people the Jews looked down upon — whose worship was considered wrong, whose company was avoided, who would not have been welcomed in the temple or greeted in the marketplace. And yet this was the one who stopped.

"When he saw him," Jesus said, "he was moved with compassion."

The Samaritan climbed down from his animal. He knelt in the dust beside the wounded man. He opened the saddlebag he carried and took out oil and wine — common provisions for a journey, now used for a different purpose. Carefully, he poured oil over the open wounds, then wine, cleaning away the dirt and dried blood. He tore strips of cloth and wound them around the man's arms, his chest, and his head, binding each wound tightly. His hands moved with care, as though the man were someone he had known all his life.

Then he lifted the man — slowly, gently — and set him on his own animal. He steadied him with one arm and began to walk beside the animal, leading it by the reins down the long road toward the nearest inn. The road was hot. The stones were rough under his sandals. The man swayed on the animal's back, barely conscious, his head falling forward against the animal's neck.

At the inn, the Samaritan brought him inside. He laid him on a bed. He stayed beside him through the evening, checking the bandages, offering water, watching his breathing. The lamps burned low. The sounds of the road faded. The night grew quiet outside the walls.

In the morning, the Samaritan rose early. He took out two denarii — two days' wages for a laborer — and gave them to the innkeeper. "Take care of him," he said. "Whatever you spend beyond this, I will repay you when I return."

Then he gathered his things and walked out into the pale morning light, continuing his journey down the road alone.

Jesus stopped speaking. The crowd was still. The lawyer stood where he had risen, his arms at his sides, his face unreadable.

"Now which of these three," Jesus said, "do you think was a neighbor to the man who fell among the robbers?"

The lawyer looked at the ground. When he spoke, his voice was low and quiet.

"The one who showed mercy on him."

Jesus held his gaze.

"Go," he said, "and do likewise."

The crowd sat in the stillness that followed. The breeze moved through the branches overhead, and the leaves shifted, casting small patterns of light and shadow across the people gathered there. No one spoke. The lawyer remained standing, his eyes still on the ground, the words settling around him like dust from the road.
