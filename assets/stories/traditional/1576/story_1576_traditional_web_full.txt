Yahweh sent Moses to Pharaoh, with Aaron his brother to speak for him, and told him beforehand that Pharaoh would not listen — that Yahweh would harden Pharaoh's heart and multiply his signs, until by great judgments he brought Israel out of Egypt. So they went in; and when Pharaoh demanded a wonder, Aaron's rod became a serpent and swallowed up the serpents of the magicians. Yet Pharaoh's heart was hardened, and he would not listen.

Then the plagues began. Aaron struck the river, and it turned to blood; the fish died, and Egypt could not drink. The magicians did the same, and Pharaoh's heart was hardened. Frogs swarmed out of the river into every house, and Pharaoh promised to let the people go if Moses would pray them away; but when the frogs died and there was a respite, he hardened his own heart. The dust became gnats, and the magicians who could not do the like said, "This is the finger of God" — but Pharaoh's heart was hardened still. Swarms of flies filled Egypt while Goshen was spared; Pharaoh bargained, and asked for prayer, then hardened his heart this time also, and would not let them go.

The livestock of Egypt died in the field while Israel's did not, and Pharaoh's heart was stubborn. Moses scattered ashes toward the sky, and boils broke out on man and beast, until the magicians could not stand before him. And now it was Yahweh who hardened the heart of Pharaoh. Hail fell such as Egypt had never seen, with thunder and with fire that ran along the ground, striking all that was in the field, though Goshen was spared; Pharaoh confessed, "I have sinned; Yahweh is righteous," yet when it ceased he hardened his heart once more. Locusts covered the land and ate all that the hail had left; Pharaoh confessed again and begged for prayer, but Yahweh hardened his heart. Then a darkness that could be felt lay three days over Egypt, while Israel had light in their dwellings; Pharaoh would let the people go but keep back their flocks, and Moses would not leave a hoof behind; and Yahweh hardened Pharaoh's heart, and he drove Moses out — "See my face no more."

One plague remained. At midnight, Moses warned, every firstborn in Egypt would die, from Pharaoh's own son on the throne to the son of the servant behind the mill, and all the firstborn of the cattle; but against Israel not a dog would so much as move its tongue. Then he went out from Pharaoh in hot anger.

And Yahweh told his people how they would be kept. Each household was to take a lamb without blemish, kill it at evening on the fourteenth day, and strike its blood on the two doorposts and the lintel. They were to eat it that night, roasted, with unleavened bread and bitter herbs, dressed for the road and in haste, for it was Yahweh's Passover. When he passed through to strike Egypt, the blood would be a sign upon their houses; where he saw it, he would pass over, and no plague would fall there. This night they were to keep forever, and to tell their children it was the sacrifice of Yahweh's Passover, who spared the houses of Israel when he struck Egypt. And the people bowed their heads and worshiped.

At midnight Yahweh struck all the firstborn of Egypt, from the firstborn of Pharaoh on his throne to the firstborn of the captive in the dungeon, and all the firstborn of the cattle. Pharaoh rose in the night, and all his servants, and all Egypt, and there was a great cry, for there was not a house without one dead. He called for Moses and Aaron. "Rise up, get out from among my people," he said, "both you and the children of Israel; go, serve Yahweh, as you have said. Take your flocks and your herds, and be gone — and bless me also." The Egyptians pressed them to leave in haste, saying, "We are all dead men." So the children of Israel went out from Rameses, about six hundred thousand men on foot besides their families, carrying the dough that had no time to rise — the night to be kept to Yahweh, who brought them out of the land of Egypt.
