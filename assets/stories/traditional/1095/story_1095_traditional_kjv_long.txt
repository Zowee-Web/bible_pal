Saul of Tarsus was a man consumed by certainty. He had been trained in the law of Moses from his youth, educated under the great teacher Gamaliel in Jerusalem, and formed into a Pharisee of the straitest order. He knew the scriptures. He kept the commandments. He believed with absolute conviction that the followers of Jesus of Nazareth were spreading a dangerous heresy — a lie that threatened the very foundations of the faith to which he had given his life.

He had stood by at the stoning of Stephen — had held the garments of them that cast the stones, watching as Stephen fell upon his knees beneath the blows and cried out, "Lord, lay not this sin to their charge." Stephen died. And Saul flinched not. If anything, the death hardened his purpose. These people must needs be stopped.

He went unto the high priest in Jerusalem. He asked for letters of authority — official writings addressed to the synagogues in Damascus, giving him power to find and arrest any that belonged to the Way, whether men or women, and to bring them bound unto Jerusalem. The letters were granted without question. Saul was known. He was trusted. He was zealous.

He set out upon the road to Damascus. The journey was long — some hundred and fifty miles northward and eastward through dry, hilly country. He travelled with companions, men who would help him carry out the arrests. The road wound through rocky terrain, past olive groves and small villages, across wide stretches of open ground where the sun pressed down and the dust rose with every step. Saul walked at the head of the company, his face set, his pace steady, his mind given over to the names, the houses, the synagogues where the followers of Jesus were said to gather.

Then, as they drew near unto Damascus, all things ceased.

A light from heaven — sudden, blinding, past all bearing — shone round about him. It was brighter than the sun at midday. It was not lightning. It was not fire. It was light — pure, vast, unbearable — and it engulfed him utterly. He fell to the earth. His face struck the dust. His hands went up about his head. His eyes were shut fast against the brilliance that pressed through his eyelids.

A voice spake unto him out of the light.

"Saul, Saul, why persecutest thou me?"

The voice was clear. Direct. It knew his name. It spoke it twice, as a father speaketh unto a child that hath gone terribly wrong.

He lay upon the ground, trembling. "Who art thou, Lord?" he said.

"I am Jesus whom thou persecutest."

The words fell upon him as stones. Jesus. The man whose followers he hunted. The man whose movement he had purposed to destroy. The man who, by all that Saul had believed, was dead and buried and gone.

"Arise," the voice said, "and go into the city, and it shall be told thee what thou must do."

The light faded. Saul lay upon the road, breathing hard. The dust was warm beneath his face. His companions stood about him, confounded. They had heard the sound — the voice, or something like a voice — but they had seen no man. They looked upon Saul, then upon one another, then upon the empty road.

Saul opened his eyes.

He could see nothing.

The light that had struck him down had taken his sight. All was dark — not the darkness of night, but a thick and utter blindness, as though the world had been blotted out. He stretched forth his hands and felt only air.

His companions took him by the arms and led him the rest of the way into Damascus. The man who had set forth to arrest the followers of Jesus was now led through the city gate as a helpless child, shuffling forward with his hands before him, his face blank with shock.

They brought him unto a house upon the street called Straight. He went within and sat down. He ate not. He drank not. For three days he sat in the darkness, and he prayed.

In another part of Damascus, a certain disciple named Ananias received a vision. The Lord spake unto him. "Arise, and go into the street which is called Straight, and enquire in the house of Judas for one called Saul, of Tarsus: for, behold, he prayeth, and hath seen in a vision a man named Ananias coming in, and putting his hand on him, that he might receive his sight."

Ananias was afraid. He had cause to be. "Lord," said he, "I have heard by many of this man, how much evil he hath done to thy saints at Jerusalem: and here he hath authority from the chief priests to bind all that call on thy name."

But the Lord said unto him, "Go thy way: for he is a chosen vessel unto me, to bear my name before the Gentiles, and kings, and the children of Israel: for I will shew him how great things he must suffer for my name's sake."

So Ananias went. He walked through the streets of Damascus unto the house upon the street called Straight. He entered in. He found Saul — sitting in the dark, his eyes open but seeing nothing, his hands folded in his lap, his face yet marked with the dust of the road.

Ananias put his hands upon him. The touch was gentle. His voice was steady.

"Brother Saul," said he. "The Lord, even Jesus, that appeared unto thee in the way as thou camest, hath sent me, that thou mightest receive thy sight, and be filled with the Holy Ghost."

And immediately there fell from his eyes as it had been scales. Light flooded in — not the blinding, overwhelming light of the road, but the common, beautiful light of a room in Damascus. The walls. The floor. The face of Ananias, looking down upon him with a countenance that was not fear, not judgment, but welcome.

Saul blinked. He could see.

He arose, and was baptized. And when he had received meat, he was strengthened. The man who had come unto Damascus to destroy the church went forth from that house as one of its own.
