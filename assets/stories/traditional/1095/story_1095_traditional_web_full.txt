Saul of Tarsus was breathing threats and murder against the disciples of the Lord. He had watched Stephen die — had stood by, holding the cloaks of those who stoned him — and it had not satisfied him. If anything, it had sharpened his conviction. These followers of Jesus were dangerous. They were spreading a lie, and he intended to stop them.

He went to the high priest in Jerusalem and asked for letters of authority — official documents addressed to the synagogues in Damascus, giving him the right to arrest anyone he found who belonged to the Way, men or women, and bring them back to Jerusalem in chains.

The letters were granted. Saul set out on the road to Damascus with a group of companions. The road ran north and east through dry, hilly country. The journey was long — several days of walking under the hot sun, through rocky terrain, past small villages and stretches of empty road where the only sound was the wind and the crunch of sandals on gravel. Saul walked with purpose, his face set, his mind fixed on the work ahead.

Then, as he came near Damascus, everything changed.

A light from heaven flashed around him — sudden, blinding, brighter than the noonday sun. It engulfed him completely. He fell to the ground, face-first in the dust, his arms over his head, his eyes squeezed shut against the unbearable brightness.

A voice spoke to him out of the light.

"Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me?"

He lay on the ground, trembling. The voice was clear, direct, personal. "Who are you, Lord?" he said.

"I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting. Rise, and go into the city, and you will be told what you must do."

The men traveling with Saul stood frozen on the road. They had heard the voice — or the sound of it — but they saw no one. When the light faded and Saul opened his eyes, he could see nothing. The brilliance had taken his sight. Everything was dark.

His companions took him by the hand and led him the rest of the way into Damascus. The man who had set out to arrest followers of Jesus was now led through the streets like a child, blind and helpless.

For three days, Saul sat in darkness. He was staying in a house on the street called Straight. He did not eat. He did not drink. He prayed.

In the same city, a disciple named Ananias received a vision from the Lord. "Go to the street called Straight," the Lord said, "and ask at the house of Judas for a man from Tarsus named Saul. He is praying, and he has seen in a vision a man named Ananias coming and laying hands on him so that he might regain his sight."

Ananias was afraid. "Lord," he said, "I have heard many reports about this man — how much harm he has done to your saints in Jerusalem. And he has authority from the chief priests to arrest everyone who calls on your name."

The Lord said to him, "Go, for he is a chosen instrument of mine to carry my name before the Gentiles and kings and the children of Israel. I will show him how much he must suffer for my name's sake."

So Ananias went. He found the house on the street called Straight. He went inside and found Saul — sitting in the dark, his eyes open but seeing nothing, his hands folded in his lap.

Ananias laid his hands on him. "Brother Saul," he said, "the Lord Jesus, who appeared to you on the road as you came here, has sent me so that you may regain your sight and be filled with the Holy Spirit."

Immediately something like scales fell from Saul's eyes. Light poured in. He blinked. He could see — the room, the walls, the face of Ananias looking down at him.

He rose and was baptized. Then he ate food, and his strength returned.
