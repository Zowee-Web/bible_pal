Saul of Tarsus was a man consumed by certainty. He had been trained in the law of Moses from childhood, educated under the great teacher Gamaliel in Jerusalem, and shaped into a Pharisee of the strictest order. He knew the scriptures. He kept the commandments. He believed, with absolute conviction, that the followers of Jesus of Nazareth were spreading a dangerous lie — a heresy that threatened the very foundations of the faith he had given his life to defend.

He had been present at the stoning of Stephen — had stood on the edge of the crowd, holding the cloaks of those who threw the stones, watching as Stephen fell to his knees under the blows and cried out, "Lord, do not hold this sin against them." Stephen died. And Saul did not flinch. If anything, the death hardened his resolve. These people had to be stopped.

He went to the high priest in Jerusalem. He asked for letters of authority — official documents addressed to the synagogues in Damascus, granting him the right to find and arrest anyone who belonged to the Way, men or women, and bring them back to Jerusalem in chains. The letters were granted without hesitation. Saul was known. He was trusted. He was zealous.

He set out on the road to Damascus. The journey was long — roughly a hundred and fifty miles north and east through dry, hilly country. He traveled with a group of companions, armed men who would help him carry out the arrests. The road wound through rocky terrain, past olive groves and small villages, across wide stretches of open ground where the sun pressed down and the dust rose with every step. Saul walked at the head of the group, his face set, his pace steady, his mind running through the names, the addresses, the synagogues where the followers of Jesus were said to gather.

Then, as they drew near to Damascus, everything stopped.

A light from heaven — sudden, blinding, overwhelming — flashed around him. It was brighter than the sun at midday. It was not lightning. It was not fire. It was light — pure, vast, unbearable — and it engulfed him completely. He fell to the ground. His face struck the dust. His hands went up over his head. His eyes clenched shut against the brilliance that pressed through his eyelids.

A voice spoke to him out of the light.

"Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me?"

The voice was clear. Direct. Personal. It knew his name. It spoke it twice, the way a father speaks to a child who has gone terribly wrong.

He lay on the ground, trembling. "Who are you, Lord?" he said.

"I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting."

The words fell on him like stones. Jesus. The man whose followers he was hunting. The man whose movement he had vowed to destroy. The man who, by every account Saul had believed, was dead and buried and gone.

"Rise," the voice said, "and go into the city, and you will be told what you must do."

The light faded. Saul lay on the road, breathing hard. The dust was warm under his face. His companions stood around him, frozen. They had heard the sound — the voice, or something like a voice — but they had seen no one. They looked at Saul, then at each other, then at the empty road.

Saul opened his eyes.

He could see nothing.

The light that had struck him down had taken his sight. Everything was dark — not the darkness of night, but a thick, absolute blindness, as though the world had been erased. He reached out his hands and felt only air.

His companions took him by the arms and led him the rest of the way into Damascus. The man who had set out to arrest the followers of Jesus was now led through the city gates like a helpless child, shuffling forward with his hands stretched out before him, his face stunned and blank.

They brought him to a house on the street called Straight. He went inside and sat down. He did not eat. He did not drink. For three days he sat in the dark, and he prayed.

In another part of Damascus, a disciple named Ananias received a vision. The Lord spoke to him directly. "Go to the street called Straight," the Lord said, "and ask at the house of Judas for a man from Tarsus named Saul. He is praying. In a vision he has seen a man named Ananias coming and placing his hands on him so that he might regain his sight."

Ananias was afraid. He had every reason to be. "Lord," he said, "I have heard from many people about this man — how much evil he has done to your saints in Jerusalem. And here he has authority from the chief priests to arrest all who call upon your name."

The Lord said to him, "Go. For he is a chosen instrument of mine to carry my name before the Gentiles and kings and the children of Israel. I will show him how much he must suffer for the sake of my name."

So Ananias went. He walked through the streets of Damascus to the house on the street called Straight. He went inside. He found Saul sitting in the dark — his eyes open but unseeing, his hands folded in his lap, his face still marked with dust from the road.

Ananias laid his hands on him. The touch was gentle. His voice was steady.

"Brother Saul," he said. "The Lord Jesus, who appeared to you on the road as you were coming here, has sent me so that you may regain your sight and be filled with the Holy Spirit."

Immediately, something like scales fell from Saul's eyes. Light flooded in — not the blinding, overwhelming light of the road, but the ordinary, beautiful light of a room in Damascus. The walls. The floor. The face of Ananias, looking down at him with an expression that was not fear, not judgment, but welcome.

Saul blinked. He could see.

He rose. He was baptized. Then he took food, and his strength returned. The man who had come to Damascus to destroy the church walked out of that house as one of its own.
