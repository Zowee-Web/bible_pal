In the third year of the reign of Jehoiakim king of Judah, Nebuchadnezzar came up against Jerusalem and besieged it. He took part of the vessels of the house of God with him to Babylon, and he took young men of the house of the king of Judah, and of the princes — children in whom there was no blemish, well-favored, skillful in all wisdom, cunning in knowledge, understanding science, such as had ability to stand in the king's palace.

Daniel was one of them. Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah were with him.

They were lodged together in a house of the king. The prince of the eunuchs, whose name was Ashpenaz, had charge over them. They were given new names that came from the gods of Babylon — Belteshazzar, Shadrach, Meshach, Abednego — names meant to take the names of their fathers' God off their tongues.

They were given the king's portion to eat.

The portion was set before them at the appointed hour each day. Wine from the king's table, in a cup. Meat from the king's table, on a plate. The meat had been offered to the king's gods before it came out of the king's kitchens. The wine had been poured in the king's banqueting hall before what was left was sent down to the young men who were being trained.

Three years of this was the plan. At the end of the three years, the trained ones would stand before the king himself.

Daniel was a young man. He had been in Jerusalem in the year before. He had seen the temple of Yahweh and had eaten at the table of his own father's house. He had known what was clean and what was not clean. He had been taught the law of Moses from his childhood.

Daniel purposed in his heart that he would not defile himself with the king's food, nor with the wine which the king drank.

The decision did not show in his face when he made it. He kept it inside, where the men of Babylon could not see it. Then he went out, when the hour was right, to the prince of the eunuchs.

He asked the prince that he might not defile himself.

The prince was afraid.

He said, "I fear my lord the king, who has appointed your food and your drink. Why should he see your faces worse than the other youths who are of your own age? You would endanger my head with the king."

Daniel did not press him there. He took the answer the prince had given. He waited.

After some days he went to the steward whom the prince had set over him and over Hananiah and Mishael and Azariah. He spoke to him in a place where the prince could not hear, and where the other youths could not hear. He spoke quietly.

He said, "Test your servants, I beg you, ten days. Let them give us vegetables to eat, and water to drink. Then let our faces be examined before you, and the faces of the youths who eat the king's portion. As you see, deal with your servants."

The steward listened to him in this matter.

He gave them vegetables and water for ten days. He had the vegetables brought to them in the morning. He had the water drawn from the well of the house. The four ate together at the appointed hour, while the other youths ate the king's meat at the long table down the hall.

The ten days passed.

At the end of the ten days, the steward looked at the four faces. He looked at the faces of the other youths. He saw that Daniel's face, and Hananiah's, and Mishael's, and Azariah's were fairer. He saw that they were fatter in flesh than all the youths who ate of the king's food.

He took away the king's portion from before them. He gave them vegetables, from that day on, and the wine he set aside for himself.

The three years passed.

In those years, God gave the four young men knowledge and skill in all learning and wisdom. They were taught the language of the Chaldeans, and the writings, and the science of the stars. They learned what the Babylonian teachers could teach them. And to Daniel, beyond what the teachers gave, God gave understanding in all visions and dreams.

The others did not yet know that he had this understanding. It was a thing that would come out later, in the rooms of Nebuchadnezzar.

When the days were finished that the king had appointed, the prince of the eunuchs brought all the trained young men in before Nebuchadnezzar.

The king sat on his high seat in the great hall of his palace. The young men stood in their ranks before him. The king spoke with each of them in turn. He asked them what he wished to ask.

He came to Daniel.

He came to Hananiah, and to Mishael, and to Azariah.

Among them all was found no one like these four.

Therefore they stood before the king from that day on. They served in the king's court. They were brought in when the king inquired of them on matters of wisdom and understanding.

In every such matter, the king found them ten times better than all the magicians and enchanters who were in all his realm.

Daniel continued before the king from that year.

He continued through the years of Nebuchadnezzar, through the years of the king's madness in the field and the king's return to his throne, through the years of Belshazzar after Nebuchadnezzar, through the night that Belshazzar was slain, and into the first year of King Cyrus the Persian, when the captivity was ending and the people of God were given leave to return to Jerusalem.

The choice he had made at the table when he was a young man held him through all those years.
