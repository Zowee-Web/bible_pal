After these things, the cupbearer of the king of Egypt and his baker offended their lord. Pharaoh was angry with his two officers. He put them in custody in the house of the captain of the guard, into the prison, the place where Joseph was bound.

The captain of the guard assigned them to Joseph. He took care of them. They stayed in prison many days.

Joseph had learned this house by then. The corridor that ran past the cells. The smell of the lamp wick at evening. The sound the bolt made when it slid back in the morning. He knew which stones held heat through the night and which gave it up at once. He knew the hours by the slant of the light at the high window. He brought the bread. He brought the water. He carried the basin away. He came when he was called. He had been carrying his own steady waiting for so long that it no longer showed in his face.

Then one morning the two new men were sad. Not the sadness of confinement. A nearer kind. He saw it on them before they spoke.

"Why do you look so sad today?" he said.

They said, "We have dreamed a dream, and there is no one who can interpret it."

Joseph said, "Don't interpretations belong to God? Please tell it to me."

The cupbearer spoke first. "In my dream, behold, a vine was in front of me. In the vine were three branches. It was as though it budded, it blossomed, and its clusters produced ripe grapes. Pharaoh's cup was in my hand, and I took the grapes and pressed them into Pharaoh's cup, and I gave the cup into Pharaoh's hand."

Joseph said, "This is its interpretation. The three branches are three days. Within three more days, Pharaoh will lift up your head and restore you to your office. You will give Pharaoh's cup into his hand, the way you did when you were his cupbearer."

He stopped. The cell was quiet. He spoke again, lower.

"But remember me when it is well with you. Please show kindness to me, and make mention of me to Pharaoh, and bring me out of this house. For indeed, I was stolen away out of the land of the Hebrews. Here also I have done nothing that they should put me into the dungeon."

The cupbearer nodded. He said he would.

When the baker saw that the interpretation was good, he spoke too. "I also was in my dream. Behold, three baskets of white bread were on my head. In the uppermost basket there were all kinds of baked food for Pharaoh, and the birds ate them out of the basket on my head."

Joseph looked at him a long moment.

"This is its interpretation. The three baskets are three days. Within three more days, Pharaoh will lift up your head from off you, and will hang you on a tree, and the birds will eat your flesh from off you."

The baker said nothing. The cupbearer said nothing. Joseph went out and saw to the bread.

The two men counted. Joseph counted with them. One day. Two days.

On the third day, which was Pharaoh's birthday, he made a feast for all his servants. He lifted up the head of the chief cupbearer and the head of the chief baker among his servants. He restored the chief cupbearer to his position again, and he gave the cup into Pharaoh's hand. But he hanged the chief baker, as Joseph had interpreted to them.

Word came back into the prison by midday. Joseph heard it where he stood. He set down what he was carrying. He stood a moment longer than he meant to.

Then he went on with the work. He brought the bread. He brought the water. He carried the basin away. He came when he was called. The cell that had held three was again the cell that held him.

Yet the chief cupbearer did not remember Joseph, but forgot him.
