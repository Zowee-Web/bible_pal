After these things, the cupbearer of the king of Egypt and his baker offended their lord. Pharaoh was angry with his two officers, the chief cupbearer and the chief baker. He put them in custody in the house of the captain of the guard, into the prison, the place where Joseph was bound.

The captain of the guard assigned them to Joseph. He took care of them. They stayed in prison many days.

Joseph had learned this house by then. The corridor that ran past the cells. The smell of the lamp wick at evening, and the smell of the morning when the lamp had burned down and the wick had begun to thread the air with thin smoke. He knew the bolt. The bolt on the outer door was heavy, and it slid back with a sound that traveled the length of the corridor before any footstep followed. He knew which stones in the wall held heat through the night, and which gave it up at once. He knew the hours by the slant of the light at the high window. The bar of light moved a hand's breadth between morning and noon and another hand's breadth before evening, and then it was gone, and then the lamp.

He brought the bread. He brought the water. He carried the basin away. He came when he was called. He had been carrying his own steady waiting for so long that it no longer showed in his face. He did not speak of the pit or of the colored coat or of the road to Egypt. He did not speak of the house of Potiphar. He served the men who were given to him. The keeper had committed them to his hand. He served them with both hands.

Then one morning the two new men were sad. Not the sadness of confinement, which Joseph knew, which had settled in the corners of every cell in the place. A nearer kind. He saw it on them before they spoke. The cupbearer sat with his back against the wall and his hands turned upward on his knees. The baker sat across from him and did not lift his eyes from the floor.

"Why do you look so sad today?" Joseph said.

They said to him, "We have dreamed a dream, and there is no one who can interpret it."

Joseph said to them, "Don't interpretations belong to God? Please tell it to me."

The chief cupbearer spoke first. He kept his voice low, as men do who have just woken from something. "In my dream, behold, a vine was in front of me. In the vine were three branches. It was as though it budded, it blossomed, and its clusters produced ripe grapes. Pharaoh's cup was in my hand, and I took the grapes, and pressed them into Pharaoh's cup, and I gave the cup into Pharaoh's hand."

Joseph said to him, "This is its interpretation. The three branches are three days. Within three more days, Pharaoh will lift up your head, and restore you to your office. You will give Pharaoh's cup into his hand, the way you did when you were his cupbearer."

He stopped. The cell was quiet. He spoke again, lower, and the cupbearer leaned forward a little to hear.

"But remember me when it is well with you. Please show kindness to me, and make mention of me to Pharaoh, and bring me out of this house. For indeed, I was stolen away out of the land of the Hebrews. Here also I have done nothing that they should put me into the dungeon."

The cupbearer nodded. He said he would. He took Joseph's wrist a moment and let it go.

When the chief baker saw that the interpretation was good, he spoke too, eager now, the words coming faster. "I also was in my dream. Behold, three baskets of white bread were on my head. In the uppermost basket there were all kinds of baked food for Pharaoh, and the birds ate them out of the basket on my head."

Joseph looked at him a long moment.

"This is its interpretation. The three baskets are three days. Within three more days, Pharaoh will lift up your head from off you, and will hang you on a tree, and the birds will eat your flesh from off you."

The baker said nothing. The cupbearer said nothing. Joseph went out and saw to the bread.

The two men counted. Joseph counted with them. He could not help it.

The first day passed. He brought the morning meal and the evening meal. The light at the window moved the hand's breadth and the hand's breadth and was gone. The lamp was lit. The lamp burned down. He lay on his pallet in his own cell with his hands folded across his chest, and he listened to the cupbearer and the baker breathe in the cell across the corridor. Neither of them slept much. He heard them turn. He heard the cupbearer cough once. He heard a long silence and then nothing.

The second day passed. He brought the bread. He brought the water. He carried the basin away. He did not look long at either man's face. The cupbearer stood near the window all afternoon and watched the bar of light move. The baker lay with his face turned to the wall.

On the third day, which was Pharaoh's birthday, he made a feast for all his servants. He lifted up the head of the chief cupbearer and the head of the chief baker among his servants. He restored the chief cupbearer to his position again, and he gave the cup into Pharaoh's hand. But he hanged the chief baker, as Joseph had interpreted to them.

Word came back into the prison by midday. The guard at the outer door said it to the keeper, and the keeper said it down the corridor, and Joseph heard it where he stood with the morning basin in his hand. He set the basin down on the stone. He stood a moment longer than he meant to. He laid his hand flat on the wall, and the stone was warm where the sun had been falling on the outer side of it.

Then he went on with the work. He took up the basin. He carried it out. He brought the bread to the next cell, and the next. He came when he was called. He took the empty cup from the keeper's table and washed it and set it back. He banked the lamp for the night when the light at the window had gone. He lay down on his pallet and folded his hands across his chest.

The cell that had held three was again the cell that held him.

Outside the prison, somewhere across the city, a cup was being lifted at a long table. A hand that knew the weight of it was setting it again into the hand of Pharaoh. The cupbearer wore his old robe. The day's work had returned to him.

Yet the chief cupbearer did not remember Joseph, but forgot him.
