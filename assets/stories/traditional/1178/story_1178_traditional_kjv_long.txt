It came to pass after these things, that the butler of the king of Egypt and his baker had offended their lord the king of Egypt. Pharaoh was wroth against two of his officers, against the chief of the butlers, and against the chief of the bakers. He put them in ward in the house of the captain of the guard, into the prison, the place where Joseph was bound.

The captain of the guard charged Joseph with them, and he served them. They continued a season in ward.

Joseph had learned this house by then. The corridor that ran past the cells. The smell of the lamp wick at evening, and the smell of the morning when the lamp had burned down and the wick had begun to thread the air with thin smoke. He knew the bolt. The bolt on the outer door was heavy, and it slid back with a sound that traveled the length of the corridor before any footstep followed. He knew which stones in the wall held heat through the night, and which gave it up at once. He knew the hours by the slant of the light at the high window. The bar of light moved a hand's breadth between morning and noon and another hand's breadth before evening, and then it was gone, and then the lamp.

He brought the bread. He brought the water. He carried the basin away. He came when he was called. He had been carrying his own steady waiting for so long that it no longer shewed in his face. He spake not of the pit, nor of the coat of many colours, nor of the road to Egypt. He spake not of the house of Potiphar. He served the men who were given unto him. The keeper had committed them to his hand. He served them with both hands.

Then one morning the two new men were sad. Not the sadness of confinement, which Joseph knew, which had settled in the corners of every cell in the place. A nearer kind. He beheld it on them before they spake. The butler sat with his back against the wall and his hands turned upward upon his knees. The baker sat across from him and lifted not his eyes from the floor.

"Wherefore look ye so sadly to day?" Joseph said.

They said unto him, "We have dreamed a dream, and there is no interpreter of it."

Joseph said unto them, "Do not interpretations belong to God? tell me them, I pray you."

The chief butler spake first. He kept his voice low, as men do who have just woken from something. "In my dream, behold, a vine was before me. And in the vine were three branches: and it was as though it budded, and her blossoms shot forth; and the clusters thereof brought forth ripe grapes. And Pharaoh's cup was in my hand: and I took the grapes, and pressed them into Pharaoh's cup, and I gave the cup into Pharaoh's hand."

Joseph said unto him, "This is the interpretation of it. The three branches are three days. Yet within three days shall Pharaoh lift up thine head, and restore thee unto thy place: and thou shalt deliver Pharaoh's cup into his hand, after the former manner when thou wast his butler."

He stopped. The cell was quiet. He spake again, lower, and the butler leaned forward a little to hear.

"But think on me when it shall be well with thee, and shew kindness, I pray thee, unto me, and make mention of me unto Pharaoh, and bring me out of this house. For indeed, I was stolen away out of the land of the Hebrews: and here also have I done nothing that they should put me into the dungeon."

The butler nodded. He said he would. He took Joseph's wrist a moment and let it go.

When the chief baker saw that the interpretation was good, he spake also, eager now, the words coming faster. "I also was in my dream, and, behold, I had three white baskets on my head: And in the uppermost basket there was of all manner of bakemeats for Pharaoh; and the birds did eat them out of the basket upon my head."

Joseph looked upon him a long moment.

"This is the interpretation thereof. The three baskets are three days. Yet within three days shall Pharaoh lift up thy head from off thee, and shall hang thee on a tree; and the birds shall eat thy flesh from off thee."

The baker said nothing. The butler said nothing. Joseph went out and saw to the bread.

The two men counted. Joseph counted with them. He could not help it.

The first day passed. He brought the morning meal and the evening meal. The light at the window moved the hand's breadth and the hand's breadth and was gone. The lamp was lit. The lamp burned down. He lay upon his pallet in his own cell with his hands folded across his breast, and he hearkened to the butler and the baker breathe in the cell across the corridor. Neither of them slept much. He heard them turn. He heard the butler cough once. He heard a long silence and then nothing.

The second day passed. He brought the bread. He brought the water. He carried the basin away. He looked not long upon either man's face. The butler stood near the window all afternoon and watched the bar of light move. The baker lay with his face turned to the wall.

It came to pass the third day, which was Pharaoh's birthday, that he made a feast unto all his servants. He lifted up the head of the chief butler and of the chief baker among his servants. He restored the chief butler unto his butlership again; and he gave the cup into Pharaoh's hand. But he hanged the chief baker, as Joseph had interpreted unto them.

Word came back into the prison by midday. The guard at the outer door said it unto the keeper, and the keeper said it down the corridor, and Joseph heard it where he stood with the morning basin in his hand. He set the basin down upon the stone. He stood a moment longer than he meant to. He laid his hand flat upon the wall, and the stone was warm where the sun had been falling on the outer side of it.

Then he went on with the work. He took up the basin. He carried it out. He brought the bread to the next cell, and the next. He came when he was called. He took the empty cup from the keeper's table and washed it and set it back. He banked the lamp for the night when the light at the window was gone. He lay down upon his pallet and folded his hands across his breast.

The cell that had held three was again the cell that held him.

Without the prison, somewhere across the city, a cup was being lifted at a long table. A hand that knew the weight of it was setting it again into the hand of Pharaoh. The butler wore his old robe. The day's work had returned unto him.

Yet did not the chief butler remember Joseph, but forgat him.
