"Now faith is assurance of things hoped for, proof of things not seen."

It is the word the writer chose to begin with. He set it down at the head of his letter the way a man sets down a stone at the head of a road — the way is going to be long, and this is the first marker. Faith. Assurance. Proof. He did not begin with an argument. He began with a definition. Then he began to name the names of those who had walked by it before.

For by this, he wrote, the elders obtained testimony. By this they were approved.

By faith, he said, we understand that the universe has been framed by the word of God — so that what is seen has not been made out of things which are visible. There had been a word, and there had been a making. The substance of the world was older than the eye that looked at it. To begin with this was to set the whole list of names that followed inside a frame larger than any of them. Then he turned to the men and women themselves.

He began with Abel.

By faith Abel offered to God a more excellent sacrifice than Cain. The writer did not tell the story again. He had no need to. Everyone who heard the letter knew the field, and the altar, and the brother who came after with the stone. What the writer said about Abel was this: through the sacrifice he had testimony given to him that he was righteous. And through it, being dead, he still spoke. The blood on the ground had not finished its sentence. The voice of it was still going up out of the earth. Faith, when it had spoken once, did not stop speaking — not even after the throat had been closed.

Then Enoch.

By faith Enoch was taken away, so that he would not see death. He was not found, the writer said, because God translated him. Before his translation, he had testimony given to him that he had been well pleasing to God. The walking with God in the cool of the day. The years of it. The slow turning of seasons over the head of a man who kept walking — and then, one day, the place where he had been was empty, and the path he had been walking was empty, and his name passed out of the rolls of the living without an answer to give.

The writer paused, between Enoch and Noah, to say a thing he wanted his hearers to remember. Without faith, he wrote, it is impossible to be well pleasing to him. For he who comes to God must believe that he exists — and that he is a rewarder of those who seek him. He set the sentence down inside the list like a man setting a lamp on a shelf at the middle of a long room, so that what came before and what came after would be lit by the same light. Then he went on.

By faith Noah, being warned about things not yet seen, moved with godly fear. He prepared an ark for the saving of his house. The sky over Noah's head had given no warning that anyone else could read. The ground beneath his feet was dry. There were neighbors who watched. There were neighbors who laughed. The years passed. The wood was cut. The pitch was laid. The rooms were framed. Noah did not stop. Through the building, the writer said, he condemned the world. Through the building, he became heir of the righteousness which is according to faith.

Then Abraham.

By faith Abraham, when he was called, obeyed to go out to the place which he was to receive for an inheritance. He went out, the writer set down plainly, not knowing where he went. There were no maps in Abraham's hand. There were no scouts ahead. There was only the call, and the road, and the going. He left the country where his father's house stood. He left the country where his father's grave waited. He went out.

By faith he lived as an alien in the land of promise, as in a land not his own. He dwelt in tents with Isaac and Jacob, the heirs with him of the same promise. The tents could be folded. The tent stakes could be drawn from the ground. Nothing he stood inside was a wall. He looked, the writer said, for the city which has the foundations — whose builder and maker is God. He had been called to a country, and he had been given tents. The country was farther on.

By faith even Sarah herself received power to conceive. She bore a child when she was past age, since she counted him faithful who had promised. The laughter that had risen in her at the tent door had not been the last word. The womb that had been closed had been opened. Therefore as many as the stars of the sky in multitude were fathered by one man, and him as good as dead. The writer let the line stand without explanation. The dead had become a father. The barren had become a mother. The promise had broken open the bodies that had given it up.

Then the writer stopped naming, and he stepped back from his list, and he set down a sentence that was meant to be remembered.

These all died in faith, not having received the promises. They had seen them. They had embraced them from afar. They had confessed that they were strangers and pilgrims on the earth. They desired a better country, that is, a heavenly one. Therefore God is not ashamed of them, to be called their God. For he has prepared a city for them.

The city was prepared. The names had been gathered. The list was not finished.

By faith Abraham, being tested, offered up Isaac. He who had gladly received the promises was offering up his only begotten son. He concluded — the writer used the word — that God is able to raise up even from the dead. Figuratively speaking, the writer said, he did receive him back. The knife had been lifted, and the knife had been lowered, and the son had walked down the mountain at his father's side, and the father had received his son out of the place where faith had given him over.

By faith Isaac blessed Jacob and Esau, even concerning things to come.

By faith Jacob, when he was dying, blessed each of the sons of Joseph, and worshiped, leaning on the top of his staff. The old man, the writer wrote, did not put his hands on the heads of his grandsons in his own strength. The staff held him up. The blessing went out.

By faith Joseph, when his end was near, made mention of the departure of the children of Israel. And he gave instructions concerning his bones.

The bones would not stay where they were. The body of Joseph would lie in Egypt, and the people of Joseph would lie in Egypt, but neither one of them was going to stay. Faith had reached past the grave again — the way it had reached past Abel's altar, the way it had reached past Enoch's empty path, the way it had reached past Sarah's closed womb. The promise was farther on. The writer set the last name down, and the faith of them went on speaking.
