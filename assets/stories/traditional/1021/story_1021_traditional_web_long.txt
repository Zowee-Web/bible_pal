Jacob rose in the night.

He could not sleep. Esau was coming. Esau, his brother — the one he had cheated of a birthright with a bowl of stew, the one whose blessing he had stolen by dressing in goatskins and lying to his blind father's face. Esau, who had sworn to kill him. Esau, who now approached with four hundred men.

Jacob had already divided his camp into two companies and sent gifts ahead in waves — goats, sheep, camels, cattle, donkeys. He had arranged and calculated and prayed.

Now, in the dark, with nothing left to arrange, he rose.

He took his two wives, Leah and Rachel. He took his two female servants, Zilpah and Bilhah. He took his eleven children. He led them to the ford of the Jabbok, where the river ran shallow over a bed of smooth stones. The water was cold. It caught the faint starlight and broke it into moving fragments. The children clung to their mothers' hands. The youngest were carried, held tight against shoulders. The servants stepped carefully, testing each stone before putting weight on it. The cold water ran over their ankles, their calves. On the far bank, they climbed out and kept moving.

Jacob sent everything he had across the stream. The flocks. The goods. The bundles and provisions. All of it moved over the Jabbok in the darkness, animal by animal, load by load, until the near bank was empty.

Then Jacob was left alone.

The camp was gone. The last footstep had faded. The river murmured below the bank — a low, continuous sound, indifferent to whatever stood on its shore. The stars hung thick overhead, cold and distant. The darkness pressed in from every side. The hills were black shapes against a blacker sky. Somewhere beyond them, Esau was coming.

Jacob stood in the silence. A man with no more plans. No more gifts to send. No more arrangements to make. Whatever was going to happen, he would face it here, beside the water, alone.

A man wrestled with him.

There was no announcement. No voice called from the dark. A figure came upon him suddenly, and hands seized him, and they were locked together before Jacob had time to understand what was happening. The man's grip was powerful. Jacob braced his feet in the dirt. He pushed back. They strained against each other, each trying to find leverage, each trying to throw the other to the ground.

Jacob was strong. He had spent twenty years working Laban's flocks in the hill country — lifting stones from the mouths of wells that would take two ordinary men to move, wrestling rams during shearing season, hauling water, building stone pens with his bare hands. His palms were calloused thick as leather. His shoulders and back were hard with muscle. He knew how to plant his feet and hold.

They wrestled through the night.

The hours ground past. Neither man prevailed. They shifted and strained and grappled in the dirt beside the river. The sound of the Jabbok ran beneath them — a steady murmur that neither paused nor changed. Stones scraped under their feet. Dust rose and settled. Sweat soaked through Jacob's garments and ran into his eyes. His breath came in short, hard bursts. The muscles in his arms burned. His legs shook with the effort of holding his ground.

The man's strength did not diminish. Whatever Jacob gave, the man matched. Whatever hold Jacob tried, the man countered. They were locked in a contest that had no resolution, only endurance.

There were no words between them. Only the scrape of feet on gravel, the grunt of effort, the rasp of hard breathing. The darkness was total. Jacob could feel the man's shoulders under his hands but could not see the face.

The stars wheeled overhead. The night stretched on.

When the man saw that he did not prevail against Jacob, he reached out and touched the hollow of Jacob's thigh.

The touch was precise. It was not a punch or a kick. It was a single point of contact — a finger, perhaps, or the flat of a palm — pressed against the joint where the thighbone met the hip. And the socket came apart. The bone wrenched free of its housing with a grinding, sickening shift.

The pain was immediate and absolute. It ripped through Jacob's side like fire. His leg buckled. His weight collapsed to one side. The world tilted. Every nerve in his body screamed at him to let go, to fall, to curl around the agony in his hip and stop moving.

Jacob held on.

He dug his fingers into the man's shoulders. He locked his arms. His ruined hip sent waves of pain through his torso with every breath, every shift of weight. He could no longer plant his feet. He could no longer push. He hung on the man like a weight, clinging with the last of his strength, refusing to release his grip.

The man said, "Let me go, for the day breaks."

Jacob looked toward the east. The sky along the horizon had begun to change. A thin gray light crept upward from behind the hills. The shapes of trees and rocks emerged slowly from the darkness. The stars nearest the horizon were fading. Dawn was coming, and the man wanted to leave before it arrived.

Jacob said, "I will not let you go unless you bless me."

His voice was ragged. Broken. His body shook with exhaustion and pain. His hip was dislocated. His muscles had gone past burning into a dull, trembling weakness. But the words came out clear.

He had spent his life grasping — Esau's heel at birth, the birthright with a bowl of stew, the blessing by covering his arms in goatskin. All his life, reaching for things, seizing them, holding them with both hands.

Now, in the dirt beside the Jabbok, with his hip destroyed and his body failing, he grasped again. But this time he reached for the right thing. He reached for God himself, and he would not let go.

The man said to him, "What is your name?"

"Jacob."

The name hung in the predawn air. Jacob. Heel-grabber. Supplanter. He had deceived his father, cheated his brother, been cheated in turn by Laban. The name was a history, and the history was not kind.

The man said, "Your name will no longer be called Jacob, but Israel. For you have struggled with God and with men, and have prevailed."

Israel. He who struggles with God. The new name was given in the dark, beside a river, to a man with a shattered hip who refused to open his hands. It was not earned by cleverness or deception. It was earned by endurance — by the willingness to hold on through a whole night of pain, through the breaking of his own body, through the long hours when there was nothing left but the grip of his hands and the stubbornness of his will.

Jacob spoke once more. "Please tell me your name."

The man answered, "Why is it that you ask about my name?"

He did not give it. The question was its own answer. Jacob already knew who this was. His body knew. The shattered hip knew. The blessing he had just received knew.

The man blessed him there. Beside the Jabbok. In the gray light before sunrise. The blessing came to a man lying in the dust with a ruined body. It came without ceremony. It simply came.

Jacob called the name of that place Peniel. "For I have seen God face to face," he said, "and my life is preserved."

He said it with wonder. He had seen God. He had wrestled God. He had clung to God with a broken body through the longest night of his life. And he was still alive.

The sun rose on him as he crossed over Peniel. He limped on his thigh. Each step sent a bolt of pain through his side. The river lay behind him. The land stretched ahead — open, bright, warming under the new sun. Somewhere out there, Esau was coming. The future was waiting. And the man who had once been called Jacob walked into it under a new name.

He limped forward. Broken, blessed, renamed. The sun warmed his face, and he did not look back.
