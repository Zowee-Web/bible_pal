Jacob rose up in the night.

He could not sleep. Esau was coming. Esau, his brother — the one he had beguiled of a birthright with a mess of pottage, the one whose blessing he had stolen by putting on goatskins and lying before his blind father's face. Esau, who had sworn to slay him. Esau, who now drew nigh with four hundred men.

Jacob had already divided his camp into two bands and sent gifts before him in waves — goats, sheep, camels, cattle, asses. He had ordered and reckoned and prayed.

Now, in the dark, with nothing remaining to arrange, he rose.

He took his two wives, Leah and Rachel. He took his two womenservants, Zilpah and Bilhah. He took his eleven sons. He led them to the ford Jabbok, where the river ran shallow over a bed of smooth stones. The water was cold. It caught the faint starlight and broke it into moving fragments. The children clung to their mothers. The youngest were borne in arms. The servants stepped with care, proving each stone before setting weight upon it. Upon the far bank they climbed forth and went on.

Jacob sent over all that he had. The flocks. The goods. The bundles and provision. All of it passed over the Jabbok in the darkness, beast by beast, burden by burden, until the near bank stood empty.

And Jacob was left alone.

The camp was gone. The last footfall had died away. The river murmured below the bank — a low and ceaseless sound, heedless of whatsoever stood upon its shore. The stars hung thick above, cold and far. The darkness pressed in from every quarter. The hills were black shapes set against a blacker sky. Somewhere beyond them, Esau was coming.

Jacob stood in the silence. A man with no more devices. No more gifts to send forth. No more things to order. Whatsoever was to come, he would face it here, beside the water, alone.

There wrestled a man with him.

There was no word given beforehand. No voice called out of the dark. A figure came upon him of a sudden, and hands seized him, and they were locked together ere Jacob could understand what befell him. The man's grip was mighty. Jacob set his feet in the dust. He pressed back. They strained one against the other, each seeking purchase, each seeking to cast the other down.

Jacob was strong. Twenty years serving Laban's flocks — lifting well-stones, wrestling rams, drawing water, raising pens with his bare hands. His palms were calloused thick as leather. His shoulders were hard with toil. He knew how to hold fast.

They wrestled through the night.

The hours ground past. Neither prevailed. They shifted and strained and grappled in the dust beside the river. The sound of the Jabbok ran beneath them — a steady murmur that neither paused nor changed. Stones scraped under their feet. Dust rose and settled. Sweat soaked through Jacob's garments and ran into his eyes. His breath came in short, hard bursts. The sinews of his arms burned. His legs trembled with the strain of holding ground.

The man's strength waned not. Whatsoever Jacob gave, the man answered. Whatsoever hold Jacob tried, the man countered. They were locked in a contest that yielded no end, only endurance.

There were no words between them. Only the scrape of feet upon gravel, the groaning of effort, the rasp of hard breathing. The darkness was full. Jacob could feel the man's shoulders beneath his hands but could not see the face.

The stars turned overhead. The night drew not to its end.

When the man saw that he prevailed not against Jacob, he put forth his hand and touched the hollow of his thigh.

The touch was precise. It was no blow, no strike. It was a single point set upon the body — a finger, it may be, or the flat of a palm — pressed against the joint where the thighbone met the hip. And the socket came asunder. The bone wrenched free of its housing with a grinding, sickening shift.

The pain was sudden and utter. It tore through Jacob's side as fire. His leg gave way beneath him. His weight fell to one side. The world tilted. Every sinew in his body cried out to loose his hold, to fall, to draw close about the agony in his hip and be still.

Jacob held fast.

He dug his fingers into the man's shoulders. He locked his arms. His ruined hip sent waves of anguish through his body with every breath, every shift of weight. He could no longer set his feet. He could no longer press forward. He hung upon the man as a dead weight, clinging with the last of his strength, refusing to loose his grip.

The man said, "Let me go, for the day breaketh."

Jacob looked toward the east. The sky along the horizon had begun to change. A thin grey light crept upward from behind the hills. The shapes of trees and stones came slowly forth from the darkness. The stars nearest the horizon were growing faint. Dawn was nigh, and the man would depart ere it came.

Jacob said, "I will not let thee go, except thou bless me."

His voice was broken. Spent. His body shook with weariness and pain. His hip was out of joint. His muscles had passed beyond burning into a dull and trembling weakness. But the words came forth clearly.

He had spent the whole of his life grasping — Esau's heel at birth, the birthright with a mess of pottage, the blessing by wrapping his arms in goatskin. All his days, reaching for things, seizing them, holding them fast.

Now, in the dust beside the Jabbok, with his hip destroyed and his body failing, he grasped once more. But this time he laid hold of the right thing. He laid hold of God himself, and he would not let go.

The man said unto him, "What is thy name?"

"Jacob."

The name hung in the air before the dawn. Jacob. Heel-catcher. Supplanter. He had deceived his father, beguiled his brother, been beguiled in his turn by Laban. The name was a chronicle, and the chronicle was not gentle.

The man said, "Thy name shall be called no more Jacob, but Israel. For as a prince hast thou power with God and with men, and hast prevailed."

Israel. He that striveth with God. The new name was given in the dark, beside a river, unto a man whose hip was shattered and who refused to open his hands. It was not won by cleverness or craft. It was won by endurance — by the readiness to hold fast through a whole night of anguish, through the breaking of his own body, through the long hours when nothing remained save the grip of his hands and the stubbornness of his will.

Jacob spake once more. "Tell me, I pray thee, thy name."

The man answered, "Wherefore is it that thou dost ask after my name?"

He gave it not. The question was its own answer. Jacob knew already whom he had met. His body knew. The shattered hip knew. The blessing he had received knew.

The man blessed him there. Beside the Jabbok. In the grey light ere the sunrise. The blessing came unto a man lying in the dust with a broken body. It came without ceremony or show. It simply came.

Jacob called the name of that place Peniel. "For I have seen God face to face," said he, "and my life is preserved."

He spake it with wonder. He had seen God. He had wrestled with God. He had clung to God with a broken body through the longest night of all his days. And yet he lived.

The sun rose upon him as he passed over Peniel. He halted upon his thigh. Each step sent a bolt of pain through his side. The river lay behind him. The land stretched before him — open, bright, warming beneath the new sun. Somewhere yonder, Esau was coming. The days to come were waiting. And the man who once was called Jacob walked forth under a new name.

He went forward, halting. Broken, blessed, renamed. The sun warmed his face, and he looked not back.
