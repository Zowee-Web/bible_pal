Jacob rose in the night. Esau was coming — Esau, whom he had cheated of a birthright and a blessing, now approaching with four hundred men. Jacob had divided his camp and sent gifts ahead. He had done everything a man could do.

Now, in the dark, he rose.

He took his two wives, Leah and Rachel. He took his two female servants. He took his eleven children. He sent them across the ford of the Jabbok. The water ran shallow at the crossing, cold over smooth stones. The children clung to their mothers. Jacob sent everything he had across — flocks, goods, all of it.

Then Jacob was left alone.

The camp was empty. The river murmured below the bank. The stars hung thick overhead, and the darkness pressed close. He stood in the silence — a man stripped of every arrangement, every companion. Whatever was coming, he would meet it here.

A man wrestled with him.

There was no warning. No voice calling out of the dark. A figure came upon him suddenly, and hands seized him, and they were locked together before Jacob understood what was happening. Jacob braced his feet in the dirt. The man pushed against him. Jacob pushed back. They locked together, straining, each trying to throw the other down.

Jacob was strong. Twenty years working Laban's flocks — lifting well-stones, wrestling rams, hauling water. His hands were calloused. His shoulders were broad. He knew how to hold on.

They wrestled through the night. Neither prevailed. The sound of the Jabbok ran beneath them. Stones scraped under their feet. Sweat soaked through Jacob's garments. His breath came hard. The man's strength did not diminish.

There were no words between them. Only the scrape of feet on gravel, the grunt of effort, the hard breathing of two bodies locked against each other in the dark. The stars turned overhead.

When the man saw that he did not prevail, he reached out and touched the hollow of Jacob's thigh. The touch was precise — a single point of contact. The hip socket wrenched out of its joint.

The pain was immediate. It shot through Jacob's side like fire. His leg buckled. His weight shifted.

Jacob held on.

His fingers dug into the man's shoulders. His ruined hip screamed with every movement. He could no longer plant his feet. He could only cling.

The man said, "Let me go, for the day breaks."

The eastern sky had begun to change. A thin gray light crept along the horizon. Dawn was coming.

Jacob said, "I will not let you go unless you bless me."

His voice was ragged. His body shook. His hip was dislocated, his strength nearly spent. But the words were certain. He had spent his whole life grasping — Esau's heel at birth, the birthright with a bowl of stew, the blessing through deception. Now, in the dirt beside the Jabbok with a broken body, he grasped the right thing.

The man said, "What is your name?"

"Jacob."

The name carried its own weight. Jacob — heel-grabber, supplanter, the one who takes by trickery. He had been named for the way he came into the world, clutching his brother's heel. He had lived under that name his entire life.

The man said, "Your name will no longer be called Jacob, but Israel. For you have struggled with God and with men, and have prevailed."

A new name. Given in the dark, beside a river, to a man with a dislocated hip who refused to let go.

Jacob said, "Please tell me your name."

"Why is it that you ask about my name?"

He did not give it. But he blessed Jacob there, beside the Jabbok, as the first light spread across the water.

Jacob called that place Peniel. "For I have seen God face to face," he said, "and my life is preserved."

The sun rose on him as he crossed over Peniel. He limped on his thigh. Each step sent pain through his side. The river lay behind him. The land opened ahead — where Esau was coming, where the future waited.

He limped forward. Broken, blessed, renamed. The sun warmed his face, and he did not look back.
