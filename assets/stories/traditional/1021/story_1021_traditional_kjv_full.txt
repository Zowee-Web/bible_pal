Jacob rose up in the night. Esau was coming — Esau, whom he had beguiled of a birthright and a blessing, now drawing nigh with four hundred men. Jacob had divided his camp and sent gifts before him. He had done all that a man might do.

Now, in the dark, he rose.

He took his two wives, Leah and Rachel. He took his two womenservants. He took his eleven sons. He sent them over the ford Jabbok. The water ran shallow at the crossing, cold upon the smooth stones. The children clung to their mothers. Jacob sent over all that he had — flocks, goods, the whole of it.

And Jacob was left alone.

The camp was empty. The river murmured below the bank. The stars hung thick above, and the darkness pressed close. He stood in the stillness — a man stripped of every device, every companion. Whatsoever was coming, he would meet it here.

There wrestled a man with him.

There was no warning. A figure came upon him in the dark, and hands took hold of him, and they strove together. Jacob set his feet in the dust. The man pressed against him. Jacob pressed back. They locked together, straining, each seeking to cast the other down.

Jacob was strong. Twenty years serving Laban's flocks — lifting stones from wells, wrestling rams, drawing water. His hands were hardened. His shoulders were broad. He knew how to take hold.

They wrestled through the night. Neither prevailed. The sound of the Jabbok ran beneath them. Stones scraped under their feet. Sweat soaked through Jacob's garments. His breath came hard. The man's strength waned not.

There were no words between them. Only the scrape of feet upon gravel, the groaning of effort, the hard breathing of two bodies locked one against the other in the dark. The stars turned overhead.

When the man saw that he prevailed not, he put forth his hand and touched the hollow of his thigh. The touch was precise — a single point upon the body. The hip was wrenched from its socket.

The pain was sudden and complete. It shot through Jacob's side as fire. His leg gave way. His weight shifted.

Jacob held fast.

His fingers took hold of the man's shoulders. His ruined hip cried out with every motion. He could no longer set his feet aright. He could only cling.

The man said, "Let me go, for the day breaketh."

The eastern sky had begun to change. A thin grey light crept along the edge of the hills. Dawn was nigh.

Jacob said, "I will not let thee go, except thou bless me."

His voice was broken. His body shook. His hip was out of joint, his strength near spent. But the words were sure. He had spent the whole of his life grasping — Esau's heel at birth, the birthright with a mess of pottage, the blessing through guile. Now, in the dust beside the Jabbok with a broken body, he laid hold of the right thing.

The man said unto him, "What is thy name?"

"Jacob."

The name bore its burden. Heel-catcher. Supplanter. He had lived beneath it all his days.

The man said, "Thy name shall be called no more Jacob, but Israel. For as a prince hast thou power with God and with men, and hast prevailed."

A new name. Given in the dark, beside a river, unto a man whose hip was out of joint and who refused to loose his hold.

Jacob said, "Tell me, I pray thee, thy name."

"Wherefore is it that thou dost ask after my name?"

He gave it not. But he blessed Jacob there, beside the Jabbok, as the first light spread upon the water.

Jacob called that place Peniel. "For I have seen God face to face," said he, "and my life is preserved."

The sun rose upon him as he passed over Peniel. He halted upon his thigh. Each step sent pain through his side. The river lay behind him. The land opened before him — where Esau was coming, where the days to come awaited.

He went forward, halting. Broken, blessed, renamed. The sun warmed his face, and he looked not back.
