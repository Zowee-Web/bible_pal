In the year that King Uzziah died, Isaiah saw the Lord.

King Uzziah had ruled over Judah for a very long time. He had been king in Jerusalem for fifty-two years. That is longer than most people could remember. He had been strong. He had built towers and dug wells and led armies. But now Uzziah was gone. The throne in Jerusalem sat empty. The palace halls were quiet.

And in that year — that heavy, uncertain year — Isaiah saw something.

He saw the Lord sitting on a throne.

It was not the throne in the palace. This throne was in the temple. And it was high. Higher than anything Isaiah had ever seen. It was lifted up far above the floor, far above the steps, far above the pillars that held up the temple roof. The Lord sat upon it.

The hem of His robe filled the whole temple.

The fabric flowed down from the throne like water pouring from a great height. It spread across the stone floor in every direction. It reached the walls. It pooled against the base of the pillars. It covered the steps and the corners and the spaces between the stones. There was no part of the temple floor that the robe did not touch. The whole room was full of it.

Above the throne stood angels.

They were called seraphim. Isaiah had never seen anything like them. Each one had six wings. Not two wings, like a bird. Not four. Six. Two wings covered the face. Two wings covered the feet. And with the last two wings, each one flew. They hovered in the air above the throne, their wings moving slowly, holding them steady in the smoky space beneath the ceiling.

They were bright. They were tall. And they were not silent.

They called out to one another. One seraphim would cry, and another would answer. Their voices were enormous. The sound filled the temple the way the robe filled the floor. It pressed against the walls and pushed through every crack and opening.

"Holy, holy, holy is the Lord of hosts!"

That is what they said. Over and over. One voice calling, another answering.

"The whole earth is full of His glory!"

The doorposts shook.

The thick wooden frames that held the great temple doors began to rattle. They moved back and forth in their stone sockets. The noise was sharp — wood scraping against stone. The walls trembled. The floor trembled. The very foundations of the temple moved beneath Isaiah's sandals. He could feel the stone shifting under his feet, as if the ground itself was answering the voices of the seraphim.

Then smoke filled the room.

It rose from the altar at the center of the temple. It curled upward in thick white columns, twisting around the stone pillars. It drifted past the wings of the seraphim. It spread across the ceiling and rolled down along the walls. The air grew thick. The edges of things disappeared. The robe, the throne, the wings — they all shimmered and flickered behind the moving smoke.

The temple smelled of fire. Of burning wood and incense and something older and deeper that had no name.

Isaiah stood in the middle of it all.

His chest rose and fell. His hands hung at his sides. His eyes were wide. The smoke stung them, but he did not close them. He could not close them.

He opened his mouth and cried out.

"Woe is me!" he said. His voice cracked. It sounded small against the echo of the seraphim. "I am ruined!"

He did not step forward. He did not step back. He stood where he was, surrounded by smoke and sound.

"I am a man of unclean lips," he said. "And I live among a people of unclean lips."

His breath came in short bursts. The smoke moved around his shoulders and past his face.

"And my eyes," he said, "have seen the King — the Lord of hosts!"

The words left his mouth and disappeared into the haze. For a moment, nothing changed. The seraphim still hovered. The smoke still curled. The throne still rose.

Then one of the seraphim moved.

It came down from above the throne. Its wings folded and opened and folded again, carrying it through the smoky air in a swift, straight line. It flew toward Isaiah. Toward the place where he stood on the shaking stone floor.

In its hand, it held something.

A coal. A burning coal. It glowed orange at the center and red at the edges. It was so hot that the air around it rippled and bent. The seraphim had not picked it up with bare fingers. It had used tongs — long metal tongs — and it had lifted the coal straight from the altar fire. From the very place where the smoke rose. From the very center of the burning.

The seraphim came close.

Isaiah could feel the heat before the coal touched him. It came first as a warmth near his chin, then a sharp, pressing heat against his face. The angel reached out with the tongs.

The burning coal touched Isaiah's lips.

It pressed there, hot and bright, against the mouth that had just spoken. Against the lips that had called themselves unclean.

Then the seraphim spoke.

"See," the angel said. Its voice was close — not like the great cry of holy, holy, holy, but near and clear, like a parent speaking gently to a child. "This has touched your lips."

Isaiah did not move.

"Your guilt is taken away," the seraphim said. "Your sin is forgiven."

The angel lifted the coal from Isaiah's mouth. The tongs pulled back. The seraphim rose again, its wings opening wide, carrying it upward through the smoke and back toward the space above the throne. The heat lingered on Isaiah's lips. He could feel it there — a warmth that stayed, a mark that could not be seen but could be felt.

The smoke still drifted. The pillars still rose. The seraphim still hovered on their six wings, two covering the face, two covering the feet, two spread wide for flying. The robe still covered the floor. The throne still stood, high and lifted up.

The temple was quieter now. The shaking had stopped. The doorposts had settled in their sockets. The foundations were still. But the smoke continued to move — slowly, gently — weaving between the pillars like breath in a sleeping room.

Then a voice spoke.

It did not come from the seraphim. It came from the throne. It came from the place where the Lord sat, high above the robe and the smoke and the altar and the angels. It filled the temple. Not with shaking this time. Not with trembling. But with a question.

"Whom shall I send?"

The words rolled through the room. They passed over Isaiah and through the smoke and touched the walls.

"And who will go for us?"

The question hung in the air. The seraphim did not answer. The smoke did not answer. The stones and the pillars and the great wooden doorposts said nothing.

Isaiah stood on the temple floor. His sandals pressed against the cold stone. The warmth of the coal still rested on his lips. His hands were no longer limp at his sides. His chest was no longer heaving. His eyes were no longer stinging.

He lifted his face toward the throne.

"Here am I," Isaiah said. "Send me."
