In the year that King Uzziah died, Isaiah saw the Lord.

King Uzziah had ruled over Judah for a very long time. He had been king in Jerusalem for fifty and two years. That is longer than most men could remember. He had been strong. He had built towers and digged wells and led armies. But now Uzziah was no more. The throne in Jerusalem sat empty. The palace halls were quiet.

And in that year — that heavy, uncertain year — Isaiah beheld a thing.

He saw the Lord sitting upon a throne.

It was not the throne in the palace. This throne was in the temple. And it was high. Higher than any thing Isaiah had ever beheld. It was lifted up far above the floor, far above the steps, far above the pillars that held up the temple roof. The Lord sat upon it.

The hem of his robe filled the whole temple.

The fabric flowed down from the throne like water pouring from a great height. It spread across the stone floor in every direction. It reached the walls. It pooled against the base of the pillars. It covered the steps and the corners and the spaces betwixt the stones. There was no part of the temple floor that the robe did not touch. The whole house was full of it.

Above the throne stood angels.

They were called seraphim. Isaiah had never beheld any thing like them. Each one had six wings. Not two wings, like a bird. Not four. Six. With twain he covered his face. With twain he covered his feet. And with the last twain, each one did fly. They hovered in the air above the throne, their wings moving slowly, holding them steady in the smoky space beneath the ceiling.

They were bright. They were tall. And they were not silent.

They cried one unto another. One seraphim would cry, and another would answer. Their voices were enormous. The sound filled the temple as the robe filled the floor. It pressed against the walls and passed through every crack and opening.

"Holy, holy, holy is the Lord of hosts!"

Thus did they say. Over and over. One voice calling, another answering.

"The whole earth is full of his glory!"

The posts of the door shook.

The thick wooden frames that held the great temple doors began to rattle. They moved to and fro in their stone sockets. The noise was sharp — wood scraping against stone. The walls trembled. The floor trembled. The very foundations of the temple moved beneath Isaiah's sandals. He could feel the stone shifting under his feet, as though the ground itself were answering the voices of the seraphim.

Then smoke filled the house.

It rose from the altar in the midst of the temple. It curled upward in thick white columns, twisting round about the stone pillars. It drifted past the wings of the seraphim. It spread across the ceiling and rolled down along the walls. The air grew thick. The edges of things vanished. The robe, the throne, the wings — they all shimmered and flickered behind the moving smoke.

The temple bore the savour of fire. Of burning wood and incense and somewhat older and deeper that had no name.

Isaiah stood in the midst of it all.

His chest rose and fell. His hands hung at his sides. His eyes were wide. The smoke stung them, yet he did not close them. He could not close them.

He opened his mouth and cried out.

"Woe is me!" he said. His voice cracked. It sounded small against the echo of the seraphim. "For I am undone!"

He did not step forward. He did not step back. He stood where he was, compassed about by smoke and sound.

"For I am a man of unclean lips," he said. "And I dwell in the midst of a people of unclean lips."

His breath came in short bursts. The smoke moved round about his shoulders and past his face.

"For mine eyes," he said, "have seen the King — the Lord of hosts!"

The words left his mouth and vanished into the haze. For a moment, nothing changed. The seraphim yet hovered. The smoke yet curled. The throne yet rose.

Then one of the seraphim moved.

He came down from above the throne. His wings folded and opened and folded again, bearing him through the smoky air in a swift, straight line. He flew toward Isaiah — unto the place where he stood upon the shaking stone floor.

In his hand he held somewhat.

A coal. A live coal. It glowed orange in the midst and red at the edges. It was so hot that the air round about it rippled and bent. The seraphim had not taken it with bare fingers. He had used tongs — long metal tongs — and he had lifted the coal straight from off the altar. From the very place where the smoke arose. From the very midst of the burning.

The seraphim drew near.

Isaiah could feel the heat before the coal touched him. It came first as a warmth near his chin, then a sharp, pressing heat against his face. The angel reached out with the tongs.

The live coal touched Isaiah's lips.

It pressed there, hot and bright, against the mouth that had just spoken. Against the lips that had called themselves unclean.

Then the seraphim spake.

"Lo," the angel said. His voice was close — not as the great cry of holy, holy, holy, but near and clear, like a parent speaking gently to a child. "This hath touched thy lips."

Isaiah moved not.

"Thine iniquity is taken away," the seraphim said. "Thy sin is purged."

The angel lifted the coal from off Isaiah's mouth. The tongs drew back. The seraphim rose again, his wings opening wide, bearing him upward through the smoke and back toward the space above the throne. The heat lingered upon Isaiah's lips. He could feel it there — a warmth that abode, a mark that could not be seen but could be felt.

The smoke yet drifted. The pillars yet rose. The seraphim yet hovered upon their six wings, two covering the face, two covering the feet, two spread wide for flying. The robe yet covered the floor. The throne yet stood, high and lifted up.

The temple was quieter now. The shaking had ceased. The posts of the door had settled in their sockets. The foundations were still. But the smoke continued to move — slowly, gently — weaving betwixt the pillars like breath in a sleeping chamber.

Then a voice spake.

It came not from the seraphim. It came from the throne. It came from the place where the Lord sat, high above the robe and the smoke and the altar and the angels. It filled the temple. Not with shaking this time. Not with trembling. But with a question.

"Whom shall I send?"

The words rolled through the house. They passed over Isaiah and through the smoke and touched the walls.

"And who will go for us?"

The question hung in the air. The seraphim answered not. The smoke answered not. The stones and the pillars and the great wooden posts of the door said nothing.

Isaiah stood upon the temple floor. His sandals pressed against the cold stone. The warmth of the coal yet rested upon his lips. His hands were no longer limp at his sides. His chest no longer heaved. His eyes no longer stung.

He lifted up his face toward the throne.

"Here am I," Isaiah said. "Send me."
