The two men lay flat upon the roof of Rahab's house, under stalks of flax which she had spread over them. The flax was dry. It scratched the side of the face.

Below, in the street, the king's captain came up the slope unto her door.

Rahab heard the butt of his spear smite the wood. Once. Twice.

She went down with her hair covered. She opened the door but in part.

"There came men in unto me," she said, "but I wist not whence they came. And it came to pass about the time of shutting of the gate, when it was dark, that the men went out. Whither the men went, I wot not. Pursue after them quickly; for ye shall overtake them."

The captain's eye went past her into the house. The lamp burned upon the table where she had been sitting. The bread had been set out for three.

He did not look long.

"Go," she said again. "They are upon the road already."

The captain turned. The spears turned with him. The gate of the city was shut behind them.

She climbed back up unto the roof. She knelt down beside the flax.

"I know that the Lord hath given you the land," she said. "Your terror is fallen upon us. The inhabitants of the land faint because of you. We have heard how the Lord dried up the water of the Red sea when ye came out of Egypt. Our hearts did melt, neither did there remain any more courage in any man. For the Lord your God, he is God in heaven above, and in earth beneath.

Now therefore, swear unto me by the Lord — since I have shewed you kindness, that ye will also shew kindness unto my father's house. Give me a true token. Save alive my father, and my mother, and my brethren, and my sisters, and all that they have. Deliver our lives from death."

The men spake from under the flax. "Our life for yours," they said, "if ye utter not this our business. And it shall be, when the Lord hath given us the land, that we will deal kindly and truly with thee."

She brought them unto the window.

The window was set in the wall of the city, above the slope. Beyond the window was open air.

She bound a scarlet line unto the wood of the frame. The cord hung without the wall.

She let the men down by a second cord. Their feet found the stones of the wall, then the slope, then the dust of the road below.

"Get you to the mountain," she said down unto them. "Hide yourselves there three days, until the pursuers be returned. And afterward may ye go your way."

They gat them up into the mountain.

The cord abode in the window. The slope was empty. The dust their feet had stirred was already settling in the silence.
