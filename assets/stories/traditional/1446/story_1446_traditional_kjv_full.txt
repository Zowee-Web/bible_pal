Joshua sent the two men from Shittim in secret. He spake unto them low, where no other stood. "Go," he said. "View the land, even Jericho." They went forth at evening and crossed the plain alone.

They came into the city by the gate at dusk. The streets were narrow. The walls were high. They walked as men that had no business there, and they went unto the house of a woman named Rahab, which stood against the city wall. They slept there.

Word ran ahead of them in the city. Before the gate was shut for the night, the king of Jericho had heard. He sent his messengers up the slope unto her door.

The butt of a spear smote the wood. Once. Twice.

Rahab had already moved. She had brought the two men up the outer stairs to the roof. She had laid them down flat and pulled the stalks of flax over their bodies, dry stalks she had spread there in order. The flax scratched the side of the face. They moved not.

She covered her hair and went down. She opened the door but in part.

"There came men in unto me," she said, "but I wist not whence they came. And it came to pass about the time of the shutting of the gate, when it was dark, that the men went out. Whither the men went, I wot not. Pursue after them quickly; for ye shall overtake them."

Her voice did not shake. The captain looked past her into the room. The lamp burned low upon the table. He did not look long. He turned. The spears turned with him. They went down the slope toward the fords of the Jordan, and the gate of the city was shut behind them.

She drew the door closed. She stood a moment with her hand against the wood. Then she climbed back up unto the roof.

She knelt down beside the flax. The men came forth from under it slowly.

"I know that the Lord hath given you the land," she said. "The terror of you is fallen upon us. The inhabitants of the land faint before you. We have heard how the Lord dried up the water of the Red sea when ye came out of Egypt, and what was done unto Sihon and Og. Our hearts did melt. Neither did there remain any more spirit in any man. For the Lord your God, he is God in heaven above, and in earth beneath."

She drew breath.

"Now therefore, I pray you, swear unto me by the Lord. Since I have shewed you kindness, shew kindness also unto my father's house. Give me a true token. Save alive my father, and my mother, and my brethren, and my sisters, and all that they have. Deliver our lives from death."

The men answered her from where they sat. "Our life for yours," they said, "if ye utter not this our business. And it shall be, when the Lord hath given us the land, that we will deal kindly and truly with thee."

She brought them unto the window.

The window was set in the wall of the city, above the slope. Beyond it was open air. She bound a scarlet line unto the wood of the frame. The cord hung without the wall, red against the stone.

She let the men down by a second cord. Their hands gripped it. Their feet found the wall, then the slope, then the dust of the road below.

"Get you to the mountain," she said down unto them. "Hide yourselves there three days, until the pursuers be returned. And afterward may ye go your way."

They gat them up into the hills.

The slope was empty. The scarlet line stirred a little in the night air. She drew back from the window, and the lamp within the room burned on.
