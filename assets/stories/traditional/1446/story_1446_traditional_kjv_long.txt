Joshua the son of Nun sent the two men out of Shittim in secret. He spake unto them low, where no other stood. "Go," he said. "View the land, even Jericho." He did not send them with the camp watching. He sent them in the way a man sendeth what he cannot afford to lose.

They crossed the plain at evening. They came down through the dry brush toward the city walls. Jericho rose above the slope, set high and shut, its towers cut against the last of the light. The two men walked as men that had no business there. They had put off the marks of Israel. They carried no spears. They carried only the look of two travellers come at dusk.

They went in by the gate before it shut. The streets were narrow. The walls were high. People stood in doorways and watched them pass without speaking. The two men did not slow. They walked steadily through the lower quarter and came unto the house of a woman named Rahab, which stood against the wall of the city. They went inside. They slept there.

Word ran ahead of them. Before the gate was barred for the night, the king of Jericho had heard. He sent his messengers up the slope unto Rahab's door.

The butt of a spear smote the wood. Once. Twice. The sound carried into the upper room.

Rahab had already moved. The moment she had understood who the men were, she had brought them up the outer stairs to the roof. She had laid them down flat along the boards. She had pulled the stalks of flax over their bodies, dry stalks she had spread there in order to ret in the sun. The flax scratched the side of the face. It smelled of dust and of summer. She had pressed it down over the shape of their shoulders, so that the roof looked as it had looked the day before.

Now she stood within her door. She covered her hair. She set her face. She drew the latch back.

She opened the door but in part. The captain's men stood upon the step. The torches threw their faces in long shadow.

"There came men in unto me," she said, "but I wist not whence they came. And it came to pass about the time of the shutting of the gate, when it was dark, that the men went out. Whither the men went, I wot not. Pursue after them quickly; for ye shall overtake them."

Her voice did not shake. The captain looked past her into the room. The lamp burned low upon the table. A cup stood beside it. He did not look long. He turned. The spears turned with him. The torches went away down the slope toward the fords of the Jordan, and the gate of the city was shut behind them.

She drew the door closed. She stood a moment with her hand against the wood. She listened. The footsteps were gone. The lamp behind her made one small ring of light upon the floor.

She climbed the outer stairs unto the roof.

She knelt down beside the flax. She lifted it from over them slowly. The two men sat up. The dust of the flax was upon their hair and upon their shoulders. They spake not. They watched her.

"I know that the Lord hath given you the land," she said. "The terror of you is fallen upon us. The inhabitants of the land faint before you. We have heard how the Lord dried up the water of the Red sea when ye came out of Egypt. We have heard what was done unto the two kings of the Amorites that were beyond the Jordan, unto Sihon and unto Og, whom the Lord utterly destroyed. As soon as we had heard it, our hearts did melt. Neither did there remain any more spirit in any man. For the Lord your God, he is God in heaven above, and in earth beneath."

She drew breath. The roof was quiet. Below them in the streets the city had gone still for the night. Above them the stars were out.

"Now therefore, I pray you, swear unto me by the Lord," she said. "Since I have shewed you kindness, shew kindness also unto my father's house. Give me a true token. Save alive my father, and my mother, and my brethren, and my sisters, and all that they have. Deliver our lives from death."

The men spake from where they sat. "Our life for yours," they said, "if ye utter not this our business. And it shall be, when the Lord hath given us the land, that we will deal kindly and truly with thee."

One of them looked toward the window in the wall.

"When we come into the land," he said, "thou shalt bind this line of scarlet thread in the window which thou didst let us down by. Gather thy father, and thy mother, and thy brethren, and all thy father's household, unto thee into the house. Whosoever shall go out into the street, his blood shall be upon his head. Whosoever shall be with thee in the house, his blood shall be on our head, if any hand be laid upon him. But if thou utter this our business, then we will be quit of thine oath."

"According unto thy words, so be it," she answered.

She brought them unto the window.

The window was set in the wall of the city, above the slope. Beyond it was open air. The wall fell away beneath it for the height of three men. Below the wall ran the road, pale in the moonlight, going away into the hills.

She took up a length of scarlet cord and bound it unto the wood of the frame. The cord hung without the wall, red against the stone.

She let the men down by a second cord. Their hands gripped it. Their feet found the wall, then the slope, then the dust of the road below. They stood there a moment, looking up unto her. She leaned out from the window.

"Get you to the mountain," she said down unto them. "Lest the pursuers find you. Hide yourselves there three days, until the pursuers be returned. And afterward may ye go your way."

They turned. They gat them up into the hills. She watched until the slope took them from her, and then she watched the empty road, and then she drew back within.

The scarlet line abode at the window. The night air moved it a little against the stone. Below, in the lower room, her father slept, and her mother, and her brethren and her sisters. None of them yet knew. She would tell them in the morning. She would bring them up the stairs into this house, and they would abide, and the scarlet line would abide, and the city would do whatsoever it would do beyond the wall.

She set the lamp upon the sill near the cord. The light thereof touched the red, and the red was steady in the long dark of the city, and she sat down beside the window to wait for the morning.
