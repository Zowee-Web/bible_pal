Joshua the son of Nun sent the two men out of Shittim in secret. He spoke to them low, where no one else stood. "Go," he said. "View the land. Especially Jericho." He did not send them with the camp watching. He sent them in the way a man sends what he cannot afford to lose.

They crossed the plain at evening. They came down through the dry brush toward the city walls. Jericho rose above the slope, set high and shut, its towers cut against the last of the light. The two men walked as men who had no business there. They had taken off the marks of Israel. They carried no spears. They carried only the look of two travelers come at dusk.

They went in by the gate before it shut. The streets were narrow. The walls were high. People stood in doorways and watched them pass without speaking. The two men did not slow. They walked steadily through the lower quarter and came to the house of a woman named Rahab, which stood against the wall of the city. They went inside. They slept there.

Word ran ahead of them. Before the gate was barred for the night, the king of Jericho had heard. He sent his messengers up the slope to Rahab's door.

The butt of a spear struck the wood. Once. Twice. The sound carried into the upper room.

Rahab had already moved. The moment she had understood who the men were, she had taken them up the outer stairs to the roof. She had laid them down flat along the boards. She had pulled the stalks of flax over their bodies, dry stalks she had spread there in order to ret in the sun. The flax scratched the side of the face. It smelled of dust and of summer. She had pressed it down over the shape of their shoulders so that the roof looked as it had looked the day before.

Now she stood inside her door. She covered her hair. She set her face. She drew the latch back.

She opened the door only part of the way. The captain's men stood on the step. The torches threw their faces in long shadow.

"There came men in to me," she said, "but I did not know where they came from. About the time of the shutting of the gate, when it was dark, the men went out. Where the men went, I do not know. Pursue them quickly. You will overtake them."

Her voice did not shake. The captain looked past her into the room. The lamp burned low on the table. A cup stood beside it. He did not look long. He turned. The spears turned with him. The torches went away down the slope toward the fords of the Jordan, and the gate of the city was shut behind them.

She drew the door closed. She stood a moment with her hand against the wood. She listened. The footsteps were gone. The lamp behind her made one small ring of light on the floor.

She climbed the outer stairs to the roof.

She knelt down beside the flax. She lifted it from over them slowly. The two men sat up. The dust of the flax was on their hair and on their shoulders. They did not speak. They watched her.

"I know that Yahweh has given you the land," she said. "The terror of you has fallen on us. The inhabitants of the land melt before you. We have heard how Yahweh dried up the water of the Red Sea when you came out of Egypt. We have heard what was done to the two kings of the Amorites who were beyond the Jordan, to Sihon and to Og, whom Yahweh utterly destroyed. As soon as we had heard it, our hearts melted. No spirit remained in any man. For Yahweh your God is God in heaven above, and on earth beneath."

She drew breath. The roof was quiet. Below them in the streets the city had gone still for the night. Above them the stars were out.

"Now therefore, swear to me by Yahweh," she said. "Since I have dealt kindly with you, deal kindly with my father's house. Give me a true sign. Save alive my father and my mother, my brothers and my sisters, and all that they have. Deliver our lives from death."

The men spoke from where they sat. "Our life for yours," they said, "if you do not talk about this business of ours. When Yahweh gives us the land, we will deal kindly and truly with you."

One of them looked toward the window in the wall.

"When we come into the land," he said, "tie this line of scarlet thread in the window you let us down through. Gather your father and your mother, your brothers, and all your father's household into your house. Whoever goes out into the street, his blood will be on his head. Whoever is with you in the house, his blood shall be on our head, if any hand is laid on him. But if you talk about this business of ours, we will be guiltless of this oath."

"Let it be as you have said," she answered.

She brought them to the window.

The window was set in the wall of the city, above the slope. Beyond it was open air. The wall fell away beneath it for the height of three men. Below the wall ran the road, pale in the moonlight, going away into the hills.

She took up a length of scarlet cord and tied it to the wood of the frame. The cord hung outside the wall, red against the stone.

She let the men down by a second cord. Their hands gripped it. Their feet found the wall, then the slope, then the dirt of the road below. They stood there a moment, looking up at her. She leaned out from the window.

"Go to the mountain," she said down to them. "Lest the pursuers find you. Hide yourselves there three days, until the pursuers have returned. Afterward, go your way."

They turned. They went up into the hills. She watched until the slope took them from her, and then she watched the empty road, and then she drew back inside.

The scarlet line stayed at the window. The night air moved it a little against the stone. Below, in the lower room, her father slept, and her mother, and her brothers and her sisters. None of them yet knew. She would tell them in the morning. She would bring them up the stairs into this house, and they would stay, and the scarlet line would stay, and the city would do whatever it would do beyond the wall.

She set the lamp on the sill near the cord. The light of it touched the red, and the red was steady in the long dark of the city, and she sat down beside the window to wait for the morning.
