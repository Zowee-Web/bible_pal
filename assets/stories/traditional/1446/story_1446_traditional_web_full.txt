Joshua sent the two men from Shittim in secret. He spoke to them low, where no one else stood. "Go," he said. "View the land. Especially Jericho." They left at evening and crossed the plain alone.

They came into the city by the gate at dusk. The streets were narrow. The walls were high. They walked as men who had no business there, and they went to the house of a woman named Rahab, which stood against the city wall. They slept there.

Word ran ahead of them in the city. Before the gate was shut for the night, the king of Jericho had heard. He sent his messengers up the slope to her door.

The butt of a spear struck the wood. Once. Twice.

Rahab had already moved. She had taken the two men up the outer stairs to the roof. She had laid them down flat and pulled the stalks of flax over their bodies, dry stalks she had spread there in order. The flax scratched the side of the face. They did not move.

She covered her hair and went down. She opened the door only part of the way.

"There came men in to me," she said, "but I did not know where they came from. About the time of the shutting of the gate, when it was dark, the men went out. Where the men went, I do not know. Pursue them quickly. You will overtake them."

Her voice did not shake. The captain looked past her into the room. The lamp burned low on the table. He did not look long. He turned. The spears turned with him. They went down the slope toward the fords of the Jordan, and the gate of the city was shut behind them.

She drew the door closed. She stood a moment with her hand against the wood. Then she climbed back up to the roof.

She knelt down beside the flax. The men came out from under it slowly.

"I know that Yahweh has given you the land," she said. "The terror of you has fallen on us. The inhabitants of the land melt before you. We have heard how Yahweh dried up the water of the Red Sea when you came out of Egypt, and what was done to Sihon and Og. Our hearts melted. No spirit remained in any man. For Yahweh your God is God in heaven above, and on earth beneath."

She drew breath.

"Now therefore, swear to me by Yahweh. Since I have dealt kindly with you, deal kindly with my father's house. Give me a true sign. Save alive my father and my mother, my brothers and my sisters, and all that they have. Deliver our lives from death."

The men answered her from where they sat. "Our life for yours," they said, "if you do not talk about this business of ours. When Yahweh gives us the land, we will deal kindly and truly with you."

She brought them to the window.

The window was set in the wall of the city, above the slope. Beyond it was open air. She tied a scarlet cord to the wood of the frame. The cord hung outside the wall, red against the stone.

She let the men down by a second cord. Their hands gripped it. Their feet found the wall, then the slope, then the dirt of the road below.

"Go to the mountain," she said down to them. "Hide yourselves there three days, until the pursuers have returned. Afterward, go your way."

They went up into the hills.

The slope was empty. The scarlet line moved a little in the night air. She drew back from the window, and the lamp inside the room burned on.
