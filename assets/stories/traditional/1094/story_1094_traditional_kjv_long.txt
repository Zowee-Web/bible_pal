The word of the Lord came unto Jonah the son of Amittai. It was not an invitation. It was not a counsel. It was a command, spoken plainly, without ambiguity.

"Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and cry against it; for their wickedness is come up before me."

Nineveh. The very name bore weight. It was the capital of Assyria — a vast, walled city deep in the heart of Mesopotamia, known for its cruelty, its might, and the terror it wrought upon the nations round about. The Assyrians had crushed cities, carried whole peoples into captivity, and left a trail of suffering across the ancient world. To go to Nineveh and speak against it was not merely perilous — it was, by any human reckoning, foolishness.

Jonah rose. But he went not eastward toward Nineveh. He went west — as far west as a man might travel.

He went down to Joppa, a port upon the coast of Israel where the Mediterranean waves brake against the stone harbour walls and the air smelled of salt and pitch and drying fish. Ships rocked at their moorings, their rigging creaking in the wind. Jonah found a vessel bound for Tarshish — a distant port upon the far western edge of the known world. He paid the fare thereof, climbed aboard, and went down into the hold of the ship.

The ship put forth. The sails filled. The harbour shrank behind them, then the coastline, then the land itself. Jonah was in the belly of the ship, in the dark, moving away from all that God had bidden him do. He was fleeing from the presence of the Lord.

The sea was wide and open. For a season the voyage was uneventful — the ship cutting through the water, the wind steady, the horizon empty in every quarter.

Then the Lord hurled a great wind upon the sea.

It came without warning — a violent, shrieking gale that fell from the sky and smote the water with the force of a fist. The sea was troubled. Waves rose — towering walls of black water capped with white foam — and they crashed against the hull, sending spray flying across the deck. The ship pitched and rolled. Timbers groaned. The mast swayed. Water poured in through the seams. The ship was like to be broken.

The mariners were afraid. They were men of the sea — men who had spent their lives upon the water, who knew its ways — and they were afraid. Every man cried unto his god, shouting prayers into the wind, gripping whatsoever they might hold. They cast forth the wares that were in the ship into the sea — crates, barrels, rolls of cloth, jars of oil — heaving them over the side, trying to lighten the vessel ere it was dragged under.

And Jonah was gone down into the sides of the ship, and was fast asleep.

The shipmaster found him in the hold, lying in the dark while the ship was torn apart above him. "What meanest thou, O sleeper?" the captain cried above the roar of the tempest. "Arise, call upon thy God, if so be that God will think upon us, that we perish not."

The men gathered upon the listing deck. They resolved to cast lots — to learn for whose cause this evil had come upon them. The lots were cast. The lot fell upon Jonah.

They compassed him about. The wind whipped their hair. The spray stung their faces. "Tell us, we pray thee," they said, "for whose cause is this evil upon us? What is thine occupation? Whence comest thou? What is thy country? Of what people art thou?"

Jonah looked upon them. The ship pitched beneath his feet. "I am an Hebrew," he said, "and I fear the Lord, the God of heaven, which hath made the sea and the dry land."

The words fell upon them more heavily than the waves. The God that made the sea. The sea that was now seeking their destruction.

"What is this that thou hast done?" they whispered. For Jonah had told them that he fled from the presence of the Lord.

"What shall we do unto thee," they asked, "that the sea may be calm unto us?"

Jonah hesitated not. "Take me up," he said, "and cast me forth into the sea; so shall the sea be calm unto you: for I know that for my sake this great tempest is upon you."

The mariners would not do it. They were not men of blood. They took up the oars and rowed hard, striving to bring the ship to land. But they could not. The sea wrought and was tempestuous against them. The waves were higher. The wind screamed. The ship groaned and shuddered.

At the last, having no other course, they cried unto the Lord. "We beseech thee, O Lord, we beseech thee, let us not perish for this man's life, and lay not upon us innocent blood: for thou, O Lord, hast done as it pleased thee." Then they took up Jonah, and cast him forth into the sea.

And the sea ceased from her raging.

The wind was stilled. The waves fell flat. The surface of the water grew calm — suddenly, completely, impossibly calm. The ship steadied. The ropes went slack. The only sound was the dripping of water from the rigging and the breathing of the men standing upon the deck.

A great fear fell upon them — not the fear of the storm, but a deeper fear. They offered a sacrifice unto the Lord, and made vows.

Beneath the surface, Jonah sank. The water closed over him — dark and cold and heavy. He went down.

And the Lord had prepared a great fish to swallow up Jonah. It rose from the deep and took him whole. And Jonah was in the belly of the fish three days and three nights.
