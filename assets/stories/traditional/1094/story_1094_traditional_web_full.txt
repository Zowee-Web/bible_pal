The word of the Lord came to Jonah, the son of Amittai. The message was clear and direct. "Arise," God said. "Go to Nineveh, that great city, and cry out against it, for their wickedness has come up before me."

Nineveh was east — deep in the heart of Assyria, a city of massive walls and crowded streets, feared and hated by the nations around it. The journey would have been long, dangerous, and deeply unwelcome.

Jonah rose. But he did not go east. He went west.

He went down to Joppa, a port city on the Mediterranean coast where the waves broke against the stone harbor walls and the smell of salt and pitch hung in the air. He found a ship bound for Tarshish — as far west as the known world stretched, as far from Nineveh as a man could sail. He paid the fare, boarded the ship, and went below deck into the hold. The ship cast off from the harbor, the sails filled, and the coast of Israel fell away behind them. He was running from the presence of the Lord.

The sea was open and wide. The wind was favorable. The ship moved steadily west.

Then the Lord sent a great wind upon the sea. It came without warning — a violent, howling storm that struck the ship broadside. Waves crashed over the deck. The timbers groaned and cracked. The mast swayed. Water poured through the seams of the hull. The ship was in danger of breaking apart.

The sailors were terrified. Every man cried out to his own god — shouting prayers into the wind, gripping the rails, stumbling across the pitching deck. They threw the cargo overboard, heaving crates and barrels and rolls of cloth into the churning water, trying to lighten the ship before it was dragged under.

And Jonah was below deck, fast asleep.

The captain found him in the hold, lying in the dark. "How can you sleep?" he shouted over the wind. "Get up! Call on your God! Maybe he will take notice of us so that we will not perish."

The sailors decided to cast lots to find out who had brought this disaster upon them. They drew the lots. The lot fell on Jonah.

They surrounded him. "Tell us," they said. "Who are you? Where do you come from? What have you done?"

Jonah looked at them. "I am a Hebrew," he said. "I worship the Lord, the God of heaven, who made the sea and the dry land." And he told them he was running away from God.

The men were terrified — more than they had been of the storm itself. "What should we do to you," they asked, "to make the sea calm?"

"Pick me up," Jonah said, "and throw me into the sea. Then it will become calm. I know that this great storm is my fault."

The sailors did not want to do it. They rowed hard, trying to reach shore. But the storm grew worse. The waves were higher. The wind screamed. Finally, they cried out to the Lord: "Please, O Lord, do not let us perish for this man's life, and do not hold us responsible for innocent blood." Then they picked Jonah up and threw him into the sea.

And the sea stopped raging. The wind dropped. The waves flattened. The surface of the water grew calm. The sailors stood on the deck in the sudden stillness, and a deep fear of the Lord came over them.

The Lord provided a great fish. It rose from the deep water and swallowed Jonah whole. And Jonah was in the belly of the fish for three days and three nights.
