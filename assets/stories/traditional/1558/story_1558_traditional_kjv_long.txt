It was the third hour of the day when they put him upon the cross.

The multitude had walked up the hill behind him in the hot morning. He had borne the beam through the streets of the city. He had fallen under it. Simon of Cyrene, who had come to the city for the feast, had been pulled out of the multitude and compelled to bear it for him the rest of the way. Now they were at the top, on the hill that men called the Place of a Skull, without the walls.

The soldiers were parting his raiment below him, casting lots for the seamless robe — the one his mother had made for him, the one that should not be rent. They cast the lots openly, upon the rock, without thinking of it. They had done this with many men. There was no ceremony to it. The work moved on.

The women that followed him from Galilee stood afar off, and beheld. Mary, his mother, was among them. Mary Magdalene was among them. Mary the wife of Cleophas was among them. The other women whose names are not all written down were among them. John, the disciple whom he loved, was somewhere upon the edge of the multitude. He had not gone away. He had not been able to depart.

The first word he said was for the soldiers.

"Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do."

It was not the manner of word said in that place. The soldiers down below looked not up. They were good at what they were doing, and what they were doing was the same work they did at every cross. They had not asked who he was, and they knew not. He said the word for them notwithstanding. The text saith not whether any of them heard. It saith only that he said it.

The rulers passed beneath him and derided him. "He saved others," they said. "Let him save himself, if he be Christ, the chosen of God." It was their best jest. They were satisfied with it. The multitude around them laughed.

The soldiers, coming and going at their watch, mocked him. They offered him vinegar from the jar. One called up unto him about saving the king of the Jews. The superscription over his head, written in Greek, and Latin, and Hebrew, that every passer-by might read it in his own tongue, said it for them: THIS IS THE KING OF THE JEWS. Pilate had written it so, and would not change it when the priests required it.

Two malefactors had been crucified with him, one on his right and the other on his left.

The one on the left railed on him in the same words the rulers had used. "If thou be Christ, save thyself and us!" His voice was bitter. He was dying in pain. He used the only weapon he had left.

The one on the right answered him back. He had been silent until then. He spake now without looking at his neighbour, for he could not turn his head far. He spake as one who had been thinking upon this a long time.

"Dost not thou fear God, seeing thou art in the same condemnation? And we indeed justly; for we receive the due reward of our deeds: but this man hath done nothing amiss."

There was a pause. The wind moved across the hill. The soldiers cast their lot.

Then he turned his face toward Jesus. He could not move much. But he turned his face.

"Lord," he said, "remember me when thou comest into thy kingdom."

He asked not to be taken down. He asked not to be spared. He asked only that the man on the cross beside him would remember his name on the other side of the door he was about to walk through.

Jesus turned his head toward him. His second word was for the man beside him.

"Verily I say unto thee, To day shalt thou be with me in paradise."

The man heard. The text saith not what his face did when he heard it. He was a thief. He had been upon the cross many hours. There was not much his face could do. But he heard. Whatever came to pass within him at that moment, no man else hath ever fully seen.

It was about the sixth hour now. The sun went out. Not slowly, as the sun goeth out at evening. All at once. A darkness came over all the earth, and abode there, until the ninth hour. The veil of the temple, far off in the city — the great curtain that separated the holy place from the most holy, woven thick in blue and purple and crimson — was rent in twain from the top to the bottom. The priests in the inner court understood not what was come to pass. The people on the hill understood not either. They stood beholding. No man knew what to do. The multitude grew quieter than multitudes grew at this manner of thing.

He hung there in the dark. The soldiers stayed at their posts. The women departed not. The hour passed. There was nothing but the hill and the cross and the darkness.

Then Jesus cried with a loud voice. It was his third word.

"Father, into thy hands I commend my spirit."

And having said thus, he gave up the ghost.

The centurion that stood facing him saw what was done. He was a Roman soldier. He had been at many crosses. He had seen many men die. He had seen most kinds of dying that there were to see. He had not seen one die thus. He glorified God. He said: certainly, this was a righteous man.

The other soldiers looked at one another. They said nothing. They went back to their work.

And the people that had come together to behold turned and went home, smiting their breasts. The hill emptied. They had come for a spectacle and had received something they had not been prepared to receive, and they had not yet words for what to do with it. They should have words later. They had them not yet.

The women that followed him from Galilee departed not. They stood afar off, and beheld these things. They watched the soldiers take him down, when the request came from below. They watched the men bear his body unto the sepulchre that Joseph of Arimathaea had offered. They watched where he was laid, that they might know how to come back when the sabbath was past.

And so it was on the day the King of the Jews was crucified, on the hill without the city, between the sixth hour and the ninth.
