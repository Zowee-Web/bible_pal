Noah was a righteous man, blameless among the people of his time. He walked with God. The text does not catalogue his virtues or list his deeds. It says he was righteous. It says he was blameless. It says he walked with God. That was enough.

He had three sons: Shem, Ham, and Japheth.

The earth was corrupt in God's sight. Violence filled it — not the quiet, hidden violence of private wrongs, but something open and total. All flesh had corrupted their way on the earth. The word "all" carries its full weight here. Not most. Not many. All. Men struck each other in the roads and took what they wanted. The strong seized the fields of the weak and dared anyone to object. Blood dried in the dirt outside the gates of cities, and no one paused to remark on it. Disputes were settled with fists and blades. The marketplaces rang with curses. Children grew up watching cruelty and learned to call it ordinary.

God looked at the earth. He saw what it had become. And he spoke to Noah.

"The end of all flesh has come before me, for the earth is filled with violence through them. I will destroy them with the earth."

The statement was absolute. No qualification softened it. No appeal was invited. The judgment had been rendered, and the sentence was total destruction. The earth itself would share in the undoing.

Then God gave instructions. They came with the precision of a master builder addressing a skilled craftsman, each specification exact, each measurement deliberate.

"Make yourself an ark of gopher wood."

The word "ark" described a vessel — not a ship with sails and rudder, but a sealed container, a chest built to float. It would have no means of navigation. There would be no helm, no oars, no way to steer. It was designed to endure, not to travel. To preserve, not to journey.

"Make rooms in the ark, and cover it inside and outside with pitch."

Rooms. Compartments. Spaces divided for different purposes — quarters for the family, pens for animals, storage for food. And pitch on every surface, inside and out. The dark resin would have to be gathered in enormous quantities, heated over fire until it ran thick, and spread by hand across hundreds of cubits of wood. Every joint sealed. Every seam filled. Every crack where water might press through, covered twice over.

The dimensions were given. The length of the ark: three hundred cubits. The breadth: fifty cubits. The height: thirty cubits. These were not approximate figures. They were exact. A vessel of this size had never been built — not by Noah, not by anyone in his generation or any generation before it. Three hundred cubits of hull. A man standing at one end could barely make out the far wall. The scale was enormous. The timber required would strip whole hillsides bare. Each tree felled, each log dragged to the building site, each trunk split into planks with wedges and mauls.

A window was to be set near the top, finished to a cubit from the upper edge. Light and air would enter from above, near the roofline. A door was to be placed in the side of the ark — a single entrance, large enough for the animals that would come. And three decks: lower, second, and third. Three levels of space, stacked one above the other, supported by beams and crossbraces running the full length of the hull.

The labor would take years. Noah would have to source the gopher wood — finding stands of the right trees, felling them, hauling the trunks to the building site. He would have to split the logs into planks and shape them with adzes. He would have to frame the ribs of the hull, lay the keel, build the walls up plank by plank. Each deck would need to be floored and supported. The rooms would need to be framed and enclosed. And every surface — every plank, every beam, every joint — would need pitch applied on both sides.

God explained what was coming. He did not speak in metaphor. He said it plainly.

"I will bring a flood of waters on the earth to destroy all flesh in which is the breath of life from under the sky. Everything that is on the earth will die."

A flood. Water from above and below, rising until it covered the ground, the trees, the hills, the high places. Everything that breathed — man, woman, beast, bird — would perish. The earth would be returned to the waters.

But God established a covenant with Noah. The word "covenant" carried weight. It was not a suggestion. It was a binding agreement, initiated by God himself.

"You will come into the ark — you, your sons, your wife, and your sons' wives with you."

Eight people. Out of all the earth, eight would be preserved. Noah and his wife. Shem, Ham, and Japheth, each with his wife. They would enter the ark and live.

Of every living thing of all flesh, Noah was to bring two of every sort into the ark to keep them alive with him. Male and female. Birds after their kind. Animals after their kind. Every creeping thing of the ground after its kind. Two of every sort would come to Noah. The text says they would come — not that Noah would hunt or trap them, but that they would come to him of their own accord.

He was also to take food. Every sort of food that is eaten — grain, fruit, dried stores, fodder for livestock, seed for birds, whatever each creature required. He was to gather it and store it aboard the ark as provision for his family and for every living thing that would share the vessel.

The full scope of what God asked was immense. Not merely the construction of the largest vessel ever built, but the provisioning of it. Food for eight people and for every kind of animal, stored in sufficient quantity to last an unknown duration. The planning alone — calculating rations, organizing storage, ensuring predators were separated from prey, that water was accessible, that waste could be managed — would have tested the most capable mind.

And this in a world that mocked the very idea. No rain had come. No flood was visible. The sky was clear. The rivers ran in their courses. The ground was dry. A man building a massive vessel on dry land, stocking it with food, preparing pens for animals — the scene would have invited ridicule from every neighbor who passed. The corruption continued. The violence went on. And in the middle of it, on open ground, one man cut timber and measured beams and heated pitch.

The text records no complaint from Noah. No question asked. No objection raised. No bargaining for easier terms. No request for a smaller boat, a shorter timeline, a different plan. The silence of Noah in this passage is one of its most striking features. God spoke. Noah listened. And then Noah acted.

He did this. According to all that God commanded him, so he did.

Plank by plank. Beam by beam. Deck by deck. He cut the gopher wood and shaped it. He fitted the joints and sealed them with pitch, inside and out. He built the rooms and laid the floors of the three decks. He set the window near the top, finished to a cubit from the upper edge. He framed the door in the side. He measured and built and measured again — three hundred cubits long, fifty cubits wide, thirty cubits high.

The work went on. Season after season, the ark took shape on the open ground. The smell of fresh-cut wood and hot pitch hung in the air around the building site. Sawdust gathered in drifts around the base of the growing hull. The sound of hammers and adzes carried across the land — a steady rhythm of labor that did not pause, did not waver, did not stop.

Noah did according to all that God commanded him. So he did.
