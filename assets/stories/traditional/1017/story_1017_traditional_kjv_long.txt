Noah was a just man, and perfect in his generations. Noah walked with God. The record giveth no catalogue of his virtues, no list of mighty deeds. It saith he was just. It saith he was perfect in his generations. It saith he walked with God. That was enough.

He begat three sons: Shem, Ham, and Japheth.

The earth was corrupt before God. Violence filled it — not the quiet, hidden violence of private wrongs, but something open and total. All flesh had corrupted his way upon the earth. The word "all" beareth its full weight here. Not most. Not many. All. Men struck one another in the roads and took what they desired. The strong seized the fields of the weak and dared any man to gainsay them. Blood dried in the dust outside the gates of cities, and none paused to remark upon it. Quarrels were settled with fists and blades. The marketplaces rang with curses. Children grew up beholding cruelty and learned to call it common.

God looked upon the earth. He beheld what it had become. And he spake unto Noah.

"The end of all flesh is come before me, for the earth is filled with violence through them. Behold, I will destroy them with the earth."

The word was absolute. No softening followed. No appeal was bidden. The judgment had been rendered, and the sentence was utter destruction. The earth itself would share in the undoing.

Then God gave instruction. It came with the exactness of a master builder addressing a skilled workman, each measure laid out in turn, each dimension certain.

"Make thee an ark of gopher wood."

The word "ark" bespoke a vessel — not a ship with sail and rudder, but a sealed chest, wrought to float. It would have no means to steer. There would be no helm, no oar, no way to set a course. It was fashioned to endure, not to voyage. To preserve, not to journey.

"Rooms shalt thou make in the ark, and shalt pitch it within and without with pitch."

Rooms. Chambers. Spaces divided for sundry purposes — dwelling quarters for the family, pens for beasts, stores for food. And pitch upon every surface, within and without. The dark resin would have to be gathered in vast quantity, heated over fire till it ran thick, and spread by hand across hundreds of cubits of wood. Every joint sealed. Every seam filled. Every crack where water might press through, covered twice over.

The dimensions were given. The length of the ark: three hundred cubits. The breadth thereof: fifty cubits. The height thereof: thirty cubits. These were not figures given by guess. They were exact. A vessel of such measure had never been wrought — not by Noah, not by any man in his generation or in any generation before it. Three hundred cubits of hull. A man standing at the one end could scarce discern the far wall. The scale was vast. The timber required would strip whole hillsides bare. Each tree felled, each log dragged to the place of building, each trunk cleft into planks with wedge and maul.

A window was to be set near the top, finished in a cubit above. Light and air would enter from on high, near the roof. The door of the ark was to be set in the side — a single entrance, of breadth sufficient for the beasts that would come. And three stories within: lower, second, and third. Three levels of dwelling space, laid one above another, borne up by beams and braces running the full length of the hull.

The labour would take years. Noah must find the gopher wood — felling the proper trees, hauling the trunks to the place of building. He must split the logs into planks and shape them with the adze. He must frame the ribs of the hull, lay the keel, raise the walls plank upon plank. Each story must be floored and braced. The rooms must be framed and enclosed. And every surface — every plank, every beam, every joint — must have pitch laid upon it, within and without.

God declared what was to come. He spake not in figure or riddle. He said it plain.

"Behold, I do bring a flood of waters upon the earth, to destroy all flesh wherein is the breath of life from under heaven. Every thing that is in the earth shall die."

A flood. Waters from above and beneath, rising until they covered the ground, the trees, the hills, the high places. All that drew breath — every man, every woman, every beast, every fowl — would perish. The earth would be given back unto the waters.

But with Noah, God established his covenant. The word "covenant" bore weight. It was not a suggestion, not a wish. It was a binding word, spoken by God himself.

"Thou shalt come into the ark — thou, and thy sons, and thy wife, and thy sons' wives with thee."

Eight souls. Out of all the earth, eight would be kept alive. Noah and his wife. Shem, Ham, and Japheth, each with his wife. They would enter the ark and live.

Of every living thing of all flesh, two of every sort were to be brought into the ark, to keep them alive with him. Male and female. Of fowls after their kind. Of cattle after their kind. Of every creeping thing of the earth after his kind. Two of every sort would come unto Noah. The record saith they would come — not that Noah must hunt them or snare them, but that they would come unto him of their own accord.

He was also to take food. Of all food that is eaten — grain, fruit, dried provision, fodder for beasts, seed for fowls, whatsoever each creature had need of. He was to gather it and lay it up in the ark, for provision unto his household and unto every living thing that would share the vessel.

The whole compass of what God required was immense. Not merely the framing of the greatest vessel ever wrought, but the provisioning of it. Food for eight souls and for every manner of beast, stored in measure sufficient for a time none could foretell. The reckoning alone — of rations, of space, of the keeping apart of hunter from prey, of water and waste and the ordering of every need — would have tried the ablest mind.

And all this in a world that counted the work as folly. No rain had fallen for this cause. No flood was seen. The sky stood clear. The rivers ran in their accustomed channels. The ground was dry. A man raising a vast vessel upon dry land, filling it with stores, readying pens for creatures — the sight would have drawn scorn from every neighbour who passed. The corruption went on. The violence continued. And in the midst of it, upon open ground, one man hewed timber and measured beams and heated pitch.

The record keepeth no account of complaint from Noah. No question asked. No objection raised. No bargaining for lighter terms. No plea for a lesser vessel, a shorter labour, a different way. The silence of Noah in this passage is among its most striking marks. God spake. Noah hearkened. And Noah wrought.

Thus did Noah. According to all that God commanded him, so did he.

Plank upon plank. Beam upon beam. Story upon story. He hewed the gopher wood and shaped it. He fitted the joints and sealed them with pitch, within and without. He framed the rooms and laid the floors of the three stories. He set the window near the top, finished in a cubit above. He made the door in the side of the ark. He measured and wrought and measured again — three hundred cubits in length, fifty cubits in breadth, thirty cubits in height.

The work went on. Season upon season, the ark took form upon the open ground. The smell of new-hewn timber and hot pitch hung upon the air about the place of building. Sawdust gathered in drifts about the base of the rising hull. The sound of hammer and adze carried across the surrounding land, a steady measure of labour that paused not, faltered not, ceased not.

Thus did Noah. According to all that God commanded him, so did he.
