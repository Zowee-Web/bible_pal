The children of Israel stood at the brink of the sea. Behind them rose the noise of the host of Egypt, filling the breadth of the horizon — the low thunder of hooves, the rattling of chariots, the cracking of whips upon the air. A great pillar of dust climbed up out of the south, ascending into the evening sky and spreading abroad like a stain upon the heavens. The wind bore unto them the smell of horses, and of leather, and of hot metal. There was no place left whereunto they might flee. Before them lay the sea, gray and flat, touching the sky at its far edge. Small waves moved against the shore in a slow and steady measure. The people looked unto the sea. They looked unto the dust. They spake not a word.

Then stretched Moses forth his hand over the sea.

And the Lord sent a strong east wind. It came suddenly, pressing hard upon the face of the waters. The sea rippled, and pushed back against it, and the wind ceased not. It blew through the night, steady and loud, laying tents flat and bending the grass along the shore. Loose sand lifted off the dunes and traveled in long low ribbons across the ground. Ropes snapped against the tent poles. The beasts braced themselves into the wind, with heads lowered and ears laid flat. And the sea was parted. The face of the waters was cloven down the midst. The waters drew back unto the right, and drew back unto the left, and the bottom of the sea was uncovered in long uneven strips. First the dark mud, glistening and soft. Then packed sand. Then stone worn smooth by the long passing of currents, streaked with the dull green of old weed. And the ground dried under the force of the wind. The waters rose higher on either hand, climbing and holding, standing as walls. And between those walls stretched a wide corridor of dry ground, cutting straight across the bed of the sea unto the farther shore.

And the people went forward. Thousands upon thousands. Fathers and mothers bore their children upon their hips and shoulders. Men led their cattle by cord, pulling steadily, speaking low. Women walked with bundles balanced upon their shoulders, their robes pressed tight against them by the wind. The wind pressed hard against their robes and their hair. Their sandals struck firm ground where but hours before the fish had swum. Pieces of shell crunched beneath their feet. The walls of water towered on either side — dark, and heavy, and still. Spray drifted off the tops of the walls, catching what little light remained in the sky. And within the water itself, shapes moved — the long shadows of fishes suspended in the standing wall, drawn neither forward nor back. The sound of the sea held in its place was deep and constant, a low humming in the chest.

They passed over, every one. Every tribe, every household, every beast. Wide enough was the path for all of them. The little ones looked up unto the waters and gripped tighter the hands of their fathers. Some pointed. Some turned away their faces. The ground held firm beneath their feet. Old men leaned upon their staves and ceased not to go forward. Mothers spake quietly unto the babes upon their bosoms. The line of the people stretched from the one shore unto the other, a long dark thread between two standing walls.

And behind them, the Egyptians beheld the open way. The captains of Pharaoh gave the word. The chariots rolled forth upon the dry bed of the sea. The horses drove hard, their hooves hammering the packed sand. The horsemen followed after in close ranks. The whole strength of the host of Egypt entered into the corridor between the walls of water — the iron-rimmed wheels turning swiftly, the spears held upright, the armor catching the faint light. Banners snapped above the chariots. The drivers leaned forward over the reins. They were set to close the gap.

Then the Lord looked down upon the host of the Egyptians through the pillar of fire and of the cloud, and he troubled them.

The chariot wheels seized. Some locked fast, dragging hard against the ground. Others cracked and brake away from the axles, spinning loose and rolling sideways. The heavy frames dropped and scraped along the ground, casting up sand and broken stone. The horses pulled sideways, tangled in their harness, with wide eyes and open mouths. Riders fell from their beasts and struck the earth hard. The close array was broken. Chariot piled upon chariot with the sharp crack of splintering wood. The soldiers cried out. The captains cried out over the soldiers. And the ground that had been firm and dry now dragged at every wheel and every hoof, soft where it had been solid.

The Egyptians called one unto another. Their voices carried across the bed of the sea, sharp with terror.

"Let us flee," they cried, "for the Lord fighteth for Israel."

They sought to turn back. Chariots swung wide and jammed against the wreckage of others. Horses reared and kicked against the broken frames. Men hewed at their harness with the sword and strove to pull the horses free. The host that had charged forward in close ranks was now a tumult of wood and iron and men labouring to move every way at once. And above them, the walls of water yet stood.

And the Lord said unto Moses, Stretch forth thine hand over the sea, that the waters may return upon the Egyptians, upon their chariots, and upon their horsemen.

Moses stood upon the farther shore. The first gray light of the dawn was coming up behind him, spreading low across the waters and touching the tops of the standing walls. He stretched forth his hand over the sea.

And the walls moved. The water on the right hand leaned inward. The water on the left hand leaned inward. The sea returned to his strength. It came not slowly, but all at once, a great rushing from either side, meeting in the midst with a sound as of a mountain breaking asunder. White foam burst upward where the walls smote together. The waters covered the chariots first. Then the horsemen. Then the footmen. The current drew all things down and under — men, and horses, and iron, and wood. Spears spun upon the surface a moment and were gone. Helmets bobbed and sank. The face of the waters churned white for a long moment, and then was still.

The sea lay flat again. The wind fell. The morning light spread across the waters, and the surface gave back the pale sky. A single gull crossed overhead and called once.

There remained not a man of the Egyptians. Not one chariot. Not one horse. The whole host of Pharaoh that had gone in after Israel into the sea was utterly consumed.

And along the farther shore, the bodies of the Egyptians began to wash up against the sand. Armor glinted in the shallow water. Broken wood of the chariot frames rolled in the low waves. A horse's bridle drifted in, the leather dark and heavy with the sea. Pieces of cloth from the torn banners floated in slow circles.

And the children of Israel stood upon the dry ground, and looked out across the sea. They saw the great work which the Lord had done against Egypt. They fell upon their knees. They bowed their faces unto the ground. No man spake. The only sound was the soft moving of the water against the shore.

The morning was still. The water lapped quietly against the shore. Pieces of harness and splintered wheels rocked in the shallows at the water's edge.
