The people of Israel stood at the edge of the sea. Behind them, the sound of the Egyptian army filled the horizon — the low thunder of hooves, the rattle of chariot frames, the sharp crack of whips. Dust rose in a wide column to the south, climbing into the evening sky and spreading across it like a stain. The wind carried the smell of horses and leather and hot metal. There was nowhere left to go. The water spread before them, gray and flat, touching the sky at the far edge. Small waves moved against the shore in a slow, steady rhythm. The people looked at the sea. They looked at the dust. They did not speak.

Moses stretched out his hand over the sea.

The Lord sent a strong east wind. It came suddenly, pressing hard against the surface. The water rippled, then pushed back, and the wind did not stop. It blew through the night, steady and loud, flattening tents and bending the grass along the shore. Loose sand lifted off the dunes and traveled in long low ribbons across the ground. Ropes snapped against tent poles. Animals braced themselves into the wind, heads lowered, ears flat. The sea pulled apart. The surface split down the center. Water drew back to the right and drew back to the left, exposing the bottom in long uneven strips. First dark mud, glistening and soft. Then packed sand. Then stone worn smooth by years of current, streaked with the dull green of old weed. The ground dried under the force of the wind. The water rose higher on each side, climbing and holding, standing like walls. Between those walls stretched a wide corridor of dry ground cutting straight across the seabed to the far shore.

The people moved forward. Thousands of them. Families carried children on their hips and shoulders. Men led animals by rope, pulling steadily, speaking low. Women walked with bundles balanced on their shoulders, robes pressed tight against their bodies by the wind. The wind pressed hard against their robes and hair. Their sandals struck solid ground where hours before fish had swum. Pieces of shell crunched underfoot. The walls of water towered on both sides — dark, heavy, still. Spray drifted off the tops of the walls, catching what little light remained in the sky. Inside the water itself, shapes moved — long shadows of fish suspended in the standing wall, pulled neither forward nor back. The sound of the sea held in place was deep and constant, a low hum in the chest.

They walked all the way across. Every tribe, every family, every animal. The path was wide enough for all of them. Children looked up at the water and gripped their parents' hands tighter. Some pointed. Some turned their faces away. The ground held firm beneath their feet. Old men leaned on staffs and kept moving. Mothers spoke quietly to babies in their arms. The line of people stretched from one shore to the other, a long dark thread between two standing walls.

Behind them, the Egyptians saw the open path. Pharaoh's commanders gave the order. Chariots rolled forward onto the dry seabed. The horses drove hard, hooves hammering against the packed sand. Horsemen followed in tight columns. The full strength of the Egyptian army entered the corridor between the walls of water — iron-rimmed wheels turning fast, spears held upright, armor catching the faint light. Banners snapped above the chariots. Drivers leaned forward over the reins. They were determined to close the gap.

Then the Lord looked down on the Egyptian army through the pillar of fire and cloud, and he threw them into confusion.

The chariot wheels seized. Some locked in place, dragging hard against the ground. Others cracked and broke away from the axles, spinning loose and rolling sideways. The heavy wooden frames dropped and scraped against the ground, throwing up sand and broken stone. Horses pulled sideways, tangled in their harness, eyes wide, mouths open. Riders fell from their mounts and struck the ground hard. The tight formation collapsed. Chariots piled against each other with the sharp crack of splintering wood. Soldiers shouted. Commanders shouted over the soldiers. The ground that had been firm and dry now dragged at every wheel and hoof, soft where it had been solid.

The Egyptians called out to one another. Their voices carried across the seabed, sharp with panic.

"Let us flee from the face of Israel," they said, "for the Lord fights for them against the Egyptians."

They tried to turn around. Chariots swung wide and jammed against the wreckage of others. Horses reared and kicked against the broken frames. Men cut harnesses with their swords and tried to pull horses free. The army that had charged forward in tight ranks was now a disordered mass of wood and iron and men trying to move in every direction at once. Above them, the walls of water still stood.

The Lord said to Moses, "Stretch out your hand over the sea, that the waters may come back upon the Egyptians, upon their chariots, and upon their horsemen."

Moses stood on the far shore. The first gray light of dawn was coming up behind him, spreading low across the water and touching the tops of the standing walls. He stretched out his hand over the sea.

The walls moved. The water on the right leaned inward. The water on the left leaned inward. The sea returned to its normal flow. It came not slowly but all at once, a great rush from both sides, meeting in the center with a sound like a mountain breaking apart. White foam exploded upward where the walls collided. The water covered the chariots first. Then the horsemen. Then the foot soldiers. The current pulled everything down and under — men, horses, iron, wood. Spears spun on the surface for a moment and then disappeared. Helmets bobbed and sank. The surface churned white for a long moment, then settled.

The sea lay flat again. The wind dropped. The morning light spread across the water, and the surface reflected the pale sky. A single gull crossed overhead and called once.

Not one Egyptian soldier remained. Not one chariot. Not one horse. The entire army of Pharaoh that had followed Israel into the sea was gone.

Along the far shore, the bodies of Egyptian soldiers began to wash up against the sand. Armor glinted in the shallow water. Broken wood from chariot frames rolled in the low waves. A horse's bridle drifted in, the leather dark and heavy with water. Pieces of cloth from torn banners floated in slow circles.

The people of Israel stood on dry ground and looked out across the sea. They saw the great work the Lord had done against Egypt. They dropped to their knees. They bowed their faces to the ground. No one spoke. The only sound was the soft movement of water against the shore.

The morning was still. The water lapped quietly against the shore. Pieces of harness and splintered wheels rocked in the shallows at the waterline.
