And the children of Israel stood upon the edge of the sea. Behind them rose the sound of the host of Egypt, filling all the land — the low thunder of hooves upon the earth, the rattling of chariot frames one against another, the sharp report of whips. Dust ascended in a broad pillar toward the south. There remained no place to flee. The water lay before them, grey and still, stretching unto the far heavens where sea and sky were joined together.

And Moses stretched forth his hand over the sea.

Then the Lord sent a great wind out of the east. It came upon them suddenly, pressing hard against the face of the waters. The surface rippled and was driven back, and the wind abated not. All through the night it blew, steady and loud, beating down the tents and bending low the grass along the shore. And the sea was rent asunder. The waters divided, drawing back upon the right hand and upon the left, and the bottom was laid bare in long and uneven stretches. First there appeared dark mud, then sand pressed firm, then stone made smooth by the passage of many waters across long years. And the ground was dried by the strength of the wind. The waters rose up on either side, climbing and holding fast, standing as walls. And between those walls there opened a broad way of dry ground, cutting straight across the floor of the sea unto the farther shore.

And the people went forward. Thousands upon thousands. Families bore their children in their arms. Men led their beasts by rope. Women walked bearing burdens upon their shoulders. The wind pressed hard upon their garments and their hair. Their sandals fell upon solid earth where but hours before the fish had moved in the deep. The walls of water stood upon both sides of them — dark, heavy, and still. Spray rose from the crests of those walls and drifted, catching what little light yet lingered in the heavens. And the sound of the sea held in its place was deep and constant, a low trembling felt within the chest.

They passed across the whole breadth of it. Every tribe, every household, every beast. The path was broad enough for all. Children looked upward at the water and took hold upon the hands of their fathers and mothers with a tighter grip. The ground did not give way beneath their feet.

Now behind them, the Egyptians beheld the open way. And the commanders of Pharaoh gave the word. The chariots moved forward upon the dry seabed. The horses were driven hard. Horsemen followed after them. The full might of the army of Egypt entered into the corridor between the walls of water — iron-rimmed wheels turning swiftly, spears held upright, and armour catching the faint light. They were resolved to close the distance.

Then the Lord looked down upon the army of Egypt through the pillar of fire and of cloud, and he cast them into great confusion.

The wheels of the chariots seized fast. Some locked and would not turn. Others broke asunder and fell away from the axles. The heavy frames of wood dropped and scraped upon the ground. Horses pulled sidelong, caught in their harness. Riders were thrown down. The ordered ranks broke apart. Chariot struck against chariot. Soldiers cried aloud. The ground which had been firm and dry now dragged upon every wheel and every hoof.

And the Egyptians called one to another, and their voices carried across the seabed, sharp with terror.

They said among themselves that the Lord made war for Israel, and that they must flee from before that people's face.

They sought to turn back. Chariots swung wide and were jammed fast. Horses reared and kicked against the broken frames. The army that had charged forth in close array was now a disordered mass of wood and iron and men striving to move in every direction at once.

And the Lord spake unto Moses, saying that he should stretch forth his hand over the sea, that the waters might return upon the Egyptians and upon their chariots and upon their horsemen.

And Moses stood upon the far shore. The first grey light of the dawn was rising behind him. He stretched forth his hand over the sea.

And the walls moved. The water upon the right hand leaned inward. The water upon the left hand leaned inward. The sea returned unto its full strength. It came not by slow measure but all at once, a great rushing from both sides, meeting together in the midst with a sound as of a mountain rent in two. The water covered the chariots first. Then the horsemen. Then those who fought on foot. The current drew all things down and under — men, horses, iron, wood. The surface churned white for a moment, and then was still.

And the sea lay flat once more. The wind ceased. The morning light spread abroad upon the waters, and the surface gave back the likeness of the pale sky.

Not one soldier of Egypt remained. Not one chariot. Not one horse. The whole army of Pharaoh that had followed after Israel into the sea was no more.

Along the far shore, the bodies of Egyptian soldiers began to wash against the sand. Armour glinted in the shallows. Broken wood from chariot frames turned slowly in the low waves.

And the children of Israel stood upon dry ground and looked out across the sea. They beheld the great thing which the Lord had wrought against Egypt. They fell upon their knees. They bowed their faces unto the earth.

The morning was still. The water lapped softly against the shore. Pieces of harness and splintered wheels rocked in the shallows at the edge of the water.
