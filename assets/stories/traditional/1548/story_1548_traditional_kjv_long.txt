On the morrow, about the noon hour, the men sent from Cornelius were yet upon the way. They had set forth the day before, journeying northward along the coastal plain, past fields and small villages, past wells where wayfarers paused to drink. Now drew they nigh unto the city of Joppa. The sun stood high. The air was hot, and the air was still. Dust clung to the hems of their tunics and to the leather of their sandals.

Then Peter went up upon the housetop to pray, and it was the sixth hour. The roof lay open to the sky, and beneath him the narrow streets of Joppa ran down toward the sea. The clay tiles were warm beneath his feet. A faint wind came in off the water, bearing the savour of salt and of wet rope from the harbour. Gulls cried somewhere out above the boats. And from the courts of the city below, the sounds of the noon hour came up unto him — a woman calling to her child, the bleating of a goat, the steady knock of a hammer upon wood.

He was sore an hungered. The household was preparing the midday meal in the rooms beneath him, and the smell of bread and of oil rose up through the house. He waited for the food to be made ready. But while they yet made ready, there fell a trance upon him.

He beheld the heaven opened above him. And lo, somewhat descended — a great vessel coming down slowly, as a sheet held at the four corners by hands unseen. It lowered toward the earth, swaying a little in the air, the cloth rippling at its edges. And as it drew near, Peter saw what was within.

Every manner of creature. Four-footed beasts of the earth. Wild things with matted hair and bright eyes. Creeping things that go upon the ground. Fowls of the air, with wings folded and unfolding in the folds of the cloth. All of them stirring, gathered together. Beasts which the law alloweth and beasts which the law forbiddeth, set side by side, pressed close, none parted from his fellow.

And there came a voice unto him. Clear and plain. "Rise, Peter; slay and eat."

Peter answered straightway. "Not so, Lord; for nothing common or unclean hath ever entered into my mouth."

The voice came again the second time. The same tone. The same certainty. That which God hath made clean, let not him call common.

Peter looked upon the sheet and the creatures therein. He moved not. The voice had reproved him. He had refused, and the voice had answered his refusal.

This thing was done three times. Thrice was the vessel let down before him. Thrice came the exchange — the command, the refusal, the reproof. Then was the vessel drawn up again into the heaven, rising the way it had come, the corners gathering together, the cloth lifted away. The heavens closed above it. And Peter was left alone upon the housetop.

The sun stood yet overhead. The tiles were yet warm beneath his hands. The wind yet bore up the savour of salt from the harbour, and the gulls yet cried above the boats. Peter sat there, pressing his hands against his knees, his eyes fixed upon the empty sky where the sheet had been. He rose not up. Neither called he to any man in the house.

And while he yet pondered, there was a stirring below at the street.

The men of Cornelius were come. They had journeyed from Caesarea, asking the way at every village, and now stood they before the gate of Simon the tanner's house. Three men were they. The savour of the tannery hung about them — hides and lime and the labour of the trade. They called out from the entrance, asking whether one named Simon, who was surnamed Peter, lodged within.

Peter was yet upon the roof, yet bound up in the vision, when the Spirit spake unto him. The words were plain. "Behold, three men seek thee. Arise, get thee down, and go with them, doubting nothing; for I have sent them."

Peter arose. He brushed the dust from his hands. He crossed the housetop and went down through the dwelling, past the chambers where the midday meal was being set forth, past the servants of the household moving in the cool of the lower floor. He came unto the gate. He beheld the three men standing in the street, dusty from the way, their faces weary, waiting. He looked upon them, and they looked upon him.

"Behold, I am he whom ye seek," said he. "Wherefore are ye come?"

And they told him. Their master was Cornelius, a centurion stationed in Caesarea, a man that gave alms to the people and prayed unto God alway, well reported of among all the nation of the Jews. An holy angel had appeared unto him in his house and had bidden him send for Peter — to bring him unto the house of Cornelius, that he might hear the words Peter should speak.

Peter hearkened unto them. He answered not at once. These were men of the Gentiles, servants of a Roman officer, standing at the gate of a Jewish house. The street was quiet about them. A neighbour passed by, and looked upon the company, and went his way. And Peter turned and looked back over his shoulder, up toward the housetop where the open sky yet held the last of the noon light — a sheet full of every manner of beast, the clean and the unclean together, and a voice saying unto him, Call thou not common what God hath cleansed.

He called the men within. He brought them through the gate, into the court, into the house of Simon the tanner. He gave them lodging for the night. They did eat of the meal that had been made ready. They washed the dust from their feet. They took their rest. The house of Simon the tanner held them all beneath one roof — the Jewish believers and the three Gentile wayfarers — and the day closed about them.

On the morrow Peter made him ready and went forth with them. Certain of the brethren from Joppa went also, walking at his side. The gate of the house was opened, and the company moved together through it, and out upon the road that runneth northward along the coast unto Caesarea. The sun was up. A pale light lay over the fields, and over the sea upon their left hand. The way stretched on before them, the dust rising beneath their feet, and Peter walked with the men Cornelius had sent, and the brethren of Joppa kept pace at his side.
