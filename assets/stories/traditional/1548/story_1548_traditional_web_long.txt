The next day, around noon, the men sent from Cornelius were still traveling. They had set out the day before, moving north along the coastal plain, past fields and small villages, past wells where travelers stopped to drink. Now they were drawing near to the city of Joppa. The sun was high. The air was hot and still. Dust clung to the hems of their tunics and to the leather of their sandals.

Peter went up to the flat roof of the house to pray. It was the sixth hour. The rooftop was open to the sky, and below him the narrow streets of Joppa stretched toward the sea. The clay tiles were warm beneath his feet. A faint wind came off the water, carrying the smell of salt and wet rope from the harbor. Gulls cried somewhere out over the boats. Down in the courtyards of the city, the sounds of the noon hour rose up — a woman calling to a child, the bleat of a goat, the steady knock of a hammer on wood.

He was very hungry. The household was preparing the midday meal downstairs, and the smell of bread and cooking oil drifted up through the house. He waited for the food to be ready. But while they were still making things ready, a trance fell over him.

He saw the sky open above him. Something came down — a large object descending slowly, like a great sheet held at its four corners by invisible hands. It lowered toward the earth, swaying slightly in the air, the cloth rippling at its edges. As it came closer Peter could see what was inside.

Every kind of creature. Four-footed beasts of the earth. Wild animals with matted hair and bright eyes. Things that crept along the ground. Birds of the air, their wings folded and unfolding in the folds of the cloth. All of them moving, gathered together. Animals that the law permitted and animals that it did not, side by side, pressed close, none of them separated from the others.

A voice spoke to him. Clear and direct. "Rise, Peter. Kill and eat."

Peter answered immediately. "Not so, Lord. I have never eaten anything common or unclean."

The voice came again, a second time. The same tone. The same certainty. "What God has cleansed, do not call common."

Peter stared at the sheet and the creatures within it. He did not move. The voice had corrected him. He had refused, and the voice had answered his refusal.

This happened three times. Three times the sheet was presented before him. Three times the exchange took place — the command, the refusal, the correction. Then the vessel was pulled back up into the sky, rising the way it had come, the corners drawing together, the cloth lifting away. The heavens closed above it. Peter was alone on the rooftop.

The sun was still overhead. The tiles were still warm under his hands. The wind still carried the smell of salt from the harbor, and the gulls still cried over the boats. Peter sat there, pressing his hands against his knees, his eyes fixed on the empty sky where the sheet had been. He did not stand. He did not call out to anyone in the house.

While he was still thinking, something was happening below at the street level.

The men from Cornelius had arrived. They had traveled from Caesarea, asking directions at every village along the way, and now they stood before the gate of Simon the tanner's house. There were three of them. The smell of the tannery hung in the air around them — hides and lime and the work of the trade. They called out from the entrance, asking whether a man named Simon, who was also called Peter, was staying there.

Peter was still on the roof, still occupied with the vision, when the Spirit spoke to him. The words were plain. "Three men are looking for you. Get up. Go down to them. Go with them without hesitation, for I have sent them."

Peter stood. He brushed the dust from his hands. He crossed the rooftop and went down through the house, past the rooms where the midday meal was being laid out, past the household servants moving in the cool of the lower floor. He came to the gate. He saw the three men standing there in the street, dusty from the road, their faces tired, waiting. He looked at them, and they looked at him.

"I am the one you are looking for," he said. "Why have you come?"

They told him. Their master was Cornelius, a centurion stationed in Caesarea. "He gives alms to the people and prays to God always," they said. "He is well spoken of by the whole Jewish nation." A holy angel had appeared to him in his house and had instructed him to send for Peter — to bring him to Cornelius's house, so that Cornelius could hear whatever Peter had to say.

Peter listened to them. He did not answer right away. These were Gentile men, servants of a Roman officer, standing at the gate of a Jewish home. The street was quiet around them. A neighbor passed by and glanced at the group and walked on. Peter looked back over his shoulder, up toward the rooftop where the open sky still held the last of the noon light — a sheet full of every kind of animal, clean and unclean together, and a voice telling him not to call common what God had cleansed.

He called the men inside. He brought them through the gate, into the courtyard, into the house of Simon the tanner. He gave them lodging for the night. They ate the meal that had been prepared. They washed the dust from their feet. They rested. The house of Simon the tanner held them all under one roof — the Jewish believers and the three Gentile travelers — and the day closed around them.

The next morning Peter got ready and went out with them. Several of the believers from Joppa came along, walking beside him. The gate of the house opened, and the group moved together through it and out onto the road that led north along the coast toward Caesarea. The sun was up. A pale light lay over the fields and over the sea to their left. The road stretched ahead of them, dust rising under their feet, and Peter walked with the men Cornelius had sent, and the brothers from Joppa kept pace alongside.
