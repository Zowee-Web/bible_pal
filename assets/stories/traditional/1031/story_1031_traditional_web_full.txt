The whole congregation of Israel set out from a place called Elim and entered the wilderness of Sin. It lay between Elim and Sinai, a wide stretch of dry, empty land. Rocky ground stretched in every direction under a blazing sky. The sun beat down without mercy. There was no shade. No green fields. No markets with bread and fruit stacked in baskets.

It was the fifteenth day of the second month after they had left Egypt. About six weeks of traveling. And the people were hungry.

The whole community grumbled against Moses and Aaron. The complaints rose from every tent and every campfire.

"If only we had died by the Lord's hand in Egypt!" the people said. Their voices were angry and afraid. "There we sat by pots of meat. There we ate bread until we were full. But you have brought us out into this wilderness to kill this whole assembly with hunger."

It was a hard thing to hear. Moses and Aaron had not chosen to lead the people into the desert on their own. God had sent them. But the people were scared, and scared people say sharp things.

Then the Lord spoke to Moses. "I am going to rain bread from heaven for you. The people shall go out and gather a day's portion every day, so that I may test them, whether they will walk in my instructions or not."

Rain bread. From heaven. In the middle of the desert, where nothing grew, God was going to send food falling from the sky.

God added a special instruction for the sixth day. On that day, the people would gather twice as much as they gathered on other days.

Moses and Aaron spoke to the people. "In the evening," they said, "you shall know that it was the Lord who brought you out of the land of Egypt. And in the morning, you shall see the glory of the Lord, because he has heard your grumbling against him."

Then Moses added something important. "What are we? Your grumbling is not against us. It is against the Lord."

Moses told Aaron to speak to the whole congregation. "Come near before the Lord," Aaron said, "for he has heard your grumbling."

As Aaron was speaking, the people turned and looked out toward the wilderness. And there, in the cloud that traveled with them, the glory of the Lord appeared. Bright and shining, filling the sky.

The Lord spoke to Moses again. "I have heard the grumbling of the children of Israel. Tell them this: 'At twilight you shall eat meat, and in the morning you shall be filled with bread. Then you shall know that I am the Lord your God.'"

That evening, quail came. The small brown birds flew in from somewhere beyond the horizon and settled on the camp. They covered the ground, fluttering and resting everywhere — around the tents, between the cooking fires, across the open spaces. The people gathered them and had meat for supper.

In the morning, when the dew had lifted from the ground, something else appeared. Thin flakes lay on the surface of the desert. They were small and fine, almost like frost or tiny seeds scattered on the rocky ground. They were white and delicate, covering the earth around the camp.

The people came out of their tents and looked. They bent down and picked up the flakes, turning them over in their fingers. They had never seen anything like this before.

"What is it?" they asked each other. In Hebrew, the words sounded like "man hu." And that is how it got its name — manna.

Moses told them, "It is the bread which the Lord has given you to eat. This is what the Lord has commanded: each person gather as much as he needs. Take an omer for each person according to the number of people in your tent."

An omer was a small measure, about enough to fill a large bowl. One omer per person.

The people of Israel went out and gathered. Some worked quickly and gathered a lot. Some moved more slowly and gathered less. Families with many children gathered more. Small families gathered less.

But when they brought the manna back to their tents and measured it, something remarkable happened. The person who had gathered a lot measured it out, and it came to exactly one omer per person. No extra. The person who had gathered only a little measured it out, and it was also exactly one omer per person. No shortage.

Everyone had exactly what they needed. Not too much. Not too little. Just right.

The people stood in the morning light, holding bowls of manna, looking at each other in wonder. God had fed them. In the middle of a desert where nothing grew and there was no food to be found, bread had appeared on the ground like dew. And every single person had exactly enough.
