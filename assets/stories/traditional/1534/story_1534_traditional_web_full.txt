The word of the LORD came to Elijah. Arise. Go to Zarephath, which belongs to Sidon, and stay there. I have commanded a widow there to provide for you.

So Elijah rose and went. The land was parched. Dust hung over the roads. The brook Cherith had dried behind him, its stones bare and white in the sun. There was no rain anywhere in Israel and no sign of it coming. He walked north toward the coast, toward Sidon, toward a town he did not know and a woman he had never met.

He came to Zarephath. The town sat near the sea, but the drought had reached even here. The ground around the gate was hard and pale. Weeds grew in brittle clusters along the walls. Elijah entered through the gate.

A woman was there. She was stooped over, reaching for sticks on the ground. Her clothes hung loose on her frame. She picked up one stick and then another, adding them to a small pile held against her chest with one arm. Two sticks. That was all she seemed to be after. Her movements were slow and careful, the way a person moves when they have no strength to spare.

Elijah called to her. "Please, bring me a little water in a cup so I can drink."

The woman straightened. She looked at him — a stranger, dusty from the road, standing inside the gate. She said nothing. She turned and started walking toward the well.

Before she reached it, Elijah called again. "Please bring me a piece of bread in your hand too."

She stopped. She stood still for a moment with her back to him. Then she turned around. The sticks were still pressed against her side. Her face was drawn, the skin tight around her jaw.

"As the LORD your God lives," she said, "I do not have a cake of bread. I have only a handful of flour in a jar and a little oil in a jug." She lifted the two sticks slightly. "I am gathering these so I can go home and prepare it for myself and my son. We will eat it. And then we will die."

She said this plainly. There was no begging in it, no tears. She was a woman describing the end of her provisions and the end of everything after that. The drought had taken it all. A handful of flour. A swallow of oil. One last meal for two people. She had come outside to gather the fuel for it.

Elijah looked at her. "Do not be afraid," he said.

She waited.

"Go home and do as you have said. But first, make a small cake from what you have and bring it out to me. After that, make something for yourself and your son."

The woman did not move.

"For the LORD, the God of Israel, says this," Elijah continued. "The jar of flour will not be used up. The jug of oil will not run dry. Not until the day the LORD sends rain on the face of the land."

The gate was quiet. Wind came through the opening and pushed dust along the ground. The woman held her two sticks. She looked at Elijah's face. She looked down at the sticks. She turned and walked back toward her house.

She went inside. She set the sticks down. She took the jar of flour from its place and tipped it. A handful of flour slid out into the bowl. She took the jug of oil and poured. A thin line of oil ran over the flour. She mixed it. She shaped the dough with her hands and laid the small cake on the fire. The sticks burned beneath it.

When it was done she brought the cake to Elijah. He ate.

Then she went back inside and made food for herself and her son. They ate.

The next morning the woman went to the jar and reached inside. Her hand came up with flour in it. She tilted the jug and oil poured out. She baked again. They ate again.

And so it went. Day after day. She and Elijah and her household ate for many days. The drought continued. The sky stayed empty and white. No rain fell on the fields around Zarephath or anywhere in the land. But the jar of flour did not empty. The jug of oil did not go dry.

Each morning the woman reached into the jar. Each morning there was flour against her fingers. Each time she lifted the jug, oil ran out in a slow, steady line. The jar sat in its place on the shelf. The jug sat beside it. Both of them held what they had held the day before, according to the word of the LORD spoken through Elijah.
