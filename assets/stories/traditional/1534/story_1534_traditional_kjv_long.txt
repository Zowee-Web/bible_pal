The word of the LORD came unto Elijah, saying, Arise. Get thee to Zarephath, which pertaineth to Sidon, and there abide. Behold, I have commanded a widow in that place to sustain thee.

So Elijah arose and went. The land was dry, and the dust hung upon the road and settled upon his garments and in the creases of his hands. Behind him the brook Cherith was failed, its stones lying bare and white in the sun, and the mud broken into plates that curled up at the edges. The ravens that had fed him there were gone. Northward he walked, toward the sea, toward Sidon, toward a town he knew not, and a woman he had never seen.

Long was the road. The fields he passed were the colour of straw. The olive trees stood with their leaves shut tight, holding what little water remained within them. He passed by wells where the people stood with empty vessels and turned away empty still. No man greeted him upon the way. The sun moved across the heavens, and the heat lay heavy upon the back of his neck.

And he came to Zarephath. The town sat near unto the sea, yet the drought had reached even thither. The ground about the gate was hard and pale. Brittle weeds clung along the walls, and where the beasts had been tied the earth was scuffed and bare. A goat lay in a narrow slip of shade with its ribs showing. And Elijah entered in at the gate.

And, behold, a woman was there. She was bowed down, gathering sticks from the ground. Her garments hung loose upon her, and the cloth upon her shoulder had been mended more than once. She took up one stick, and then another, and gathered them against her breast with one arm. Two sticks. That was all she sought. Her movements were slow and careful, as one moveth who hath no strength to spare. She stooped. She rose. She stooped again.

Then called Elijah unto her, saying, I pray thee, fetch me a little water in a vessel, that I may drink.

The woman straightened. She looked upon him — a stranger, dusty from the way, standing within the gate. She answered him nothing. And after a moment she turned and set out toward the well. Her sandals scraped softly upon the dry stones of the path.

Before she came thither, Elijah called yet again. I pray thee, bring me also a morsel of bread in thine hand.

And she stopped. She stood still a long while with her back unto him, and her shoulders rose and fell but once. Then she turned about. The sticks were yet pressed against her side. Her face was drawn, the skin tight along the jaw, and beneath each cheekbone there was a hollow.

As the LORD thy God liveth, she said, I have not a cake of bread. I have but an handful of meal in a jar, and a little oil in a cruse. And she lifted the two sticks a little. I am gathering these, that I may go in and dress it for me and my son. We shall eat it. And then we shall die.

Plainly she spake it. There was no begging in her voice, nor any tears. She was a woman telling out the end of her store, and the end of all things after. The drought had taken it every whit. An handful of meal. A swallow of oil. One last meal for two souls. And she had come forth to gather the fuel thereof.

Elijah looked upon her a long while. The wind stirred a loose piece of her veil against her cheek.

Fear not, he said.

She waited.

Go, and do as thou hast said. But make me thereof a little cake first, and bring it forth unto me. And afterward make for thee and for thy son.

The woman moved not. Her grip upon the sticks did not change.

For thus saith the LORD, the God of Israel, said Elijah. The jar of meal shall not be spent. The cruse of oil shall not fail. Not until the day that the LORD sendeth rain upon the face of the earth.

The gate was still. Beyond the wall a child cried out, and was answered. The wind came in through the opening and pushed the dust along the ground in low ribbons. The woman held her two sticks. She looked upon the face of Elijah. She looked down upon the sticks. She turned and went back toward her house. Her steps were the same slow steps as before.

She went in. The room was dim and cool after the glare of the street. She laid the sticks beside the small clay hearth. She took down the jar of meal from its place upon the shelf, and tipped it. An handful of meal slid forth into the bowl, pale against the dark clay. She took the cruse of oil and poured. A thin line of oil ran over the meal and gathered. She mingled it with her fingers. She shaped the dough in her hands, pressing it flat against her palm, and laid the little cake upon the fire. The sticks took the flame beneath it. And the room was filled with the smell of baking bread.

When it was done she lifted the cake from the fire and bare it out through the doorway. Elijah was waiting where she had left him. She held it forth. He took it, and did eat.

Then went she in again. She put her hand into the jar. Her fingers came up dusted with meal. She tipped the cruse. The oil ran out. She made food for herself and for her son, and they sat together and did eat. The boy ate slowly at the first, and then more quickly. She watched him.

On the morrow the woman went unto the jar and put in her hand. It came up with meal upon it, as before. She tipped the cruse, and the oil poured out in the same slow line. She baked again. They ate again. And Elijah did eat with them.

And so it was. Day after day. She and Elijah and her household did eat many days. Without, the drought went on. The heavens stayed empty and white. The well sank lower. The fields about Zarephath brake open in long seams, and no rain fell upon them, nor upon any place in the land. But within the little house, the jar of meal was not spent. The cruse of oil did not fail.

Every morning the woman put her hand into the jar. Every morning there was meal against her fingers, soft and dry and ready. Every time she lifted the cruse, the oil ran out in a slow and steady line over the lip and down into the bowl. The jar sat in its place upon the shelf. The cruse sat beside it. And both of them held that which they had held the day before, according to the word of the LORD spoken by the mouth of Elijah.
