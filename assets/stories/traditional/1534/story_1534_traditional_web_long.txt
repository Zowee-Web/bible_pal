The word of the LORD came to Elijah. Arise. Go to Zarephath, which belongs to Sidon, and stay there. I have commanded a widow there to provide for you.

So Elijah rose and went. The land was parched. Dust hung over the roads and settled on his clothes and in the creases of his hands. The brook Cherith had dried behind him, its stones bare and white in the sun, the mud cracked into plates that lifted at the edges. The ravens that had brought him bread there were gone. He walked north toward the coast, toward Sidon, toward a town he did not know and a woman he had never met.

The road was long. Fields he passed were the color of straw. Olive trees stood with leaves curled tight, holding what little water remained in them. He passed wells where people stood with empty jars and turned away again. No one greeted him on the road. The sun moved across the sky and the heat pressed down on the back of his neck.

He came to Zarephath. The town sat near the sea, but the drought had reached even here. The ground around the gate was hard and pale. Weeds grew in brittle clusters along the walls, and where animals had once been tied, the earth was scuffed and bare. A goat lay in a thin slice of shade with its ribs showing. Elijah entered through the gate.

A woman was there. She was stooped over, reaching for sticks on the ground. Her clothes hung loose on her frame, and the cloth at her shoulder had been mended more than once. She picked up one stick and then another, adding them to a small pile held against her chest with one arm. Two sticks. That was all she seemed to be after. Her movements were slow and careful, the way a person moves when they have no strength to spare. She bent. She straightened. She bent again.

Elijah called to her. "Please, bring me a little water in a cup so I can drink."

The woman straightened. She looked at him — a stranger, dusty from the road, standing inside the gate. She said nothing. After a moment she turned and started walking toward the well. Her sandals scraped softly against the dry stones of the path.

Before she reached it, Elijah called again. "Please bring me a piece of bread in your hand too."

She stopped. She stood still for a long moment with her back to him, her shoulders lifting and falling once. Then she turned around. The sticks were still pressed against her side. Her face was drawn, the skin tight around her jaw, and there was a hollow beneath each cheekbone.

"As the LORD your God lives," she said, "I do not have a cake of bread. I have only a handful of flour in a jar and a little oil in a jug." She lifted the two sticks slightly. "I am gathering these so I can go home and prepare it for myself and my son. We will eat it. And then we will die."

She said this plainly. There was no begging in it, no tears. She was a woman describing the end of her provisions and the end of everything after that. The drought had taken it all. A handful of flour. A swallow of oil. One last meal for two people. She had come outside to gather the fuel for it.

Elijah looked at her for a long moment. The wind moved a loose piece of her head covering against her cheek.

"Do not be afraid," he said.

She waited.

"Go home and do as you have said. But first, make a small cake from what you have and bring it out to me. After that, make something for yourself and your son."

The woman did not move. Her grip on the sticks did not change.

"For the LORD, the God of Israel, says this," Elijah continued. "The jar of flour will not be used up. The jug of oil will not run dry. Not until the day the LORD sends rain on the face of the land."

The gate was quiet. Somewhere beyond the wall a child called out and was answered. Wind came through the opening and pushed dust along the ground in low ribbons. The woman held her two sticks. She looked at Elijah's face. She looked down at the sticks. She turned and walked back toward her house. Her steps were the same slow steps as before.

She went inside. The room was dim and cool after the glare of the street. She set the sticks down beside the small clay hearth. She took the jar of flour from its place on the shelf and tipped it. A handful of flour slid out into the bowl, pale against the dark clay. She took the jug of oil and poured. A thin line of oil ran over the flour and pooled. She mixed it with her fingers. She shaped the dough with her hands, pressing it flat against her palm, and laid the small cake on the fire. The sticks caught and burned beneath it. The room filled with the smell of baking bread.

When it was done she lifted the cake from the fire and carried it out through the doorway. Elijah was waiting where she had left him. She held it out. He took it and ate.

Then she went back inside. She reached into the jar again. Her fingers came up dusted with flour. She tilted the jug. Oil ran out. She made food for herself and her son, and they sat together and ate. The boy ate slowly at first and then more quickly. She watched him.

The next morning the woman went to the jar and reached inside. Her hand came up with flour in it, the same as before. She tilted the jug and oil poured out in the same slow line. She baked again. They ate again. Elijah ate with them.

And so it went. Day after day. She and Elijah and her household ate for many days. Outside, the drought continued. The sky stayed empty and white. The well lowered. The fields around Zarephath cracked open in long seams, and no rain fell on them or anywhere in the land. But inside the small house, the jar of flour did not empty. The jug of oil did not go dry.

Each morning the woman reached into the jar. Each morning there was flour against her fingers, soft and dry and ready. Each time she lifted the jug, oil ran out in a slow, steady line over the lip and down into the bowl. The jar sat in its place on the shelf. The jug sat beside it. Both of them held what they had held the day before, according to the word of the LORD spoken through Elijah.
