Esau was coming, and four hundred men with him, and Jacob had only the time it took them to close the distance. He set his household in order against that meeting — the handmaids and their children first, then Leah with hers, and Rachel with Joseph kept back to the last — and then went on ahead of them all. Seven times he bowed himself to the ground as he came, and only when the last bow was made did his brother close the gap between them. Esau ran to him, and took him in his arms, and kissed him; and the two of them wept.

What Esau found when he lifted his eyes was a whole company of women and children, and he asked whose they were. They were the children God had granted his servant, Jacob told him; and one household after another came forward and bowed. And the herds Esau had passed on the road — what were those? Sent ahead, said Jacob, to find grace in his lord's sight. But Esau wanted none of it. I have enough, my brother, he said; keep what is thine. Yet Jacob pressed the gift on him, for to be received by his brother's face was to him like seeing the face of God; and he urged him until Esau took it.

Then they parted, for Jacob would not be hurried. His brother offered to lead on together, or to leave men behind, but the flocks had their young and the children were small, and to drive them hard even a day would kill them; let his lord go ahead to Seir, and he would follow at the pace the little ones could bear. So Esau turned back that day toward Seir, while Jacob journeyed the other way — to Succoth, where he built himself a house and made booths for his cattle, and gave the place its name; and on from there to Shalem, a city of Shechem in the land of Canaan. There he pitched his tent, bought the ground he camped on from the children of Hamor, and raised an altar, and called it El-elohe-Israel — God, the God of Israel.
