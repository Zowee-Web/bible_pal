The room was large, with stone walls and high windows that let in the dry Egyptian light. Linen curtains hung near the doorway. Joseph stood at the head of the room, dressed in the fine garments of his office — a gold collar at his throat, rings on his fingers, his dark hair cut in the Egyptian manner. Before him stood his brothers, clustered together, their travel-worn robes dusty from the road, their sandals cracked and pale with desert grit.

Joseph's jaw tightened. His chest rose and fell visibly. He turned toward the Egyptian attendants — the cup-bearer standing at his left, the scribes near the far wall, the servants positioned along the edges of the room — and his voice came out sharp and loud.

"Everyone leave me!"

The servants looked at one another. Then they moved, quickly, sandals scuffing against the stone floor, linen rustling as they passed through the doorway. The scribes gathered their instruments. The last attendant pulled the heavy curtain shut behind him. The room was suddenly quiet except for the breathing of the men who remained.

Joseph stood alone before his brothers. No Egyptian remained to witness what came next.

Then the sound broke from him — a loud, ragged weeping that filled the stone chamber. It was not restrained. It was not quiet. The sound carried through the walls, past the curtain, into the corridor beyond, where the Egyptian household staff paused and listened. The sound reached even to Pharaoh's house, and word began to pass among the servants there.

His brothers did not move. They stood where they were, shoulders rigid, hands still at their sides.

Joseph spoke through the weeping. His voice was rough, cracking, but the words came in their own language — the tongue of Canaan, the tongue of their father's tents.

"I am Joseph."

The brothers stared. No one answered.

"Is my father still alive?"

Silence held the room. The brothers' faces were visible in the light from the high windows — mouths open, bodies drawn back, feet shifting on the stone. Not one of them spoke a word. They could not answer him.

Joseph lifted his hand toward them. "Come near to me, please."

They came closer, slowly, their sandals dragging on the floor. They stopped a few paces from him. His face was wet, streaked, and close enough now for them to see clearly — the Egyptian paint around his eyes, the familiar shape of his jaw beneath it.

"I am Joseph, your brother, whom you sold into Egypt."

The words hung in the still air. Some of the brothers lowered their eyes. Others kept staring, their lips pressed shut.

"Now don't be grieved, and don't be angry with yourselves, that you sold me here," Joseph said. His voice steadied as he spoke. "For God sent me before you to preserve life. For these two years the famine has been in the land, and there are yet five years in which there will be no plowing and no harvest. God sent me before you to preserve for you a remnant in the earth, and to save you alive by a great deliverance."

He paused. His hand was still raised, open, palm toward them.

"So now it wasn't you who sent me here, but God. He has made me a father to Pharaoh, lord of all his house, and ruler over all the land of Egypt."

Joseph's voice grew louder, more urgent. He stepped forward.

"Hurry, and go up to my father, and tell him, 'This is what your son Joseph says: God has made me lord of all Egypt. Come down to me. Don't delay. You will dwell in the land of Goshen, and you will be near to me — you, your children, your children's children, your flocks, your herds, and all that you have. There I will provide for you, for there are yet five years of famine, so that you don't come to poverty — you, your household, and all that you have.'"

He looked from face to face. His eyes moved across the group, resting on each brother in turn.

"Behold, your eyes see, and the eyes of my brother Benjamin, that it is my mouth that speaks to you."

Benjamin stood among them, younger than the rest, and Joseph's gaze stayed on him longest.

"You shall tell my father of all my glory in Egypt, and of all that you have seen. You shall hurry and bring my father down here."

Then Joseph moved. He crossed the remaining distance and put his arms around Benjamin's neck. He wept there, holding his brother, his shoulders shaking. Benjamin wept against him.

After that, Joseph went to each of his brothers. He kissed Reuben. He kissed Simeon. He kissed Levi, Judah, Dan, Naphtali, Gad, Asher, Issachar, Zebulun — one after another, pressing his lips to their cheeks, weeping over each one. His tears left marks on their dusty robes.

Then, at last, his brothers spoke. Their voices rose in the stone room — halting at first, then louder, overlapping. They talked with him there, in the language of their father's house, while the Egyptian household waited outside and the light from the high windows moved slowly across the floor.
