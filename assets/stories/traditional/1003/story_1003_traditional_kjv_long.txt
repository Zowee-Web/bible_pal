Then Joseph could no longer contain himself before all them that stood by him, and he cried aloud, commanding every man to go out from his presence. The voice that issued from his mouth was sharp and sudden, and the servants and the stewards and the men of Egypt who attended him turned where they stood. Some had been carrying platters of silver. Others had stood along the painted walls with their arms folded. At the sound of his cry, they moved — sandals scraping against the stone floor, the rustle of linen garments gathering as they passed through the wide doorway. One by one and then in haste, they departed, until the great hall emptied of every Egyptian soul.

No man of Egypt stood with Joseph when he made himself known unto his brethren. The chamber stretched around them, vast and quiet. Columns of carved stone rose toward the ceiling, and the air carried the faint scent of incense that had burned low upon its stand. The light from the high windows fell in broad shafts across the floor, catching the dust that the departing feet had stirred.

And Joseph wept aloud. The sound of it filled the hall — not the sound of a man weeping softly behind his hand, but a cry that broke open and echoed against the stone. His voice carried beyond the walls, and the Egyptians outside heard it. Word of it reached even unto the house of Pharaoh.

His brethren stood before him. They had not moved. The eleven of them remained where they were, their robes travel-worn, their sandals cracked from the road between Canaan and this place. The bags they had carried for grain lay near their feet. Their hands hung at their sides.

Joseph spoke. His voice, still rough from weeping, came unto them plainly. He said unto them two words: I am Joseph.

The hall held the sound of it. The columns stood unmoved. The incense smoke curled faintly upward in the stillness.

He spoke again, asking whether his father yet lived. But his brethren could not answer him. They stood before his face, and their mouths did not open. Their bodies drew no nearer. Their feet remained planted upon the stone where they had stood, and their faces were turned toward him, but no word came forth from any of them. They were troubled at his presence.

Then Joseph spoke unto them once more, and this time he bade them come near. The words fell gently across the space between them. Come near to me, I pray you, he said. Some among them shifted where they stood. One foot moved, then another. Slowly, with the halting gait of men uncertain upon the ground beneath them, they drew closer. The distance between Joseph and his brethren narrowed, and the light from the high windows fell now upon them all together.

When they had come near, he spoke again. He told them plainly what they already carried in their memory: he was Joseph, their brother, whom they had sold into Egypt. The words passed through the air of that hall, and the brothers stood close enough now to see the lines upon his face, the Egyptian garments upon his body, the rings upon his fingers, the collar of gold about his neck. Close enough to hear the breath that moved through him as he spoke.

He told them not to be grieved, nor angry with themselves, that they had sold him thither. He declared unto them that God had sent him before them to preserve life. He spoke of the famine — that two years it had been in the land already, and that five years yet remained in which there would be neither earing nor harvest. The fields beyond the walls of that palace lay cracked and dry, the irrigation channels low, the granaries of lesser cities already diminished. Joseph spoke of these things as one who had walked among them and counted the stores himself.

He said that God had sent him before them to preserve a posterity in the earth, and to save their lives by a great deliverance. He repeated it, standing among them in that emptied hall, his voice steadying as he spoke: it was not they who sent him hither, but God. God had made him a father to Pharaoh, and lord of all his house, and a ruler throughout all the land of Egypt.

The brothers stood near him and heard these words. The stone floor was cool beneath their feet. The shafts of light had shifted as the sun moved overhead, and the shadows of the columns stretched longer across the ground.

Joseph spoke again, and now his words carried urgency. He commanded them to hasten — to go up unto their father and speak unto him. They were to say that his son Joseph had been made lord over all Egypt, and that their father must come down without tarrying. Joseph laid before them what awaited: they would dwell in the land of Goshen, near unto him. Their flocks and their herds and all that they possessed would be with them. He spoke of Goshen as a place with pasture and water, a land set apart within the borders of Egypt where their father and their households and their cattle might abide.

He told them he would nourish them there, for five years of famine remained, lest they and their households and all that they had come to poverty. The word poverty hung in the air of that palace hall, spoken by a man dressed in the finest linen of Egypt, standing amid columns overlaid with color and gold. He spoke it plainly, as one who named a thing he meant to prevent.

Then Joseph said unto them: Behold, your eyes see, and the eyes of my brother Benjamin, that it is my mouth that speaketh unto you. He gestured as he spoke, and his hands moved in the light. Benjamin stood among the brothers, younger than the rest, and Joseph's gaze rested upon him. The hall was still. No servant entered. No footstep sounded from beyond the doorway.

Joseph charged them further. They were to tell his father of all his glory in Egypt, and of all that they had seen. They were to make haste and bring their father down to this place.

Then Joseph moved. He stepped forward from where he had stood and fell upon the neck of his brother Benjamin. He wept there, his arms around Benjamin, his face pressed against his brother's shoulder. The fabric of Benjamin's garment grew damp beneath Joseph's tears. Benjamin wept upon Joseph's neck likewise, and the two of them stood clasped together in the broad hall while the other brothers stood near and watched.

After a time, Joseph lifted himself from Benjamin and turned to the rest. He kissed all his brethren, every one of them, and wept upon them. He went from one to the next — Reuben, Simeon, Levi, Judah, and the others — and his mouth pressed against their cheeks, and his tears fell upon their garments. The sound of weeping filled that hall again, as it had when the Egyptians first departed. The stone walls received the sound and gave it back.

After that, his brethren talked with him. Their mouths opened at last. Words passed between them — voices overlapping in that great room, the sound of brothers speaking together after long silence. The incense had gone cold upon its stand. The light through the high windows had moved again, falling now upon the far wall. The dust had settled. And in that hall, emptied of every Egyptian, the sons of Israel stood together and spoke, their voices carrying no farther than the stone that enclosed them.
