Then Joseph could no longer contain himself before all them that stood by him, and he cried aloud, commanding every attendant to depart. His voice carried through the chamber, sharp and sudden, and the servants and officials turned from their places and went out, one after another, until the hall stood empty save for Joseph and his brethren. No stranger remained when Joseph made himself known unto them.

He wept. The sound of it rose and filled the stone chamber, unrestrained, a weeping so loud that the Egyptians outside heard it, and the house of Pharaoh heard it also. His body shook where he stood, and his brothers drew no nearer. The sound echoed against the pillars and the high ceiling, carrying beyond the doors and down the corridors where the servants had withdrawn.

He spoke then, and his voice broke through the weeping. He said unto them plainly: I am Joseph.

The brothers stood before him, and not one of them answered. Their mouths did not open. Their feet did not move upon the floor. They stood fixed in their places, their faces turned toward him, and the silence after his words was as heavy as the weeping before it.

He spoke again, asking whether his father yet lived, but still they answered him not. They were troubled at his presence, and their bodies showed it — hands still at their sides, shoulders drawn in, no man stepping forward, no man stepping back.

Then Joseph called to them, bidding them come near. He said it again: Come near to me. And they came, slowly, their sandals scraping against the stone floor, closing the distance between themselves and the ruler of Egypt who now stood before them with a wet face and open hands. When they had drawn close, he told them again who he was — Joseph, their brother, whom they had sold into Egypt.

He lifted his hand as he spoke, and his voice steadied. He told them not to be grieved, nor angry with themselves, for what they had done. God, he declared, had sent him before them to preserve life. He recounted the years aloud: two years the famine had been in the land, and five years yet remained in which there would be neither earing nor harvest. The fields beyond the palace walls lay dry and cracked, the granaries of Egypt alone standing full, and Joseph spoke of these things as one who had walked among them daily.

God sent me before you, he said, to preserve you a posterity in the earth, and to save your lives by a great deliverance. His hand moved as he spoke, gesturing toward the chamber around them, toward the storehouses and the land beyond. He told them plainly: it was not they who had sent him hither, but God. God had made him a father to Pharaoh, and lord of all his house, and ruler throughout all the land of Egypt.

His brothers stood close now, near enough to see the lines upon his face, the Egyptian garments upon his frame, the gold at his neck. Joseph's voice grew urgent. He commanded them to hasten, to go up to his father and carry these words: thus saith thy son Joseph — God hath made me lord of all Egypt. Come down unto me, tarry not.

He spoke of where they would dwell. In the land of Goshen they would be settled, near unto him — they and their children and their children's children, their flocks and their herds and all that they had. There he would nourish them through the remaining five years of famine, lest they and their households come to poverty.

Joseph paused. His eyes moved across the faces of his brothers, one by one. He said that their own eyes could see, and the eyes of his brother Benjamin could see, that it was his own mouth which spoke unto them. No interpreter stood between them now. The Egyptian tongue was set aside, and the words fell from his lips in the language of their father's house.

He charged them further: tell my father of all my glory in Egypt, and of all that ye have seen. Make haste, and bring my father down hither.

Then Joseph moved. He stepped toward Benjamin, his younger brother, the son of his own mother, and fell upon his neck and wept. Benjamin stood beneath the weight of that embrace, and Joseph's weeping sounded again through the empty hall, pressing against the walls. Benjamin wept upon Joseph's neck also, and the two of them stood together, their garments pressed close, their shoulders shaking.

After that, Joseph kissed all his brethren and wept upon them, moving from one to the next. His hands gripped their arms and their shoulders. His tears fell upon their robes. One by one he drew them near, and one by one they received him.

And after that, his brethren talked with him. Their voices rose at last in that chamber — halting at first, then gathering, words passing between them where before there had been only silence and weeping. The sound of their speaking filled the room where moments before no brother had dared open his mouth.
