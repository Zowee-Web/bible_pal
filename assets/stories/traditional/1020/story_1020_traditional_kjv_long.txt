The Lord appeared unto Abraham in the plains of Mamre.

It was the heat of the day. The sun hung at its height, white and fierce. The ground baked beneath it. Dust lay still upon every path. The oaks of Mamre stood broad and ancient, their branches reaching wide, but the shade they gave was thin, scarce enough to cool the earth beneath them. A few goats had pressed themselves into shadow against a low stone wall. An ass stood without motion near the well. Nothing stirred.

Abraham sat in the tent door. He was old. The tent flap hung open, and the air within was no cooler than without. He sat in the thin edge of shade and looked forth across the camp.

He lifted up his eyes and looked, and lo, three men stood by him.

They had not been there a moment before, or perhaps he had not marked their coming across the bright, shimmering ground. They stood in the open, as travellers would — upright, waiting, saying nothing.

Abraham tarried not. He rose from the tent door and ran to meet them. Old as he was, he moved with urgency. His feet raised dust. He reached the men and bowed himself low toward the ground before them.

"My lord," said he, "if now I have found favour in thy sight, pass not away, I pray thee, from thy servant."

His voice bore urgency. In this place, hospitality was no mere courtesy. It was a necessity. Water was scarce. Shade was precious.

"Let a little water be fetched," said Abraham. "Wash your feet, and rest yourselves under the tree. I will fetch a morsel of bread, and comfort ye your hearts. After that ye shall pass on, forasmuch as ye are come to your servant."

The three men answered simply. "So do, as thou hast said."

Abraham turned and hastened into the tent. He found Sarah within, away from the sun. The air inside was dim after the brightness without. He spake quickly.

"Make ready quickly three measures of fine meal. Knead it, and make cakes upon the hearth."

Three measures was no small portion. It was enough to feed far more than three. This was not the morsel of bread Abraham had promised at the door. This was a feast — bountiful, swift, set forth as though a king had come without warning. Abraham offered no explanation. He gave the instruction and turned to go.

He ran unto the herd. The cattle stood in a knot near the far end of the camp, gathered in whatsoever shade the trees afforded. Abraham moved among them, looking with care. He chose a calf — tender, young, and good. He brought it to a young man, a servant, and the servant hasted to dress it. The calf must be slain. The blood let out. The hide taken off. The flesh prepared and set upon the fire. The coals must be rightly laid — not too fierce, not too faint. The roasting would require time, and Abraham saw to it.

Within the tent, Sarah wrought. She measured out the fine meal — the choicest flour, kept for occasions of weight. She poured it out and added water and began to knead. Her hands pressed the dough against the surface, turned it, pressed again. The rhythm was steady. She shaped the cakes — flat and round, each the breadth of a man's hand — and set them upon the heated hearthstones. The smell of bread baking filled the tent and drifted through the open flap into the still air without.

The servant tended the calf over the fire. Fat fell and hissed upon the coals. Smoke rose in a thin column. The flesh turned slowly from red to brown. Abraham gathered butter and milk whilst the cooking went on. He set out all things — the vessels, the provisions, the food as it came ready.

When the calf was dressed and the cakes were finished, Abraham carried the food himself. He set it before the three men under the tree. The butter. The milk. The calf, roasted and tender. The fresh cakes, yet warm from the hearth. All of it laid out with care beneath the broad branches of the oaks of Mamre.

Abraham sat not with his guests. He stood by whilst they did eat. A host stood. A host served. Such was the custom of the land, and Abraham kept it without question. He watched. He attended. If a vessel wanted filling, he filled it. If a dish wanted replenishing, he saw to it.

The men did eat. They ate the cakes Sarah had made. They ate the roasted calf. They drank the milk. The meal was generous — wrought in haste but given with dignity, as though these three strangers were the most honoured guests Abraham had ever received.

When they had done eating, they spake.

"Where is Sarah thy wife?"

The question was direct. Abraham answered it in kind.

"Behold, in the tent."

One of them spake again. His voice was changed now — it bore a weight and sureness the earlier words had not carried.

"I will certainly return unto thee according to the time of life, and lo, Sarah thy wife shall have a son."

The words hung in the hot, still air beneath the oaks.

Sarah was listening. She stood in the tent door, just behind the speaker, where she might hear all things but not easily be seen. She heard the promise clearly.

Now Abraham and Sarah were old. They were both well stricken in age. It had ceased to be with Sarah after the manner of women, and had so ceased many years before. She had borne this truth a long while. Her body had spoken, and the speech had been final. The promise of a child had come before, unto Abraham, more than once — beneath the stars, beside the fire, in the quiet of the tent at night. But the years had passed. Season upon season, the word had stood unfulfilled. And Sarah's body had moved beyond the possibility.

Therefore Sarah laughed within herself.

It was not a laugh that any could hear. It was not uttered aloud. It rose silently within — a swift, unbidden thing, come before thought could master it. Her lips may have stirred. Her eyes may have narrowed. But no sound came forth.

"After I am waxed old," she thought, "shall I have pleasure, my lord being old also?"

The thought was honest. It was not bitter. It was the plain truth as her body understood it. She was old. Abraham was old. The flesh doth what the flesh doth.

But the Lord said unto Abraham, "Wherefore did Sarah laugh?"

The question came without warning. Sarah had not spoken aloud. She had not stirred from the tent door. She had not laughed where any could mark it. Yet the Lord knew.

"Wherefore did she say, Shall I of a surety bear a child, which am old?"

Abraham stood still. The afternoon light fell slantwise through the oaks. The remains of the feast lay upon the ground before the visitors. The air was thick and hot.

Then the Lord spake again.

"Is any thing too hard for the Lord?"

The question bore no anger. It was no rebuke. It was a bidding to consider what manner of God had spoken this promise. This God now asked a single question, and the question answered itself.

"At the time appointed I will return unto thee, according to the time of life, and Sarah shall have a son."

Sarah heard this. Fear moved through her — not the fear of harm, but the fear of being known. She had laughed in silence, in the hidden place within her own breast. And the silence had not concealed it.

She came forward from the tent door. She spake.

"I laughed not."

He said, "Nay, but thou didst laugh."

The words were quiet. They bore no punishment. They bore no wrath. They were a simple correction — gentle, firm, and final. Sarah had laughed, and the Lord had heard. The promise stood fast. The appointed time would come. And that which had seemed beyond all hope beneath the oaks of Mamre on that hot, still afternoon would come to pass, even as the Lord had spoken.

Is any thing too hard for the Lord?
