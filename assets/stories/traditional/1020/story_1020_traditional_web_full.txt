The Lord appeared to Abraham by the oaks of Mamre in the heat of the day. The sun hung white in a pale sky. The ground baked beneath it. The oaks spread their branches wide, but the shade was thin. Animals lay still in whatever shadow they could find. The camp was quiet.

Abraham sat at the entrance of his tent. He lifted his eyes and looked. Three men stood nearby. They had not been there a moment before. They stood as travelers would, waiting.

He did not hesitate. Old as he was, he rose at once and ran from the tent door to meet them. He bowed himself to the ground.

"My lord," he said, "if I have found favor in your sight, please do not pass by your servant. Let a little water be brought. Wash your feet and rest under the tree. I will bring a morsel of bread, that you may refresh your hearts. After that you may go on your way."

They said, "Do as you have said."

Abraham hurried into the tent and found Sarah. "Quickly," he said. "Make ready three measures of fine meal. Knead it and make cakes." Three measures was no small amount. This was a feast, not a morsel. He gave the instruction and left.

He ran to the herd. He chose a calf — tender, young, and good. He gave it to a servant, who hurried to prepare it. The animal had to be slaughtered, the fire built, the meat roasted with care. None of this was quick work.

Inside the tent, Sarah measured out the fine meal. She added water and kneaded the dough with both hands, pressing and folding until it was smooth. She shaped the cakes and set them on the heated hearthstones. The smell of baking bread filled the tent and drifted into the afternoon air.

When everything was ready, Abraham gathered the food himself. He took butter and milk and the calf that had been prepared. He set it before the three men under the tree. The oaks of Mamre spread their branches above them. The shade had deepened as the afternoon wore on.

Abraham did not sit with them. He stood nearby as they ate. A host stood. A host served. The men ate the cakes Sarah had made. They ate the calf, tender and well-prepared. They drank the milk. The meal was generous — prepared in haste but offered with dignity.

When they had eaten, they spoke.

"Where is Sarah your wife?"

"There, in the tent."

One of them spoke again. His voice carried a weight the words alone did not explain.

"I will surely return to you when the season comes around, and Sarah your wife will have a son."

Sarah was listening at the tent door, just behind the speaker. She heard the promise clearly.

Now Abraham and Sarah were old, well advanced in years. The way of women had ceased to be with Sarah long ago. The promise of a child had been spoken before, to Abraham, more than once. But the years had passed, and her body had given its own answer.

She laughed within herself. It was not a laugh anyone could hear. It moved through her silently.

"After I have grown old," she thought, "shall I have pleasure, my lord being old also?"

But the Lord said to Abraham, "Why did Sarah laugh? Why did she say, 'Shall I indeed bear a child, now that I am old?'"

Sarah had not spoken aloud. She had not moved from the tent door. Yet the Lord knew.

"Is anything too hard for the Lord? At the appointed time I will return to you, and Sarah will have a son."

Sarah was afraid — not the fear of danger, but of being known. She had laughed silently, and the silence had not hidden it.

She stepped forward. "I did not laugh," she said.

He said, "No, but you did laugh."

The words were quiet. They were not punishment. They were plain and final. The promise remained. The appointed time would come. And what had seemed impossible under the oaks of Mamre on that hot afternoon would come to pass, just as the Lord had said.
