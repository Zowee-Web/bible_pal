The Lord appeared unto Abraham in the plains of Mamre in the heat of the day. The sun hung white in a pale sky. The ground baked beneath it. The oaks spread their branches wide, but the shade was thin. The beasts lay still in whatsoever shadow they could find. The camp was quiet.

Abraham sat in the tent door. He lifted up his eyes and looked, and lo, three men stood by him. They had not been there a moment before. They stood as travellers would, waiting.

He tarried not. Old as he was, he rose at once and ran from the tent door to meet them. He bowed himself toward the ground.

"My lord," said he, "if now I have found favour in thy sight, pass not away from thy servant. Let a little water be fetched, and wash your feet, and rest yourselves under the tree. I will fetch a morsel of bread, and comfort ye your hearts. After that ye shall pass on."

They said, "So do, as thou hast said."

Abraham hastened into the tent and found Sarah. "Make ready quickly three measures of fine meal," he bade her. "Knead it, and make cakes upon the hearth." Three measures was no small portion. This was a feast, not a morsel. He gave the word and departed.

He ran unto the herd. He chose a calf — tender, young, and good. He gave it unto a servant, who hasted to dress it. The beast must be slain, the fire built, the flesh roasted with care. None of this was swift work.

Within the tent, Sarah measured out the fine meal. She added water and wrought the dough with both hands, pressing and turning it until it was smooth. She shaped the cakes and laid them upon the heated hearthstones. The smell of bread baking filled the tent and drifted forth into the afternoon air.

When all was ready, Abraham gathered the food himself. He took butter and milk and the calf which had been dressed. He set it before the three men under the tree. The oaks of Mamre spread their branches above them. The shade had grown deeper as the afternoon drew on.

Abraham sat not with them. He stood by whilst they did eat. A host stood. A host served. The men ate the cakes. They ate the calf. They drank the milk.

When they had eaten, they spake.

"Where is Sarah thy wife?"

"Behold, in the tent."

One of them spake again. His voice bore a weight the words alone could not explain.

"I will certainly return unto thee according to the time of life, and lo, Sarah thy wife shall have a son."

Sarah heard this in the tent door, just behind the speaker. She heard the promise clearly.

Now Abraham and Sarah were old, well stricken in age. It had ceased to be with Sarah after the manner of women. The promise of a child had been spoken before, unto Abraham, more than once. But the years had continued, and her body had given its own answer.

Therefore Sarah laughed within herself. It was not a laugh that any might hear. It moved through her in silence.

"After I am waxed old," she thought, "shall I have pleasure, my lord being old also?"

But the Lord said unto Abraham, "Wherefore did Sarah laugh, saying, Shall I of a surety bear a child, which am old?"

Sarah had not spoken aloud. She had not moved from the tent door. Yet the Lord knew.

"Is any thing too hard for the Lord? At the time appointed I will return unto thee, and Sarah shall have a son."

Sarah was afraid — not the fear of peril, but of being known. She had laughed in silence, and the silence had not concealed it.

She came forward. "I laughed not," said she.

He said, "Nay, but thou didst laugh."

The words were quiet. They were no punishment. They were plain and sure. The promise stood. The appointed time would come. And that which seemed beyond hope beneath the oaks of Mamre on that hot afternoon would come to pass, even as the Lord had spoken.
