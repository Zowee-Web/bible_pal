In the goodly land of Persia, Esther dwelt in the royal palace of the king, a place full of gold, lofty ceilings, and thick curtains of many colours. She was the queen, though not all knew that she was of the people of God. Mordecai her cousin also dwelt there, not far from the gates of the palace. On a day, a sorrowful and troubling word came. Haman, a man that served king Ahasuerus, had persuaded the king to make a decree that should bring great trouble upon Esther's people. The people were afraid and knew not what to do.

When Mordecai heard the tidings, he rent his clothes, and put on sackcloth with ashes, and went out into the midst of the city, and cried with a loud and a bitter cry. Many people throughout the kingdom mourned likewise. Esther, safe within the palace, heard that Mordecai was grieved and sent her maid to ask what ailed him. Mordecai sent word again unto Esther and told her of the dreadful decree. He besought her to go in unto the king and to make supplication for her people. Esther was sore afraid. She remembered that whosoever should come in unto the king without being called might be in great peril. But Mordecai said unto her, "Who knoweth whether thou art come to the kingdom for such a time as this?"

Esther considered carefully. She bade Mordecai and all her people to fast and not to eat for three days. She promised that she and her maidens would do likewise. After three days, arrayed in her royal apparel, Esther went quietly unto the throne room. The gold did shine, thick curtains hid parts of the chamber, and the air was heavy. Esther drew near, and her heart did beat quickly. The king saw her standing in the inner court, and held out to Esther the golden sceptre that was in his hand. Esther felt a breathless relief, for now she was safe.

King Ahasuerus asked what she would have. She kindly desired that he and Haman would come unto a banquet she had prepared. The king consented, and Haman was lifted up because he had been bidden. At the banquet, the king asked again what she would have. Esther bade them both come to a second banquet on the morrow. Afterward, Haman went home glad of heart. But when he beheld Mordecai at the king's gate, who stood not up, nor moved for him, his joy was gone.

That night, the king could not sleep, and commanded that the chronicles of his kingdom be read aloud. The chronicles told how Mordecai had once helped to save the king. The king inquired whether Mordecai had been honoured, and learned that nothing had been done unto him. On the morrow, Haman came in, purposing to speak against Mordecai. Before he could speak, the king said, "What shall be done unto the man whom the king delighteth to honour?" Haman thought the king meant himself, and proposed a royal procession. The king said, "Make haste, and do even so unto Mordecai!" So Haman must needs honour Mordecai as he had said.

That evening, Esther prepared her second banquet. The king asked her again what she would have, promising to grant it. Esther gently said, "If I have found favour in thy sight, O king, and if it please the king, let my life be given me at my petition, and my people at my request." The king was astonished and asked, "Who is he, and where is he, that durst presume in his heart to do so?" Esther boldly answered, "The adversary and enemy is this wicked Haman!" Then Haman was sore afraid.

King Ahasuerus arose in his wrath and went into the palace garden. Haman besought Esther for his life, but the king returned and saw him troubling Esther. The king gave commandment concerning Haman, and the peril unto Esther's people was stayed. Haman bare the harm he had purposed for others.

Mordecai came in before the king, and the king gave unto Esther all that Haman had possessed. Esther told the king that Mordecai was her cousin, and Mordecai was made great in the palace. The decree that Haman had made was changed, that Esther's people might be safe. They rejoiced exceedingly and gave thanks. Many people throughout all the kingdom learned how kind and brave queen Esther had been, and they kept a feast with joy, for God had delivered them in a wondrous manner.

Esther and Mordecai often remembered that day, being thankful and quietly hopeful for every new morning that dawned.
