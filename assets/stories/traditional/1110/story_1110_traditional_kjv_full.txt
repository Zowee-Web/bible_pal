Jesus led them out as far as to Bethany. They departed the city together — through the narrow streets, past the market stalls and the houses with their flat roofs, out through the eastern gate. The road descended into the Kidron Valley. The streambed was dry. Stones lay scattered along the bottom, bleached white by the sun. They crossed over and began to climb.

The Mount of Olives rose before them. The path was steep and uneven, cut by rain and worn by the feet of pilgrims and merchants and husbandmen for generations. Olive trees stood upon both sides — ancient trunks twisted and thick, their leaves silver in the sunlight. The shade moved in patches upon the ground as the wind stirred the branches.

The disciples walked in a loose company behind Jesus. Their robes swayed with their steps. Their sandals scraped the rock. The air was warm and dry. The smell of wild thyme and sun-heated stone rose from the earth about them. Insects hummed in the low scrub along the roadside. A bird called from somewhere among the olive trees and was answered by another further up the slope.

They climbed higher. Jerusalem fell away behind them. The temple mount was visible first, then the walls, then the full breadth of the city — housetops packed close together, smoke rising from cooking fires, the distant sound of voices and beasts of burden growing faint as the distance grew. The road curved along the side of the hill. The ground levelled briefly, then rose again.

They passed the place called Gethsemane. The garden sat in its grove of ancient olive trees, the trunks dark even in the daylight. They walked past it and tarried not.

The path continued upward. The growth thinned as they gained height. The rock showed through the soil in pale slabs. The wind grew stronger — steady and clean, coming from the east across the wilderness. It pressed against their robes and lifted the hair from their foreheads.

They came unto the crest of the ridge. The land opened on both sides. To the west, Jerusalem spread below them — familiar, golden in the afternoon light. To the east, the slope descended toward Bethany. The village was small — a cluster of stone houses, low garden walls, a few fig trees and palm trees near the road. Beyond Bethany, the land rolled away in brown and green folds toward the Jordan Valley and the wilderness beyond. The Salt Sea glinted in the far distance, a strip of silver between the hills.

Jesus stayed his steps. They gathered about him upon the high ground. The wind blew across the hilltop. The sky was wide and clear above them. The light was strong.

He lifted up his hands.

The disciples stood still. The wind moved. The olive branches swayed below them upon the slopes. Jerusalem sat behind them. Bethany lay before them.

He blessed them.

And it came to pass, while he blessed them, he was parted from them, and carried up into heaven.

The disciples beheld. Their faces were turned upward. Their eyes followed after. The sky was open and fathomless above the ridge — blue fading into blue. The wind blew across their robes and their hair. They stood upon the hilltop, their feet upon the rocky ground, their eyes fastened upon the place where he had been.

The wind continued. The olive trees moved upon the slopes below. The sound of the city was far off.

They worshipped him. They fell upon their knees upon the stone and the dry grass. Their foreheads touched the earth.

Then they rose. They turned back toward Jerusalem. They went with great joy — back over the ridge, down the familiar path, past the garden, across the valley, through the gate. The road they had walked with him they now walked without him. But they mourned not. Their steps were quick. Their faces were lifted.

They went into the temple. They were continually there, praising and blessing God.

The road between the city and Bethany lay quiet behind them. The hilltop stood empty. The wind moved across the ridge where he had lifted up his hands. The olive trees swayed. The sky held nought but light.
