After some days, King Agrippa and Bernice arrived at Caesarea and greeted Festus. They remained there many days, and Festus presented Paul’s case to the king. Festus said that Felix had left a certain man in prison. When Festus had been in Jerusalem, the chief priests and elders of the Jews informed him about Paul and asked for a sentence against him.

Festus told Agrippa that he had answered them according to Roman custom. An accused man was not to be given up to destruction before meeting his accusers face to face and receiving an opportunity to answer the charge. When Paul’s accusers came to Caesarea, Festus did not delay. The next day he sat on the judgment seat and ordered Paul to be brought before him.

The accusers stood up, but according to Festus, they brought none of the charges he had expected. Instead, they raised questions about their own religion and about Jesus, who was dead and whom Paul affirmed to be alive. Festus said he was perplexed about how to investigate these matters. He asked whether Paul would go to Jerusalem to be judged there, but Paul appealed to be kept for the emperor’s decision. Festus then ordered that Paul be held until he could send him to Caesar.

Agrippa told Festus that he wished to hear Paul himself. Festus arranged for him to hear Paul the next day.

Agrippa and Bernice entered the place of hearing with great pomp, accompanied by commanding officers and the city’s principal men. At Festus’s command, Paul was brought in. Festus addressed Agrippa and everyone present. He said that the multitude of the Jews had petitioned him about Paul in Jerusalem and Caesarea, crying that Paul should no longer live.

Yet Festus said he had found that Paul had done nothing worthy of death. Because Paul had appealed to the emperor, Festus had determined to send him. His difficulty was that he had no certain charge to write to his lord. He had therefore brought Paul before the assembly, especially before Agrippa, so that an examination might give him something to write. Festus concluded that it was unreasonable to send a prisoner without specifying the charges against him.
