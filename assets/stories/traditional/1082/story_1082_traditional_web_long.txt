In the sixth month, God sent the angel Gabriel to a town in the hills of Galilee. The town was small and quiet. It was called Nazareth. Stone houses with flat roofs clustered along narrow paths that wound between the slopes. Olive trees grew in crooked rows on the terraces above the village, their silver-green leaves turning in the breeze. Grapevines climbed the low walls between the houses. The air smelled of baking bread and wood smoke and the dry herbs that grew wild in the rocky soil.

It was an ordinary place. A village where people ground their grain and patched their roofs and drew water from the well in the center of town. The kind of place where nothing remarkable had ever happened, and no one expected anything to change.

Somewhere in Nazareth, a young woman named Mary was going about her day. She was pledged to be married to a man named Joseph, from the house of David. She was a virgin — young, quiet, known in the village the way all young women were known: by her family, by her betrothed, by the work she did each day. She carried water. She ground flour. She mended cloth and swept the packed-earth floor of the house where she lived. Her hands were strong from the work, her face browned by the sun. Her life, up until this moment, had been the ordinary shape of a young woman in a country village.

Then the angel came to her.

He appeared in the room where she was. There was no warning — no sound of footsteps, no knock at the door. The air changed. The light changed. Something vast and bright filled the small space, and Gabriel stood before her. He was not like a man, though he had the shape of one. His presence filled the room the way sunlight fills a doorway.

He spoke.

"Rejoice, you highly favored one," he said. "The Lord is with you. Blessed are you among women."

Mary was greatly troubled. Her hands went still. Her breath caught. She did not run. She did not cry out. She stood in the room, surrounded by the things she knew — the grinding stone, the water jar, the folded cloth on the shelf — and tried to understand what kind of greeting this could be. Who would say these words to her. What they could mean. Why her, of all people. Why now. Why here, in this small room, in this small town, on this ordinary afternoon.

The angel saw her fear, and he spoke again.

"Do not be afraid, Mary," he said. "For you have found favor with God."

His voice was steady. The words were kind — not demanding, not harsh. They were spoken the way one speaks to a person who needs to hear something true but cannot quite believe it yet.

"Behold," he continued, "you will conceive in your womb, and bring forth a son. You will call his name Jesus."

Mary listened. She did not move.

"He will be great," Gabriel said. "He will be called the Son of the Most High. The Lord God will give him the throne of his father David, and he will reign over the house of Jacob forever. There will be no end to his kingdom."

The words filled the room. They were too large for the space — too large for the village, too large for anything she had ever heard or imagined. A throne. A kingdom without end. The Son of the Most High. And these words were being spoken to her. A young woman in Nazareth. A girl who ground flour and drew water and mended cloth.

She found her voice.

"How can this be," she said, "seeing I am a virgin?"

The question was simple and honest. It came from the only life she knew — a life where things worked in ordinary ways, where children came from husbands and wives, where the power of God was something spoken of in scrolls, not something that appeared in your own room on a Tuesday afternoon.

The angel answered her gently.

"The Holy Spirit will come on you," he said, "and the power of the Most High will overshadow you. Therefore the holy one who is born from you will be called the Son of God."

He paused. The room was perfectly still. Then he spoke again.

"Behold, Elizabeth your relative — she too has conceived a son in her old age. This is the sixth month with her who was called barren. For nothing will be impossible with God."

Elizabeth. Her relative. The one everyone had said would never bear a child. And now she was six months along. Mary held this piece of news like a small stone in her hand — something real, something she could check, something that made the impossible feel a little less impossible.

The room was quiet. Outside, the wind moved through the village. A goat bleated softly near the wall. Somewhere down the path, a door closed. Somewhere a child called out to a friend and was answered. The ordinary sounds of Nazareth continued, the same as they had been an hour ago, the same as they would be tomorrow — except that in this one room, in this one moment, everything was balanced on the edge of a young woman's answer.

Mary stood in the light that came through the doorway. She breathed in, slowly. She breathed out. The weight of what she had been told settled over her. Not a burden. Not a joy. Something deeper than either — something that asked her to step beyond everything she had ever known and say yes to something she could not see the end of.

Then she spoke.

"Behold, the servant of the Lord," she said. "Be it to me according to your word."

And the angel departed from her.

The room was quiet again. The light on the floor did not move. The grinding stone sat where she had left it. The water jar stood in the corner. Outside, the village of Nazareth continued — the goats, the children, the bread baking, the smoke rising from the flat roofs. Nothing had changed. And everything had changed.

Mary stood alone in the room, the words still in the air around her like dust settling after a great wind.
