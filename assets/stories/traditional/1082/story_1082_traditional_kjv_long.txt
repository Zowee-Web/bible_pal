In the sixth month, God sent the angel Gabriel unto a city of Galilee, named Nazareth. It was a small and quiet town. Stone houses with flat roofs stood along narrow paths that wound between the slopes. Olive trees grew in crooked rows upon the terraces above the village, their silver-green leaves turning in the breeze. Grapevines climbed the low walls between the houses. The air bore the smell of bread baking and wood smoke and the dry herbs that grew wild in the stony ground.

It was a common place. A village where men and women ground their corn and mended their roofs and drew water from the well. The manner of place where nothing of great note had come to pass, and none looked for anything to change.

Somewhere in Nazareth, a young woman called Mary went about her day. She was espoused to a man named Joseph, of the house of David. She was a virgin — young, quiet, known in the village as all young women were known: by her kindred, by her betrothed, by the work of her hands. She bore water. She ground the meal. She mended cloth and swept the earthen floor. Her hands were made strong by the labour, her face browned by the sun. Her life, until this hour, had been the ordinary shape of a young woman in a country village.

Then the angel came unto her.

He appeared in the room where she was. There was no sound of footsteps, no knock upon the door. The air changed. The light changed. Something vast and bright filled the small space, and Gabriel stood before her. He was not as a man, though he bore the form of one. His presence filled the room as sunlight filleth a doorway.

He spake.

"Hail, thou that art highly favoured," he said. "The Lord is with thee. Blessed art thou among women."

Mary was greatly troubled. Her hands went still. Her breath was caught. She ran not. She cried not out. She stood in the room, surrounded by the things she knew — the millstone, the water jar, the folded cloth upon the shelf — and cast in her mind what manner of salutation this should be. Who would speak such words unto her. What they might signify. Why her, of all women. Why now. Why here, in this small room, in this small town, upon this ordinary afternoon.

The angel saw her fear, and he spake again.

"Fear not, Mary," said he, "for thou hast found favour with God."

His voice was steady. The words were gentle — not commanding, not stern. They were spoken as one speaketh unto a soul that needeth to hear a thing true but cannot yet bring herself to believe it.

"Behold," he continued, "thou shalt conceive in thy womb, and bring forth a son, and shalt call his name Jesus."

Mary hearkened. She stirred not.

"He shall be great," Gabriel said, "and shall be called the Son of the Highest. The Lord God shall give unto him the throne of his father David, and he shall reign over the house of Jacob for ever; and of his kingdom there shall be no end."

The words filled the room. They were too great for that space — too great for the village, too great for aught she had ever heard or imagined. A throne. A kingdom without end. The Son of the Highest. And these words were spoken unto her. A young woman in Nazareth. A maiden who ground the meal and drew the water and mended the cloth.

She found her voice.

"How shall this be," she said, "seeing I know not a man?"

The question was plain and honest. It came from the only life she knew — a life in which things wrought in the common way, in which children came of husbands and wives, in which the power of God was a thing spoken of in the scrolls and not a thing that appeared within thy own room upon an afternoon.

The angel answered and said unto her gently, "The Holy Ghost shall come upon thee, and the power of the Highest shall overshadow thee. Therefore also that holy thing which shall be born of thee shall be called the Son of God."

He paused. The room was perfectly still. Then he spake again.

"And, behold, thy cousin Elisabeth — she hath also conceived a son in her old age. And this is the sixth month with her, who was called barren. For with God nothing shall be impossible."

Elisabeth. Her kinswoman. The one all had said would never bear a child. And now she was six months gone. Mary held this piece of news as a small stone in her hand — a thing real, a thing she might seek out, a thing that made the impossible seem somewhat less impossible.

The room was quiet. Without, the wind moved through the village. A goat bleated softly near the wall. Somewhere down the path a door was shut. Somewhere a child called unto another and was answered. The common sounds of Nazareth continued, the same as they had been an hour before — save that in this one room, in this one moment, all was balanced upon the edge of a young woman's answer.

Mary stood in the light that came through the doorway. She drew breath slowly. She let it go. The weight of what she had been told settled upon her. Not a burden. Not a joy. Something deeper than either — something that required her to step beyond all she had ever known and give answer to that which she could not see the end of.

Then she spake.

"Behold the handmaid of the Lord," she said. "Be it unto me according to thy word."

And the angel departed from her.

The room was quiet again. The light upon the floor moved not. The millstone sat where she had left it. The water jar stood in the corner. Without, the village of Nazareth went on — the goats, the children, the bread baking, the smoke rising from the flat roofs. Nothing was changed. And everything was changed.

Mary stood alone in the room, the words yet in the air about her, as dust that settleth after a great wind.
