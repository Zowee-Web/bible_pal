Samuel came to Saul with the word of the Lord on him. The old prophet was the one who had poured the oil over the young man's head. He had been there at the beginning. Now he stood before the king he had anointed and spoke the charge plainly.

"The Lord sent me to anoint you to be king over his people, over Israel. Now therefore listen to the voice of the words of the Lord. The Lord of Armies says, 'I remember what Amalek did to Israel, how he set himself against him in the way when he came up out of Egypt. Now go and strike Amalek, and utterly destroy all that they have, and don't spare them.'"

Saul listened. The charge was strict. The completeness of it was the point of it.

Saul gathered the people.

He summoned them to Telaim and counted them on the plain. Two hundred thousand footmen. Ten thousand men of Judah. He moved on the city of Amalek and set an ambush in the valley. Before the battle he sent word to the Kenites who lived among the Amalekites, telling them to depart, for they had shown kindness to Israel long ago when the people came up out of Egypt. The Kenites went out from among them. Then Saul struck. The army moved from Havilah as far as Shur, which is before Egypt. The Amalekites fell.

He took Agag the king alive.

He utterly destroyed all the people with the edge of the sword. But the best of the sheep and the cattle, the fat calves and the lambs, all that was good — these the king and the people spared. They were not willing to utterly destroy them. Everything that was vile and refuse, that they destroyed. The herds that were kept were driven back with the army, lowing on the road, bleating as they walked.

The word of the charge had been strict. The doing had been partial.

That night the word of the Lord came to Samuel.

"It grieves me that I have set up Saul to be king, for he has turned back from following me, and has not performed my commandments."

The old man heard it in the dark. He sat up where he lay. He did not speak. The grief in the word was not his alone now, and it did not stay in him quietly. Samuel was angry, and he cried to the Lord all night. The hours passed slowly. The lamp beside him burned low and went out. He did not relight it. He prayed for the king. He prayed against the king. He did not know which prayer the Lord would answer, and he did not stop praying either one.

Samuel rose early to meet Saul in the morning.

Word came to him as he set out on the road. Saul had gone up to Carmel. There, in his triumph, he had set up a monument for himself. Then he had turned and passed on and gone down to Gilgal. Samuel walked on toward Gilgal. The road ran between the hills. The morning was clear.

As he came near the camp he heard it. The bleating of sheep. The lowing of cattle. The sound carried far on the morning air. Samuel slowed. He listened. The sound did not stop. The sound was the answer to the question he had already been told the answer to, and the sound was still the answer when he came into the camp.

Saul came out to meet him with the greeting already on his lips.

"Blessed are you by the Lord. I have performed the commandment of the Lord."

Samuel did not return the greeting. He stood in the road. He looked at the king he had anointed, the tall young man he had once carried hope for, and the king did not meet his eyes for long.

"Then what does this bleating of the sheep in my ears, and the lowing of the cattle which I hear, mean?"

Saul's eyes shifted toward the herds behind him. The answer was ready. He had thought about it on the road.

"They have brought them from the Amalekites. The people spared the best of the sheep and of the cattle, to sacrifice to the Lord your God. The rest we have utterly destroyed."

Samuel raised his hand. He stopped him there.

"Stay, and I will tell you what the Lord said to me last night."

Saul said, "Say on."

Samuel spoke as the herds bleated on behind the king's shoulder. He did not raise his voice. The herds were loud enough. The prophet's words moved beneath the noise of them, and Saul heard every one.

"Though you were little in your own sight, were you not made the head of the tribes of Israel? The Lord anointed you king over Israel, and the Lord sent you on a journey, and said, 'Go, and utterly destroy the sinners the Amalekites, and fight against them until they are consumed.' Why then did you not obey the voice of the Lord, but took the plunder, and did that which was evil in the sight of the Lord?"

Saul answered. The answer came out fast. "But I have obeyed the voice of the Lord, and have gone the way the Lord sent me, and have brought Agag the king of Amalek, and have utterly destroyed the Amalekites. The people took of the plunder, sheep and cattle, the best of the devoted things, to sacrifice to the Lord your God in Gilgal."

Samuel did not move. The herds bleated on. When he spoke, his voice was the voice of the older man who had loved this king, and the voice of the prophet who could not unsay what the Lord had said in the night.

"Has the Lord as great delight in burnt offerings and sacrifices, as in obeying the voice of the Lord? Behold, to obey is better than sacrifice, and to listen than the fat of rams. For rebellion is as the sin of witchcraft, and stubbornness is as iniquity and idolatry. Because you have rejected the word of the Lord, he has also rejected you from being king."

The words landed and stayed. The camp behind Saul was still busy with the morning. The herds bleated. Somewhere a man laughed at something his fellow had said, and the laugh did not know what had just been spoken on the road.

Saul's face moved. The look that came over it was not the look of a man who had been surprised by the verdict. It was the look of a man who had been waiting for it.

"I have sinned. For I have transgressed the commandment of the Lord, and your words, because I feared the people, and obeyed their voice."

Samuel did not answer. He turned to go.

Saul reached out. His hand caught the skirt of the prophet's robe. He held it. He meant to hold the old man with it, the way a child holds a father's sleeve. Samuel kept walking. The fabric pulled taut between them. Then it gave. A long strip of the robe tore in the king's grip.

Samuel turned back.

He looked at the torn cloth in the king's hand. He looked at the king. He spoke the word that the cloth had already spoken without needing him.

"The Lord has torn the kingdom of Israel from you today, and has given it to a neighbor of yours, who is better than you."

Saul did not let go of the cloth. He could not seem to. He stood holding the torn strip of the prophet's robe in his fist, and the herds bleated, and Samuel turned again and walked away from him.

The old man walked back the way he had come. The hills opened ahead of him. He did not look back at the king he had anointed. He carried the grief of the Lord in him, and his own grief along with it, and the two griefs walked together down the road into the morning.

Samuel did not see Saul again until the day of Saul's death, but he mourned for him many days after, and in the night he still cried to the Lord for him, and the Lord still grieved over the king he had set up, and in the camp at Gilgal that morning the bleating of the sheep went on.
