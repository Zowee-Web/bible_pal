A certain man had two sons.

The younger of them came to his father one evening, and the words had clearly been gathered in him for some time. "Father, give me my share of your property."

The father looked at him. He did not answer for a long moment. Then he turned and went into the house, and the older brother, who had been standing near the door, watched him go. The father divided his living between them. The portion was set out. The papers were drawn. The younger son took what was his.

Not many days after, the younger son gathered all that he had and traveled into a far country.

He went out before dawn. The road climbed away from the village and over the ridge, and the house behind him grew small and then was hidden. He did not look back more than once. The country he came to was bright and full of voices. There were tables and lamps and friends he had not had the day before. There were rooms above streets and music that did not stop. He wasted his substance with reckless living. The coin moved out of his hand easily. The days ran together.

When he had spent everything, a severe famine arose in that country. The bread carts that had passed by in the mornings stopped passing. The friends grew quiet. The rooms above the streets emptied. He stood in a doorway one afternoon and counted what he had and found nothing. He began to be in need.

He went and joined himself to one of the citizens of that country. The citizen looked him over and sent him into his fields to feed pigs.

The fields were on the slope below the road. The troughs sat in the mud. Flies circled above the slop. The pods lay scattered on the ground where the pigs rooted through them. He carried the slop in a wooden bucket from the heap to the troughs and back again. The smell was in his clothes by the second morning. He would have filled his stomach with the pods that the pigs ate, but no one gave him anything. At evening he sat on the slope above the troughs and watched the light leave the field, and his belly was empty, and the pigs were nearer to him than any other living thing.

When he came to himself, he said, "How many of my father's hired servants have bread enough to spare, and I am dying with hunger! I will arise and go to my father, and will say to him, 'Father, I have sinned against heaven and in your sight. I am no longer worthy to be called your son. Make me as one of your hired servants.'"

He arose and came to his father.

The road back was long. He walked it without coin or sandals or bread. He rehearsed the speech under his breath as he went. He said the words and said them again until they were a kind of bread to him, and his feet went on.

But while he was still far off, his father saw him.

The father had been on the roof, or near the gate, or at the edge of the field where the road came up over the ridge. He saw the figure first as a shape in the heat, far down where the road thinned. The shape did not turn aside. It came on. The father's hand went to his chest. Then he was moved with compassion, and he gathered the hem of his robe in his hand, and he ran.

He ran. The household saw it from the courtyard and did not understand. The father ran down the road in the bright middle of the day, his sandals slapping the stone, his breath loud, his robe pulled high. The figure on the road stopped. The figure stood still. The father reached him, and he fell on his son's neck and kissed him. The dust from the road was on the son's clothes. The smell of the far country was still in his hair. The father did not seem to know it.

The son said to him, "Father, I have sinned against heaven and in your sight. I am no longer worthy to be called your son."

But the father said to his servants, who had run after him as best they could and stood now at the edge of the road, breathing hard, "Bring out the best robe and put it on him. Put a ring on his hand and sandals on his feet. Bring the fattened calf, kill it, and let us eat and celebrate. For this my son was dead and is alive again. He was lost and is found."

The robe was brought from the house. The ring was slid onto his hand. The sandals were tied. The fattened calf, which had been kept against a coming feast, was led out and killed. The smell of roasting meat carried on the air. Lamps were lit in the great room. Musicians were called from the village. And they began to celebrate.

Now his elder son was in the field. He had been there since the early hours, as he had been every day. As he came and drew near to the house in the late afternoon, he heard music and dancing. He stopped where the path came up through the olives. He listened. The music went on. He called one of the servants who had come out for water and asked what these things meant. The servant said to him, "Your brother has come, and your father has killed the fattened calf, because he has received him back safe and sound."

The elder son stood in the path. The light came down through the olive branches and lay on his hands. He did not move toward the house. He did not move away. He was angry, and he would not go in.

His father, inside, was told. And his father came out and pleaded with him.

He answered his father, "Look, these many years I have served you, and I never disobeyed a command of yours, but you never gave me a goat, that I might celebrate with my friends. But when this your son came, who has devoured your living with prostitutes, you killed the fattened calf for him."

But he said to him, "Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours. But it was fitting to celebrate and be glad, for this your brother was dead and is alive again. He was lost and is found."

The music carried out through the open door behind him. The lamps inside burned on. The father stood in the path and waited.

The elder son stood outside.
