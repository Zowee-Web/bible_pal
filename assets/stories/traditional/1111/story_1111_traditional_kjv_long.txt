A certain man had two sons.

And the younger of them came unto his father one evening, and the words had clearly been gathered in him for some time. "Father, give me the portion of goods that falleth to me."

The father looked upon him. He answered not for a long moment. Then he turned and went into the house, and the elder brother, which had been standing near the door, watched him go. And he divided unto them his living. The portion was set out. The papers were drawn. The younger son took that which was his.

And not many days after, the younger son gathered all together, and took his journey into a far country.

He went out before dawn. The road climbed away from the village and over the ridge, and the house behind him grew small and then was hidden. He looked not back more than once. The country he came unto was bright and full of voices. There were tables and lamps and friends he had not had the day before. There were rooms above streets and musick that ceased not. And there he wasted his substance with riotous living. The coin moved out of his hand easily. The days ran together.

And when he had spent all, there arose a mighty famine in that land. The bread carts that had passed by in the mornings stopped passing. The friends grew quiet. The rooms above the streets emptied. He stood in a doorway one afternoon and counted what he had and found nothing. And he began to be in want.

And he went and joined himself to a citizen of that country, and he sent him into his fields to feed swine.

The fields lay upon the slope below the road. The troughs sat in the mire. Flies circled above the slop. The husks lay scattered upon the ground where the swine rooted through them. He carried the slop in a wooden bucket from the heap unto the troughs and back again. The smell was in his garments by the second morning. And he would fain have filled his belly with the husks that the swine did eat, and no man gave unto him. At evening he sat upon the slope above the troughs and watched the light leave the field, and his belly was empty, and the swine were nearer unto him than any other living thing.

And when he came to himself, he said, "How many hired servants of my father's have bread enough and to spare, and I perish with hunger! I will arise and go to my father, and will say unto him, 'Father, I have sinned against heaven, and before thee, and am no more worthy to be called thy son. Make me as one of thy hired servants.'"

And he arose, and came to his father.

The road back was long. He walked it without coin or shoes or bread. He rehearsed the speech under his breath as he went. He said the words and said them again until they were a kind of bread unto him, and his feet went on.

But when he was yet a great way off, his father saw him.

The father had been upon the roof, or near the gate, or at the edge of the field where the road came up over the ridge. He saw the figure first as a shape in the heat, far down where the road thinned. The shape turned not aside. It came on. The father's hand went to his breast. And he had compassion, and he gathered the hem of his robe in his hand, and he ran.

He ran. The household saw it from the courtyard and understood not. The father ran down the road in the bright middle of the day, his sandals slapping the stone, his breath loud, his robe pulled high. The figure on the road stopped. The figure stood still. The father came unto him, and fell on his son's neck, and kissed him. The dust from the road was upon the son's garments. The smell of the far country was yet in his hair. The father seemed not to know it.

And the son said unto him, "Father, I have sinned against heaven, and in thy sight, and am no more worthy to be called thy son."

But the father said to his servants, which had run after him as best they could and stood now at the edge of the road, breathing hard, "Bring forth the best robe, and put it on him, and put a ring on his hand, and shoes on his feet. And bring hither the fatted calf, and kill it, and let us eat, and be merry. For this my son was dead, and is alive again. He was lost, and is found."

The robe was brought from the house. The ring was slid upon his hand. The shoes were tied. The fatted calf, which had been kept against a coming feast, was led out and killed. The smell of roasting meat carried upon the air. Lamps were lit in the great room. Musicians were called from the village. And they began to be merry.

Now his elder son was in the field. He had been there since the early hours, as he had been every day. And as he came and drew nigh to the house in the late afternoon, he heard musick and dancing. He stopped where the path came up through the olives. He listened. The musick went on. He called one of the servants which had come out for water, and asked what these things meant. The servant said unto him, "Thy brother is come, and thy father hath killed the fatted calf, because he hath received him safe and sound."

The elder son stood in the path. The light came down through the olive branches and lay upon his hands. He moved not toward the house. He moved not away. And he was angry, and would not go in.

His father, within, was told. Therefore came his father out, and entreated him.

And he answering said to his father, "Lo, these many years do I serve thee, neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment, and yet thou never gavest me a kid, that I might make merry with my friends. But as soon as this thy son was come, which hath devoured thy living with harlots, thou hast killed for him the fatted calf."

And he said unto him, "Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine. It was meet that we should make merry, and be glad, for this thy brother was dead, and is alive again, and was lost, and is found."

The musick carried out through the open door behind him. The lamps within burned on. The father stood in the path and waited.

The elder son stood without, and the light of the lamps lay on his hands, and the father did not turn away from him.
