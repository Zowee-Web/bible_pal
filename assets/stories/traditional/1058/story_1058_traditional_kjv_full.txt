The Lord bade Jeremiah do an uncommon thing. "Arise," saith the Lord, "and go down to the potter's house. There I have somewhat to shew thee."

So Jeremiah went forth out of the city and walked down the narrow way toward the valley. The morning air was cool, and the path was steep. He passed by the shops of the smiths, where sparks flew from their hammers and the savour of hot iron drifted into the street. He passed by the hewers of stone, where white dust covered all things — the tables, the tools, even the eyebrows of the men. At the foot of the hill, hard by the brook whence the best clay was digged out of the ground, he found the potter's house.

The door stood open. Jeremiah went in.

The shop was small and dim and cool. The ceiling was low, blackened with years of kiln smoke. Shelves lined every wall, and upon the shelves sat rows upon rows of finished vessels — great ones for storing of grain, small ones for holding of oil, bowls for mingling of bread, tall jugs for bearing of water. Every vessel was smooth and dry and waiting to be used. The floor was beaten earth, worn into shallow grooves wherein the potter's feet had pushed the wheel a thousand times.

The potter sat upon a low stool before his wheel. He was an aged man with strong hands, the knuckles swollen from years of labour. His fingers were dark with clay. A lump of wet earth — grayish brown, heavy, cold from the ground — sat upon the flat stone wheel before him. The clay bore the savour of rain and of river mud.

He pushed the wheel with his foot, and it began to spin. Slowly at the first, then faster. The stone hummed, a low sound that filled the little chamber.

The potter dipped his hands in a bowl of water and laid them about the clay. As the wheel turned, his thumbs pressed into the midst. A hollow appeared. His fingers rose along the outside, drawing the walls of the clay upward. Water ran in thin lines down the spinning surface. The lump began to change. It was becoming a jar — round at the bottom, narrower in the middle, a neck beginning to form at the top.

Jeremiah beheld it closely. The clay seemed alive. It moved betwixt the potter's hands as though it were breathing, rising and shaping itself. The wet surface caught the light from the door and gleamed.

Then something came to pass. The clay wobbled. A weak place opened in the wall — peradventure a tiny bubble of air entrapped within, peradventure a piece of sand that was too rough. The side of the jar folded inward. The neck fell down. The whole vessel crumpled upon the wheel, even as a flower withering in haste.

Jeremiah waited for the potter to cast the clay away and begin with a new lump.

But the potter did not so.

Rather, he pressed the marred jar back down with the heel of his hand. He pushed the clay into itself, folding the crumpled walls down, smoothing the work back into a round, flat lump. His palms wrought firmly but not roughly — as one handleth a thing he knoweth well. The wheel kept turning. The clay looked even as it had before the potter began — as though nothing had ever been made of it at all.

The potter dipped his hands in water again. Droplets fell from his wrists upon the spinning clay. Then he began once more.

This time his thumbs opened the clay in another manner. Wider. The walls rose not as high. A different shape was forming — a bowl with thick walls and a wide mouth, sturdy and solid and low upon the wheel. Not the same as the first thing. Somewhat new. Somewhat the potter chose, somewhat that seemed good unto him.

The bowl held. The walls were even. The rim was smooth beneath his fingertips. The potter's hands moved slowly, carefully, and the clay obeyed.

Then the Lord spake. Even there in the dusty little house, with the wheel yet turning and the potter yet working, the Lord said unto Jeremiah:

"Cannot I do with you as this potter? saith the Lord. Behold, as the clay is in the potter's hand, so are ye in mine hand."

Jeremiah stood still. The wheel hummed. The potter lifted his wet fingers from the rim and sat back upon his stool. A drop of clay-coloured water ran down his forearm and fell unto the floor.

The new bowl sat upon the spinning stone — wide and solid and smooth.

The wheel slowed. The clay held its shape. And the bowl was whole.
