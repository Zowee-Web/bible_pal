God told Jeremiah to do something unusual. "Get up," God said. "Go down to the potter's house. I have something to show you there."

So Jeremiah left the city and walked down the narrow road toward the valley. The morning air was cool and the path was steep. He passed the shops of the metalworkers, where sparks flew from their hammers and the smell of hot iron drifted into the street. He passed the stonecutters, where white dust covered everything — the tables, the tools, even the men's eyebrows. At the bottom of the hill, near the stream where the best clay was dug from the ground, he found the potter's shop.

The door stood open. Jeremiah stepped inside.

The shop was small and dim and cool. The ceiling was low, blackened with years of kiln smoke. Shelves lined every wall, and on the shelves sat rows and rows of finished pots — big ones for storing grain, small ones for holding oil, bowls for mixing bread, tall jugs for carrying water. Every pot was smooth and dry and waiting to be used. The floor was packed earth, worn into shallow grooves where the potter's feet had pushed the wheel a thousand times.

The potter sat on a low stool in front of his wheel. He was an older man with strong hands, the knuckles swollen from years of work. His fingers were dark with clay. A lump of wet earth — grayish brown, heavy, cold from the ground — sat on the flat stone wheel in front of him. The clay smelled like rain and river mud.

He pushed the wheel with his foot and it started to spin. Slowly at first, then faster. The stone hummed, a low sound that filled the little room.

The potter dipped his hands in a bowl of water and placed them around the clay. As the wheel turned, his thumbs pressed into the center. A hollow appeared. His fingers rose along the outside, pulling the walls of the clay upward. Water ran in thin lines down the spinning surface. The lump began to change. It was becoming a jar — round at the bottom, narrower in the middle, a neck beginning to form at the top.

Jeremiah watched closely. The clay looked alive. It moved between the potter's hands like it was breathing, rising and shaping itself. The wet surface caught the light from the doorway and gleamed.

Then something happened. The clay wobbled. A weak spot opened in the wall — maybe a tiny air bubble trapped inside, maybe a piece of sand that was too rough. The side of the jar folded inward. The neck collapsed. The whole thing crumpled on the wheel like a flower wilting in fast motion.

Jeremiah waited for the potter to throw the clay away and start with a new lump.

But the potter didn't do that.

Instead, he pressed the broken jar back down with the heel of his hand. He pushed the clay into itself, folding the crumpled walls down, smoothing the mess back into a round, flat lump. His palms worked firmly but not roughly — the way someone handles something they know well. The wheel kept turning. The clay looked just like it had before the potter started — like nothing had ever been made from it at all.

The potter dipped his hands in water again. Droplets fell from his wrists onto the spinning clay. Then he began once more.

This time his thumbs opened the clay differently. Wider. The walls didn't go up as high. A different shape was forming — a bowl with thick walls and a wide mouth, sturdy and solid and low to the wheel. Not the same as the first thing. Something new. Something the potter chose, something that seemed good to him.

The bowl held. The walls were even. The rim was smooth under his fingertips. The potter's hands moved slowly, carefully, and the clay obeyed.

Then God spoke. Right there in the dusty little shop, with the wheel still turning and the potter still working, God said to Jeremiah:

"Can I not do the same with you? Like the clay in the potter's hand — that is how you are in my hand."

Jeremiah stood still. The wheel hummed. The potter lifted his wet fingers from the rim and sat back on his stool. A drop of clay-colored water ran down his forearm and fell to the floor.

The new bowl sat on the spinning stone — wide and solid and smooth. It was not the jar the potter had first intended. It was something different. Something that had been broken and pressed down and reshaped by hands that knew exactly what they were doing.

The wheel slowed. The clay held its shape. And the bowl was whole.
