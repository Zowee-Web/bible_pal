The messengers came to Jerusalem with fear on their faces. They had been running. Their clothes were stained with dust and sweat from the road, and their words tumbled out in a rush: a vast army was on the march. The Moabites and the Ammonites, together with allies from the lands beyond the Dead Sea, had gathered a force so large that the scouts could not count it. They had already crossed the water. They were already climbing through the passes of the wilderness. They were at En-gedi — less than a day's march from Jerusalem.

King Jehoshaphat heard the report and was afraid.

He was a good king — the scripture says so plainly. He had walked in the ways of his father Asa. He had removed the high places and the wooden images from Judah. He had sent teachers throughout the land to instruct the people in the law of the Lord. He was faithful, devoted, careful in his worship. But he was not a warrior. He did not have the army to match what was coming. The numbers were staggering. The geography was against him. Every rational assessment of the situation pointed to one outcome: Judah would be overrun.

But Jehoshaphat did not call for weapons first. He called for prayer.

He proclaimed a fast throughout all of Judah. Word went out from Jerusalem to every town and village: come to the temple. Bring your families. Come and pray. And they came — from the hill country and the lowlands and the Negev, from farms and vineyards and shepherds' camps. They gathered in the court of the temple in Jerusalem, a great assembly of men, women, and children, standing shoulder to shoulder in the open space before the house of the Lord. The sound of their gathering filled the courts — shuffling feet, low voices, the crying of small children held against their mothers' shoulders.

Jehoshaphat stood before them. He did not address the army. He did not give a strategic briefing. He prayed. He prayed aloud, his voice carrying across the crowd.

"O Lord, God of our fathers, are you not God in heaven? Do you not rule over all the kingdoms of the nations? In your hand are power and might, so that none is able to withstand you." He traced the history — how God had driven out the inhabitants of the land before Israel, how the temple had been built as a place to stand in times of distress. "If disaster comes upon us — the sword, judgment, pestilence, or famine — we will stand before this house and before you, for your name is in this house, and cry out to you in our distress, and you will hear and save."

Then he laid out the current crisis plainly. The enemy was vast. The army of Judah was small. The invasion was already underway. And then he said the truest, most vulnerable words a king could speak before his people:

"We do not know what to do. But our eyes are on you."

The crowd was silent. Every face was turned toward the temple. Men stood with their jaws tight and their hands clenched. Women held their children close. Old men leaned on their sticks and wept. No one moved.

Then the Spirit of the Lord fell upon a man in the crowd. His name was Jahaziel — a Levite, not a general, not a priest of high rank, just a man standing among the people. He opened his mouth and spoke with an authority that was not his own.

"Listen, all Judah and inhabitants of Jerusalem, and King Jehoshaphat. Thus says the Lord to you: Do not be afraid and do not be dismayed because of this great multitude, for the battle is not yours but God's."

He told them where the enemy would come — by the ascent of Ziz — and where to position themselves. "Tomorrow, go down against them," he said. "You will not need to fight in this battle. Position yourselves. Stand still. And see the salvation of the Lord, who is with you, O Judah and Jerusalem. Do not be afraid. Do not be dismayed. Tomorrow, go out against them, and the Lord will be with you."

Jehoshaphat bowed with his face to the ground. The entire assembly fell before the Lord. Then certain Levites stood and began to praise God with a loud voice — their singing rising above the crowd, above the temple courts, up into the evening sky.

Early the next morning, the army of Judah went out into the wilderness of Tekoa. The sun was rising over the brown hills. The men marched in formation, but their faces were uncertain. The enemy was out there, beyond the ridgeline, vast and armed and coming.

Jehoshaphat stood before the troops. "Believe in the Lord your God," he said, "and you will be established. Believe his prophets, and you will succeed."

Then he made a decision that no commander in the history of warfare had ever made. He appointed singers to go out ahead of the army. Not soldiers. Not scouts. Not archers or cavalry. Singers — men and women dressed in holy garments, walking at the front of the line, unarmed, exposed, singing into the morning air:

"Give thanks to the Lord, for his steadfast love endures forever."

The army marched behind them. The singers' voices carried across the hills.

And when they began to sing and praise, the Lord set ambushes against the enemy. The Ammonites and the Moabites rose up against the men from Mount Seir and utterly destroyed them. Then they turned on each other. The alliance shattered. The vast army tore itself apart.

When the army of Judah reached the lookout point over the wilderness of Jeruel and looked down into the valley, they saw a field of fallen bodies. Not one of the enemy had escaped. The battle had been fought without Judah lifting a single weapon.

It took three days to gather the plunder — there was so much of it. Weapons, armor, clothing, precious things. On the fourth day, they gathered in a valley and blessed the Lord, and the valley was named Beracah — the Valley of Blessing — because that is what happened there.

Then they returned to Jerusalem. They came with joy — with harps and lyres and trumpets, singing as they walked through the gates. They went straight to the house of the Lord.

And the fear of God fell upon every kingdom in the surrounding lands when they heard what the Lord had done. And the realm of Jehoshaphat was quiet, for God gave him rest on every side.
