Word reached King Jehoshaphat in Jerusalem that a vast army was on the march. The Moabites and the Ammonites, together with allies from beyond the Dead Sea, had gathered a force so large it could not be counted. They had already crossed the water. They were already moving through the wilderness toward Judah. Scouts brought the reports: the enemy was at En-gedi, climbing the passes, heading for Jerusalem.

Jehoshaphat was afraid. He was a good king — faithful, just, devoted to the God of his fathers — but he was not a general. He did not have the army to match what was coming. The numbers were against him. The terrain was against him. Everything rational said: prepare to be overrun.

But Jehoshaphat did not call for weapons first. He called for prayer. He proclaimed a fast throughout all Judah, and the people gathered in the court of the temple in Jerusalem — a great assembly of men, women, and children, standing shoulder to shoulder in the open space before the house of the Lord.

Jehoshaphat stood before them and prayed aloud. His voice carried across the crowd. "O Lord, God of our fathers, are you not God in heaven? Do you not rule over all the kingdoms of the nations? In your hand are power and might, so that none is able to withstand you." He reminded God of the covenant, of the promises, of the history that had brought them to this place. And then he said the truest thing a man can say when he is outmatched: "We do not know what to do, but our eyes are on you."

All of Judah stood before the Lord. The wind stirred the edges of their garments. Children held their mothers' hands. Old men leaned on their sticks. No one spoke.

Then the Spirit of the Lord came upon Jahaziel, a Levite, standing in the middle of the assembly. He spoke with authority he had not earned by rank: "Listen, all Judah and inhabitants of Jerusalem, and King Jehoshaphat. Thus says the Lord to you: Do not be afraid. Do not be dismayed by this great multitude. For the battle is not yours, but God's."

He told them where the enemy would come — by the ascent of Ziz — and what they were to do. "You will not need to fight in this battle," he said. "Position yourselves. Stand still. And see the salvation of the Lord, who is with you."

Jehoshaphat bowed with his face to the ground. All the people fell before the Lord. Some of the Levites stood and praised God with a very loud voice, their words echoing off the temple walls.

Early the next morning, the army of Judah went out into the wilderness of Tekoa. The sun was rising over the brown hills. Jehoshaphat stood before the troops and said, "Believe in the Lord your God, and you will be established. Believe his prophets, and you will succeed."

Then he made a decision that no military commander in history would have made. He appointed singers — not soldiers, not archers, not cavalry — to go out ahead of the army. They walked at the front of the line, unarmed, singing: "Give thanks to the Lord, for his steadfast love endures forever."

And when they began to sing and praise, the Lord set ambushes against the invaders. The Ammonites and Moabites turned on the men from Mount Seir and destroyed them. Then they turned on each other. By the time the army of Judah reached the lookout point over the wilderness and looked down, the valley was covered with fallen bodies. Not one of the enemy had escaped.

It took three days to collect the spoil — there was so much of it. On the fourth day, they gathered in a valley they named Beracah, which means blessing, and they blessed the Lord in that place.

Then they returned to Jerusalem with great joy, with harps and lyres and trumpets, and they came to the house of the Lord.
