Jericho was tightly shut up because of the children of Israel. The great gate was barred from within, the cross-beam dropped into its iron sockets. No one went out, and no one came in. The wall rose high above the plain, its stones fitted close, its parapets watched day and night. From the camp of Israel on the other side of the valley, the city looked like a closed fist set against the morning.

In the tent of meeting the Lord spoke to Joshua. The word came in the quiet hour before dawn. "I have given Jericho into your hand, with its king and the mighty men of valor. The men of war shall march around the city, going around it once. So shall it be for six days. Seven priests shall bear seven shofars of rams' horns before the ark. On the seventh day the city shall be circled seven times, and the priests shall blow the shofars. When the long blast sounds, the people shall shout with a great shout, and the wall of the city shall fall down flat, and the people shall go up, every man straight before him."

Joshua heard the strategy that no captain of men would have written. He bowed his face to the floor of the tent. He rose.

He called the priests. They came to him in their linen, their feet bare, the dust of the camp soft beneath them. "Take up the ark of the covenant of the Lord," he said. "Let seven priests bear seven shofars of rams' horns before the ark." The priests nodded without speaking. He called the captains of the people. "Advance. March around the city. Let the armed men pass on before the ark of the Lord."

Then he set the silence over them. He stood where the camp could see him and lifted his voice so the back ranks could hear. "No shout shall be raised. No voice shall be heard. Let no word proceed out of any mouth until the day I tell you to shout. Then shall the people shout."

The order travelled outward through the camp. Mothers laid a finger over the lips of their children. Old men set their staffs down softly. A horse was led away from the line of march so its complaint would not break the order. The camp made itself quiet against the morning.

The procession formed as the sun lifted. The armed men went first, rank by rank, their sandals striking the dust in a steady rhythm. Behind them came the seven priests, the seven shofars at their lips. Then came the ark of the Lord, carried on the shoulders of the bearing priests, its acacia wood polished by the touch of many hands, the cherubim above it catching the light. The rear guard followed last. The shofars sounded long and low as they went, a single note carried out over the plain.

They came to the foot of the wall. They turned and walked along it. The wall rose above them, its shadow falling cool on the line of march. From the parapets above, watchers leaned between the stones. A man with a copper helmet shaded his eyes and stared down. A woman drew her child back from the edge. A captain of the city called to his archers to hold. There was no banner of war. There were no slings. There was only a line of men walking, the shofars, and the ark.

The line completed its circuit. The armed men turned away from the wall. The priests with the shofars turned. The ark turned. The rear guard followed. The procession returned to the camp. They lodged in the camp. The first day was done.

Joshua rose early on the second morning. The priests took up the ark. The shofars sounded. The procession marched the same circuit and returned. So it was the third day, and the fourth, and the fifth, and the sixth. Six mornings of silence broken only by the rams' horns. Six evenings of return. Inside Jericho the watchers grew weary of staring down at the same line walking, and weary of waiting for the assault that did not come, and the strangeness of it began to weigh more heavily on them than a siege of arrows would have weighed.

On the seventh day Israel rose at the first lifting of the dark. The priests took up the ark while stars still showed. The procession went out.

They marched once around the city. They marched a second time. The shofars sounded continually. They marched a third time, and a fourth, and a fifth, and a sixth. The dust they had raised on the first circuit had not yet settled when they raised it again on the second. The armed men did not turn their heads. The bearers of the ark did not falter. The rear guard kept pace. The wall slid past on their right hand, course after course of its fitted stone, and the shofars sounded on.

On the seventh circuit, as the head of the procession came round to the place from which it had begun, the priests drew breath and blew a long blast on the rams' horns. The note rolled out long over the plain, lifted, and broke against the wall.

Joshua lifted his voice. He spoke so that the back ranks could hear him over the shofars. "Shout! For the Lord has given you the city. The city shall be devoted, even it and all that is in it, to the Lord. Only Rahab the prostitute shall live, she and all that are with her in her house, because she hid the messengers that we sent. Keep yourselves from the devoted thing, lest you bring trouble on the camp. All the silver, and the gold, and the vessels of bronze and of iron are holy to the Lord. They shall come into the treasury of the Lord."

The people shouted. From the front rank to the last the great shout rose, a sound that had been held in for seven days and now came up into the morning all at once. The priests blew the shofars. When the people heard the sound of the shofar, they shouted with a great shout, and the wall of the city fell down flat. The stones came apart along their fitted courses. The parapets tilted and slid. A long ribbon of dust rose into the morning light along the whole length of the wall, and where the wall had stood the plain showed open ground and the houses of Jericho beyond.

Israel waited a breath. Then they went up into the city, every man straight before him.
