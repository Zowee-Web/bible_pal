Job had lost all. His cattle, his servants, his sons and daughters — all gone. His body had been smitten with sore boils from the sole of his foot unto his crown. And through all of it, Job had spoken unto God.

Now God had answered him. God had spoken out of the whirlwind, and the words had shaken the air round about Job's ears.

Job opened his mouth. "I know that thou canst do every thing," he said. "And that no thought of thine can be withholden from thee."

He paused. The wind had settled. The dust hung in the still air.

"Thou hast asked, 'Who is he that hideth counsel without knowledge?'" Job said. "I am the one. I have uttered that I understood not. Things too wonderful for me, which I knew not."

Job bowed his face. His knees pressed into the ash beneath him. Grey powder clung to his skin and his rent robe.

"Thou hast said, 'Hear, I beseech thee, and I will speak. I will demand of thee, and declare thou unto me,'" Job continued. "I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear. But now — now mine eye seeth thee."

He laid both hands flat against the ground. The ash was cool and gritty beneath his palms.

"Wherefore I abhor myself," Job said unto God. "And repent in dust and ashes."

Then God turned and spake unto Eliphaz the Temanite, one of Job's three friends who had come to sit with him in the days of his affliction.

"My wrath is kindled against thee," God said, "and against thy two friends. For ye have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job hath."

The three friends stood very still.

"Take unto you now seven bullocks and seven rams," God told Eliphaz. "And go to my servant Job. And offer up for yourselves a burnt offering. And my servant Job shall pray for you. For him will I accept, lest I deal with you after your folly — in that ye have not spoken of me the thing which is right, like my servant Job."

So Eliphaz the Temanite and Bildad the Shuhite and Zophar the Naamathite did even as the Lord had commanded them. They brought the beasts — seven bullocks with dark eyes and broad backs, and seven rams with thick wool. They builded the fire. They prepared the offering. The smoke arose in a column against the sky.

And Job prayed for his three friends.

The Lord accepted the prayer of Job.

And the Lord turned the captivity of Job, when he prayed for his friends. The Lord also gave Job twice as much as he had before.

Where Job once had seven thousand sheep, now fourteen thousand grazed across the green hills. Where he once had three thousand camels, now six thousand stood in long rows, their heads bobbing in the heat. He had a thousand yoke of oxen where afore he had five hundred. And a thousand she asses where afore he had five hundred.

The fields were full. The pastures stretched wide. The sound of beasts filled the morning air — bleating, braying, the low call of oxen.

Then came all Job's brethren to see him. His sisters came. All they that had been of his acquaintance before — neighbours, old friends, men from far away — all came to his house.

They sat down and did eat bread with him. Bread was broken. Meat was passed from hand to hand. The room was warm and full of voices.

Every man comforted Job. Every one spake kind words concerning all that he had passed through. And every one of them gave Job a token — a piece of money and an earring of gold.

The silver clinked as it was set upon the table. The gold rings caught the lamplight.

The Lord also gave Job seven sons and three daughters.

The name of the first daughter was Jemima. The name of the second was Kezia. The name of the third was Kerenhappuch.

In all the land were no women found so fair as the daughters of Job. And Job gave them inheritance among their brethren.

Job lived an hundred and forty years after all this. He saw his children grow. He saw his children's children. He saw their children too, even unto the fourth generation.

Great-grandchildren chased one another betwixt the doorposts. Gold rings gleamed upon weathered hands. Bread sat upon the long table, and four generations of voices filled every chamber.
