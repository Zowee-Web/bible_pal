Job had lost everything. His animals, his servants, his sons and daughters — all gone. His body had been covered in painful sores from the bottom of his feet to the top of his head. And through all of it, Job had spoken to God.

Now God had answered him. God had spoken out of a whirlwind, and the words had shaken the air around Job's ears.

Job opened his mouth. "I know that you can do all things," he said. "No purpose of yours can be stopped."

He paused. The wind had settled. The dust hung in the still air.

"You asked, 'Who is this that hides my plans without knowledge?'" Job said. "I am the one. I talked about things I did not understand. Things too wonderful for me to know."

Job lowered his face. His knees pressed into the ash beneath him. Gray powder clung to his skin and his torn robe.

"You said, 'Listen, and I will speak. I will question you, and you will answer me,'" Job continued. "I had heard about you with my ears. But now — now my eyes have seen you."

He placed both hands flat against the ground. The ash was cool and gritty under his palms.

"I take back what I said," Job told God. "I sit here in dust and ashes."

Then God turned and spoke to Eliphaz the Temanite, one of Job's three friends who had come to sit with him during his suffering.

"I am angry with you," God said, "and with your two friends. You did not speak what was right about me. My servant Job did."

The three friends stood very still.

"Take seven bulls and seven rams," God told Eliphaz. "Go to my servant Job. Offer a burnt offering for yourselves. And Job will pray for you. I will accept his prayer. I will not deal with you according to your foolishness — because you did not speak what was right about me, the way my servant Job did."

So Eliphaz and Bildad the Shuhite and Zophar the Naamathite did exactly what God commanded. They brought the animals — seven bulls with dark eyes and broad backs, and seven rams with thick wool. They built the fire. They prepared the offering. The smoke rose in a column against the sky.

And Job prayed for his three friends.

God accepted Job's prayer.

After Job prayed for his friends, something began to change. God restored everything Job had lost. Not just the same amount. Twice as much.

Where Job once had seven thousand sheep, now fourteen thousand grazed across the green hills. Where he once had three thousand camels, now six thousand stood in long rows, their heads bobbing in the heat. He had a thousand yoke of oxen where before he had five hundred. And a thousand female donkeys where before he had five hundred.

The fields were full. The pastures stretched wide. The sound of animals filled the morning air — bleating, braying, the low call of oxen.

Then Job's brothers came to see him. His sisters came. Everyone who had known him before — neighbors, old friends, people from far away — they all came to his house.

They sat down and ate a meal with him. Bread was broken. Food was passed from hand to hand. The room was warm and full of voices.

Each person comforted Job. Each one spoke kind words about everything he had been through. And every one of them gave Job a gift — a piece of silver and a gold ring.

The silver clinked as it was set on the table. The gold rings caught the lamplight.

God also gave Job seven sons and three daughters.

The first daughter was named Jemimah. The second was named Keziah. The third was named Keren-happuch.

In all the land, there were no women as beautiful as Job's three daughters. He gave them an inheritance right along with their brothers.

Job lived one hundred and forty years after all of this. He saw his children grow. He saw his grandchildren born. He saw their children too, and even their children after that — four generations.

Great-grandchildren chased each other between the doorposts. Gold rings gleamed on weathered hands. Bread sat on the long table, and four generations of voices filled every room.
