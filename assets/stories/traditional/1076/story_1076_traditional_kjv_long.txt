Job had lost all. His cattle, his servants, his sons and his daughters — all gone. His body had been smitten with sore boils from the sole of his foot unto his crown. He had sat down among the ashes. He had scraped his skin with a potsherd. And through all of it, Job had spoken unto God.

He had cried out. He had asked questions. He had reasoned with his three friends who came and sat with him in the dust.

Now God had answered him. God had spoken out of the whirlwind — a great rushing, spinning wind that bent the air and stirred the ground. The voice of God came from within that wind, and the words shook Job to his bones.

God had asked Job question after question. Where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the earth? Canst thou command the morning? Knowest thou where the snow is kept? Canst thou send lightnings, that they may go?

Job had hearkened. He had heard every word.

Now Job opened his mouth to answer.

"I know that thou canst do every thing," Job said. His voice was quiet. "And that no thought of thine can be withholden from thee."

He paused. The wind had settled. Dust hung in the still air like a curtain slowly falling.

"Thou hast asked, 'Who is he that hideth counsel without knowledge?'" Job said. "I am the one. I have uttered that I understood not. Things too wonderful for me, which I knew not."

The ashes beneath his knees were soft and grey. They clung to his rent robe and to the raw patches upon his skin.

"Thou hast said, 'Hear, I beseech thee, and I will speak. I will demand of thee, and declare thou unto me,'" Job continued.

He bowed his face closer to the ground.

"I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear," Job said slowly. "But now — now mine eye seeth thee."

He laid both hands flat in the ash. His fingers pressed into the gritty powder. It was cool and fine beneath his palms.

"Wherefore I abhor myself," Job said unto God. "And repent in dust and ashes."

The air was still. Job abode where he was, his forehead near the ground, ash upon his hands, ash upon his robe, ash upon his head.

Then God spake again. But this time, God spake not unto Job.

God spake unto Eliphaz the Temanite. Eliphaz was one of Job's three friends who had travelled a long way to sit with him in the days of his affliction.

"My wrath is kindled against thee," God said, "and against thy two friends."

Eliphaz stood very still. Beside him, Bildad the Shuhite and Zophar the Naamathite moved not.

"For ye have not spoken of me the thing that is right," God said. "As my servant Job hath."

The three men looked one to another. The dry wind brushed across their robes.

"Therefore," God told Eliphaz, "take unto you now seven bullocks and seven rams. And go to my servant Job. And offer up for yourselves a burnt offering."

God continued. "And my servant Job shall pray for you. For him will I accept, lest I deal with you after your folly — in that ye have not spoken of me the thing which is right, like my servant Job."

So Eliphaz and Bildad and Zophar went and did even as the Lord had commanded them.

They gathered the beasts. Seven bullocks — large, dark-eyed beasts with wide backs. Seven rams — thick with wool, their hooves stirring dust as they walked. The friends led them carefully over the dry ground unto Job.

They builded a fire. The wood crackled. The flames grew hot and orange against the pale sky. They prepared each beast and made the burnt offering, one by one.

Smoke arose in a tall column. It climbed into the open sky above them, grey against blue, twisting as the breeze caught it.

And Job prayed for his three friends.

He prayed for Eliphaz. He prayed for Bildad. He prayed for Zophar. The same three men who had sat with him in the dust and reasoned with him and told him he must have done somewhat amiss — Job lifted up their names unto God.

The Lord accepted the prayer of Job.

And the Lord turned the captivity of Job, when he prayed for his friends.

The Lord also gave Job twice as much as he had before. Not back to what he once had. Twice over.

Where Job once had seven thousand sheep, now fourteen thousand sheep dotted the green hillsides. Their wool was white against the grass. The sound of bleating carried across the valleys in the early morning.

Where he once had three thousand camels, now six thousand camels stood in long rows, their heads swaying, their broad feet planted in the warm sand. They stretched as far as the eye could see.

Job had once owned five hundred yoke of oxen. Now he had a thousand. The great brown beasts pulled ploughs across fields of dark soil, their muscles rolling beneath their hides. Servants walked beside them, guiding them row after row.

And where he once had five hundred she asses, now a thousand grazed in open pastures, flicking their tails in the afternoon sun.

The land was full. The barns were full. The storehouses were full. Every fold, every pen, every field held more than it had before.

Then people began to come.

Job's brethren arrived first. They came down the road with their robes dusty from travel, their sandals worn. They embraced Job at his door.

His sisters came next. They held his hands. They beheld his face.

Then all they that had been of his acquaintance before came to visit. Old friends. Neighbours from far-off towns. Men who had heard what had befallen him.

They all came to his house, and they sat down together and did eat bread with him.

Bread was broken and passed from hand to hand round the long table. Bowls of meat were set out — lentils, parched corn, olives, dates. The chamber bore the savour of fresh bread and warm oil. Voices filled the house, one conversation layered upon another. Laughter came from the far end of the table.

Every one that came comforted Job. Every one spake kind words concerning all that he had gone through. Every one of them gave Job a token.

Each guest set a piece of money upon the table before him. The small flat coins clinked one against another as they were laid down. And every one gave him an earring of gold.

The rings caught the lamplight. The silver grew into a heap. Job beheld the faces of his kindred and his friends gathered round his table.

The Lord also gave Job seven sons and three daughters.

The name of the first daughter was Jemima. The name of the second was Kezia. The name of the third was Kerenhappuch.

In all the land were no women found so fair as the daughters of Job. Men spake of them wheresoever they went. Their eyes, their faces, the manner wherein they carried themselves — all marked them.

And Job gave them inheritance among their brethren. His daughters received a portion of all things, even as his sons did.

Job lived an hundred and forty years after all this.

He saw his children grow. He held his children's children when they were born — tiny and squirming, wrapped in soft cloth. He bounced them upon his old knees. He saw those grandchildren grow tall and strong. Then their children came, and their children after that.

Four generations.

Job was very old when he died. Full of days. Full of years.

The last evening light came through the door of his house and fell across the long table. Great-grandchildren sat upon the floor near his feet, pulling at one another's sleeves. Gold rings glinted upon the fingers of sons and daughters. A plate of bread sat half-eaten upon the table. Four generations of voices filled the chamber, and Job sat among them, his old hands resting upon his knees.
