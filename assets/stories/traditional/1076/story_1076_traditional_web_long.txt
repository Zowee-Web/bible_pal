Job had lost everything. His animals, his servants, his sons and his daughters — all gone. His body had been covered in painful sores from the bottom of his feet to the top of his head. He had sat in ashes. He had scraped his skin with a broken piece of pottery. And through all of it, Job had spoken to God.

He had cried out. He had asked questions. He had argued with his three friends who came and sat with him in the dust.

Now God had answered him. God had spoken out of a whirlwind — a great rushing, spinning wind that bent the air and stirred the ground. The voice of God came from inside that wind, and the words shook Job to his bones.

God had asked Job question after question. Where were you when I made the earth? Can you command the morning? Do you know where the snow is kept? Can you send lightning bolts on their way?

Job had listened. He had heard every word.

Now Job opened his mouth to answer.

"I know that you can do all things," Job said. His voice was quiet. "No purpose of yours can be stopped."

He paused. The wind had settled. Dust hung in the still air like a curtain slowly falling.

"You asked, 'Who is this that hides my plans without knowledge?'" Job said. "I am the one. I talked about things I did not understand. Things too wonderful for me to know."

The ashes beneath his knees were soft and gray. They clung to his torn robe and to the raw patches on his skin.

"You said, 'Listen now, and I will speak. I will question you, and you will answer me,'" Job continued.

He lowered his face closer to the ground.

"I had heard about you with my ears," Job said slowly. "But now — now my eyes have seen you."

He placed both hands flat in the ash. His fingers pressed into the gritty powder. It was cool and fine beneath his palms.

"I take back what I said," Job told God. "I sit here in dust and ashes."

The air was still. Job stayed where he was, his forehead close to the ground, ash on his hands, ash on his robe, ash in his hair.

Then God spoke again. But this time, God did not speak to Job.

God spoke to Eliphaz the Temanite. Eliphaz was one of Job's three friends who had traveled a long way to sit with him during his suffering.

"I am angry with you," God said, "and with your two friends."

Eliphaz stood very still. Beside him, Bildad the Shuhite and Zophar the Naamathite did not move.

"You did not speak what was right about me," God said. "My servant Job did."

The three men looked at each other. The dry wind brushed across their robes.

"Here is what you must do," God told Eliphaz. "Take seven bulls and seven rams. Go to my servant Job. Offer a burnt offering for yourselves."

God continued. "My servant Job will pray for you, and I will accept his prayer. I will not deal with you according to your foolishness — because you did not speak what was right about me, the way my servant Job did."

So Eliphaz and Bildad and Zophar went and did exactly what God commanded them.

They gathered the animals. Seven bulls — large, dark-eyed animals with wide backs. Seven rams — thick with wool, their hooves stirring dust as they walked. The friends led them carefully over the dry ground toward Job.

They built a fire. The wood crackled. The flames grew hot and orange against the pale sky. They prepared each animal and made the burnt offering, one by one.

Smoke rose in a tall column. It climbed into the open sky above them, gray against blue, twisting as the breeze caught it.

And Job prayed for his three friends.

He prayed for Eliphaz. He prayed for Bildad. He prayed for Zophar. The same three men who had sat with him in the dust and argued with him and told him he must have done something wrong — Job lifted their names up to God.

God accepted Job's prayer.

After Job prayed for his friends, something began to change.

God restored Job's fortunes. Not just back to what they were before. God gave Job twice as much as he had before.

Where Job once had seven thousand sheep, now fourteen thousand sheep dotted the green hillsides. Their wool was white against the grass. The sound of bleating carried across the valleys in the early morning.

Where he once had three thousand camels, now six thousand camels stood in long rows, their heads swaying, their broad feet planted in the warm sand. They stretched as far as the eye could see.

Job had once owned five hundred yoke of oxen. Now he had a thousand. The great brown animals pulled plows across fields of dark soil, their muscles rolling beneath their hides. Servants walked beside them, guiding them row after row.

And where he once had five hundred female donkeys, now a thousand grazed in open pastures, flicking their tails in the afternoon sun.

The land was full. The barns were full. The storehouses were full. Every paddock, every pen, every field held more than it had before.

Then people began to come.

Job's brothers arrived first. They came down the road with their robes dusty from travel, their sandals worn. They embraced Job at his door.

His sisters came next. They held his hands. They looked at his face.

Then everyone who had known Job before his suffering came to visit. Old friends. Neighbors from far-off towns. People who had heard what had happened to him.

They all came to his house, and they sat down together and ate a meal with him.

Bread was broken and passed from hand to hand around the long table. Bowls of food were set out — lentils, roasted grain, olives, dates. The room smelled of fresh bread and warm oil. Voices filled the house, one conversation layered on top of another. Laughter came from the far end of the table.

Every person who came comforted Job. They each spoke kind words about everything he had gone through. Every one of them gave Job a gift.

Each guest placed a piece of silver on the table in front of him. The small flat coins clinked against one another as they were set down. And each person gave him a gold ring.

The rings caught the lamplight. The silver grew into a pile. Job looked at the faces of his family and his friends gathered around his table.

God also gave Job seven sons and three daughters.

The first daughter was named Jemimah. The second was named Keziah. The third was named Keren-happuch.

In all the land, there were no women as beautiful as Job's three daughters. People talked about them wherever they went. Their eyes, their faces, the way they carried themselves — everyone noticed.

And Job gave them an inheritance right along with their brothers. His daughters received a share of everything, just as his sons did.

Job lived one hundred and forty years after all of this.

He watched his children grow. He held his grandchildren when they were born — tiny and squirming, wrapped in soft cloth. He bounced them on his old knees. He saw those grandchildren grow tall and strong. Then their children came, and their children after that.

Four generations.

Job was very old when he died. Full of days. Full of years.

The last evening light came through the doorway of his house and fell across the long table. Great-grandchildren sat on the floor near his feet, pulling at each other's sleeves. Gold rings glinted on the fingers of sons and daughters. A plate of bread sat half-eaten on the table. Four generations of voices filled the room, and Job sat among them, his old hands resting on his knees.
