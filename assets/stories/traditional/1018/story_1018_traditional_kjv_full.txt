Jacob went out from Beersheba and went toward Haran. He journeyed alone. The road ran northward through dry country, past low hills and open scrubland where the wind stirred through thorn bushes and the only sound was the scrape of sandals upon the packed earth. Behind him lay his father's tents, his mother's counsel, and a brother whose wrath he had kindled.

He lighted upon a certain place as the sun went down. The light was failing. The sky turned to copper at the edge of the world and deepened to blue above. He would go no farther that night. He halted and looked about the open ground — no shelter, no wall, no fire. Only the bare earth and the stones strewn across it.

He took of the stones of that place and put it for his pillow. He lay down upon the hard ground. The stone was rough against his skull, unshaped, cold. The night air settled about him. Stars appeared, one upon another, then in great arcs across the dark sky. He closed his eyes.

He dreamed.

And behold, a ladder set up on the earth, and the top of it reached to heaven. It rose from the very ground whereon he lay, climbing upward through the darkness into a light beyond beholding. The angels of God were ascending and descending on it — not in haste, not in tumult, but in steady and ordered procession. They moved between heaven and earth as though this traffic were perpetual, as though the way between the two had always stood open.

The Lord stood above it. He spake, and his voice bore the weight of covenant.

"I am the Lord God of Abraham thy father, and the God of Isaac. The land whereon thou liest, to thee will I give it, and to thy seed. Thy seed shall be as the dust of the earth, and thou shalt spread abroad to the west, and to the east, and to the north, and to the south. In thee and in thy seed shall all the families of the earth be blessed."

The promise was vast — the selfsame words spoken unto Abraham, now renewed unto Jacob upon this bare ground, beneath this open sky. And then God added yet more.

"Behold, I am with thee, and will keep thee in all places whither thou goest, and will bring thee again into this land. I will not leave thee, until I have done that which I have spoken to thee of."

Not a promise made upon condition. Not a reward for merit. A declaration. I am with thee. I will keep thee. I will bring thee again. I will not leave thee.

Jacob awaked out of his sleep. The stars yet stood above him. The stone yet lay beneath his head. The ground was yet hard, the air yet cold. But something had changed.

He said, "Surely the Lord is in this place, and I knew it not."

He was afraid. Not the fear of peril but the fear of one who standeth in a place far greater than he had known. He said, "How dreadful is this place! This is none other but the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven."

He rose up early in the morning. The light came pale across the eastern hills. He took the stone that he had put for his pillow and set it up for a pillar. He poured oil upon the top of it — a small act of consecration, marking that common stone as a thing set apart. He called the name of that place Bethel, which is to say, the house of God. Before this, the city had been called Luz.

Then Jacob vowed a vow. He spake it into the morning air, standing beside the anointed stone. "If God will be with me, and will keep me in this way that I go, and will give me bread to eat and raiment to put on, so that I come again to my father's house in peace, then shall the Lord be my God. This stone, which I have set for a pillar, shall be God's house. Of all that thou shalt give me, I will surely give the tenth unto thee."

The vow was simple. Bread and raiment and safe return. A tenth of all given back. The pillar stood in the morning light, dark with oil, a single stone upon open ground — marking the place where heaven had touched the earth whilst a man slept upon the bare ground with his head upon a rock.
