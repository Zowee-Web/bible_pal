Ezekiel had been shown the temple. The man with the measuring line, whose appearance was like the appearance of brass, had walked with him through every gate and every chamber. He had measured the courts and the porches and the rooms of the priests. He had taken the measurements of the doors, and of the side rooms, and of the inner sanctuary itself. He had set down for Ezekiel the shape of the house that would one day stand. The whole of it had taken many turns of the sun. The two of them had walked through it together as patiently as if they had had a thousand years.

Now, at the end of all the measuring, the man brought him back to the door of the house.

"Look," he said.

Ezekiel looked. And behold, water came out from under the threshold of the house eastward, for the front of the house faced toward the east. The water came down from under the right side of the house, on the south of the altar. It was a small flow, no wider than a hand. It moved over the smooth stone of the threshold, almost without sound, and ran down into the courtyard, and made its way out toward the gate.

He could have stepped across it without thinking about it.

The man brought him out by the way of the north gate, because the south side was where the altar stood and one did not pass that way. He led him around by the way outside to the outer gate that looked toward the east. The water was already there. It was running on the right side of the gate, going eastward, going down into the country beyond.

Then the man went out eastward with the line in his hand.

The Arabah lay below — the long dry rift that ran from the place of Jericho down to the Salt Sea. The wilderness of En Gedi. The dead place where no fish lived and no plant grew at the shore. Ezekiel could not yet see them from where he stood, but he knew the country.

The man measured a thousand cubits. He took Ezekiel by the hand. He caused him to pass through the water. The water was to the ankles.

It had grown.

The man measured another thousand. He caused him to pass through. The water was to the knees.

The man measured another thousand. The water was to the waist. Ezekiel could feel the cold of it against his ribs. He could feel the pull of the current. It had become a real river.

The man measured another thousand. And the water was now a river that he could not pass through. The water had risen further than he would have thought possible from such a thin flow over the threshold. It was a river to swim in. A river that could not be walked through. It went on east. The far bank was a swim away.

The man turned to him. "Son of man," he said, "have you seen this?"

Ezekiel said yes. He had seen it. He did not know what else to say.

The man brought him back along the bank of the river. They walked slowly. When Ezekiel had returned to the bank where he had begun, he saw what had not been there before. On the bank of the river were very many trees, on the one side and on the other. They had grown up while he walked. They stood close together in the wet ground. Their leaves moved in the air that came up off the river. The sound of the leaves was like the sound of the trees on the hills of the country he had been taken away from.

The man waited until Ezekiel had finished looking.

"These waters flow out toward the eastern region," he said. "They will go down into the Arabah. They will go to the sea — to the dead sea — and the waters of the sea will be healed. Every living creature which swarms will live wherever the river comes. There will be a very great multitude of fish, because these waters have come there, and the waters of the sea will be healed, and everything will live wherever the river comes.

"Fishermen will stand on its banks. From En Gedi even to En Eglaim it will be a place to spread nets. Their fish will be after their kinds, as the fish of the great sea, exceedingly many."

He paused. He looked at Ezekiel.

"The miry places and the marshes will not be healed. They will be given to salt. Some of the land will keep its old work. But the river — the river is not for that."

He gestured to the trees on either bank.

"By the river on its banks, on this side and on that side, will grow every tree for food. Their leaves will not wither, and their fruit will not fail. They will bring forth new fruit every month, because the waters issue out of the sanctuary. Their fruit will be for food, and their leaves for healing."

The river ran past Ezekiel's feet, going east, going down toward the dead place, carrying the trees of healing along both its banks. He stood and watched it for a long time. The water had begun as a trickle at the door of the house. It had grown four times in four thousand cubits. Where it ended in the sea, the sea would live. The fish would come back. The fishermen would stand at En Gedi.

He thought of the country he had been taken away from. He thought of the river of Babylon by which he had sat down and wept. He thought of his own people, far from here, far from any temple, hearing nothing but the news of what was lost.

He stood at the bank, and he watched the river, and he watched the trees on each side moving in the air that came up off the water. And so it was shown to him, in the vision of the man with the measuring line, what the threshold of the house would one day pour out.
