Ezekiel had been shown the temple. The man with the measuring line, whose appearance was as the appearance of brass, had walked with him through every gate and every chamber. He had measured the courts and the porches and the rooms of the priests. He had taken the measurements of the doors, and of the side chambers, and of the inner sanctuary itself. He had set down for Ezekiel the shape of the house that should one day stand. The whole of it had taken many turns of the sun. The two of them had walked through it together as patiently as if they had had a thousand years.

Now, at the end of all the measuring, the man brought him again unto the door of the house.

"Look," he said.

Ezekiel looked. And behold, waters issued out from under the threshold of the house eastward, for the forefront of the house stood toward the east. And the waters came down from under from the right side of the house, at the south side of the altar. It was a small flow, no wider than a hand. It moved over the smooth stone of the threshold, almost without sound, and ran down into the courtyard, and made its way out toward the gate.

He could have stepped over it without taking thought.

The man brought him out of the way of the gate northward, because the south side was where the altar stood and one passed not that way. He led him about the way without unto the utter gate by the way that looketh eastward. And, behold, there ran out waters on the right side, going eastward, going down into the country beyond.

Then the man that had the line in his hand went forth eastward.

The desert lay below — the long dry rift that ran from the place of Jericho down unto the salt sea. The wilderness of En-gedi. The dead place where no fish lived and no plant grew at the shore. Ezekiel could not yet see them from where he stood, but he knew the country.

The man measured a thousand cubits. He took Ezekiel by the hand. He brought him through the waters. The waters were to the ankles.

They had grown.

He measured a thousand. He brought him through. The waters were to the knees.

He measured a thousand. The waters were to the loins. Ezekiel could feel the cold of them against his side. He could feel the pull of the current. They had become a real river.

Afterward he measured a thousand. And the waters were now a river that he could not pass over. The waters had risen further than he would have thought possible from such a thin flow over the threshold. Waters to swim in. A river that could not be passed over. It went on east. The far bank was a swim away.

The man turned unto him. "Son of man," he said, "hast thou seen this?"

Ezekiel said yea. He had seen it. He knew not what else to say.

The man brought him, and caused him to return to the brink of the river. They walked slowly. When he had returned unto the brink where he had begun, he saw what had not been there before. At the bank of the river were very many trees, on the one side and on the other. They had grown up while he walked. They stood close together in the wet ground. Their leaves moved in the air that came up off the river. The sound of the leaves was as the sound of the trees on the hills of the country he had been taken away from.

The man waited until Ezekiel had finished looking.

"These waters issue out toward the east country," he said, "and go down into the desert, and go into the sea. Which being brought forth into the sea, the waters shall be healed. And it shall come to pass, that every thing that liveth, which moveth, whithersoever the rivers shall come, shall live. And there shall be a very great multitude of fish, because these waters shall come thither. For they shall be healed, and every thing shall live whither the river cometh.

"And it shall come to pass, that the fishers shall stand upon it from En-gedi even unto En-eglaim. They shall be a place to spread forth nets. Their fish shall be according to their kinds, as the fish of the great sea, exceeding many."

He paused. He looked at Ezekiel.

"But the miry places thereof and the marishes thereof shall not be healed. They shall be given to salt. Some of the land shall keep its old work. But the river — the river is not for that."

He gestured unto the trees on either bank.

"And by the river upon the bank thereof, on this side and on that side, shall grow all trees for meat, whose leaf shall not fade, neither shall the fruit thereof be consumed. It shall bring forth new fruit according to his months, because their waters they issued out of the sanctuary. And the fruit thereof shall be for meat, and the leaf thereof for medicine."

The river ran past Ezekiel's feet, going east, going down toward the dead place, bearing the trees of healing along both its banks. He stood and beheld it a long time. The water had begun as a trickle at the door of the house. It had grown four times in four thousand cubits. Where it ended in the sea, the sea should live. The fish should come back. The fishers should stand at En-gedi.

He thought upon the country he had been taken away from. He thought upon the river of Babylon by which he had sat down and wept. He thought upon his own people, far from there, far from any temple, hearing nothing but the news of what was lost.

He stood at the bank, and he beheld the river, and he beheld the trees on each side moving in the air that came up off the water. And so it was shown unto him, in the vision of the man with the measuring line, what the threshold of the house should one day pour forth.
