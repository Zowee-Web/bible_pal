King David sat upon his throne in Jerusalem. The stone chamber was still. The guards stood along the walls. The light from the high windows fell across the floor.

David spake.

"Is there yet any that is left of the house of Saul," David asked, "that I may shew him kindness for Jonathan's sake?"

A servant whose name was Ziba was brought in. He had been a servant of the house of Saul.

"Is there not yet any of the house of Saul?" David asked again.

Ziba bowed his head. "Jonathan hath yet a son. His name is Mephibosheth. He is lame on his feet."

When Mephibosheth was but five years old, evil tidings had come — Jonathan and Saul were slain. The Philistines were prevailing. All men were sore afraid. Mephibosheth's nurse had taken him up and fled. But in her haste she stumbled, or he slipped from her arms, and the little child fell hard upon the ground. Both his feet were sore hurt. He never walked aright thereafter.

"Where is he?" David asked.

"He dwelleth in Lo-debar." A small and dusty place beyond the Jordan.

"Bring him unto me," David said.

Servants rode forth unto Lo-debar and brought Mephibosheth back unto Jerusalem. The journey was long. Mephibosheth could not walk of himself — a man bare him, or set him upon an ass. The way was dusty and the sun was hot.

The doors of the palace were opened. Mephibosheth was brought within. The hall was long and cool, the floor of smooth stone. He beheld the throne at the far end. He beheld the guards standing at attention on either side. He beheld David sitting there, watching as he was brought nigh.

He fell on his face to the ground. His lame feet were behind him. His whole body trembled.

"Mephibosheth," David said.

A small voice answered from the floor. "Behold thy servant."

David said, "Fear not: for I will surely shew thee kindness for Jonathan thy father's sake. I will restore thee all the land of Saul thy grandfather. And thou shalt eat bread at my table continually — all the days of thy life — even as one of mine own sons."

He bowed his head low, his forehead against the cool stone. "What is thy servant," he whispered, "that thou shouldest look upon such a dead dog as I am? I am nothing."

So Mephibosheth came to dwell in Jerusalem. Servants were appointed to till his land and to bring in the increase for his household. But Mephibosheth himself did eat at the king's table — the same table whereat David's own sons did sit, where the chief men of the kingdom were gathered.

Every evening, the servants set forth the meat — bread and roasted flesh, bowls of lentiles, cups of wine, dishes of figs and honey. The wooden chargers steamed. The lamps of oil flickered along the walls. The princes took their places. The officers sat down. And Mephibosheth was carried in and set in his seat.

A man that could not walk. A man of a house that had lost all.

Sitting at the king's table. Eating the king's bread. His lame feet hidden beneath the tablecloth, and his place set hard by the princes.
