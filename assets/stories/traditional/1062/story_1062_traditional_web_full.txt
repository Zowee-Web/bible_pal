King David sat on his throne in Jerusalem, and he was thinking. Not about battles or laws or the business of running a kingdom. He was thinking about his friend.

Jonathan.

Jonathan had been David's best friend in the whole world. They had made a promise to each other long ago — a covenant, which is the strongest kind of promise there is. They had promised to take care of each other's families, no matter what happened, forever.

But Jonathan was gone now. He had died in a battle on Mount Gilboa, along with his father, King Saul. That was years ago. David had become king. Life had moved on. But David had not forgotten.

"Is there anyone left from Saul's family," David asked, "that I can show kindness to — for Jonathan's sake?"

His servants looked at each other. People from the old king's family? That was unusual. When a new king took over, the old king's relatives usually had to hide. They were afraid of being punished or worse.

A servant named Ziba was brought in. He had worked for Saul's family for many years and knew their secrets.

"Is there anyone left?" David asked again.

Ziba nodded. "There is one. Jonathan's son. His name is Mephibosheth. He is lame in both feet."

When Mephibosheth was just five years old, terrible news had come — Jonathan and Saul were dead. The Philistines were winning. Everyone panicked. Mephibosheth's nurse had grabbed him and started running. But in her hurry, she tripped or he slipped, and the little boy fell hard. Both his feet were crushed. He never walked right again.

"Where is he?" David asked.

"He lives in Lo-debar." A small, dusty, faraway place out beyond the Jordan River. The grandson of a king, living in the middle of nowhere, forgotten.

"Bring him to me," David said.

Servants rode out to Lo-debar and brought Mephibosheth back to Jerusalem. The journey must have been terrifying. Mephibosheth couldn't walk on his own — someone had to carry him or help him onto a donkey. And the whole way, mile after dusty mile, he must have been thinking: Why does the king want me? Is he angry? Am I in trouble? When kings sent for people from their enemies' families, it almost never meant something good.

The doors of the palace opened. Mephibosheth was brought inside. The hallway was long and cool, the floor made of smooth stone. He saw the throne at the far end. He saw the guards standing at attention on either side. He saw David sitting there, watching him come closer.

He fell flat on his face on the stone floor. His damaged feet were behind him. His whole body trembled.

"Mephibosheth," David said.

A tiny voice answered from the floor. "Here I am. Your servant."

David didn't look angry. He didn't look threatening. He looked kind. And what he said next changed Mephibosheth's life forever.

"Don't be afraid," David said. "I am going to show you kindness for the sake of your father Jonathan. I'm going to give back all the land that belonged to your grandfather Saul. And you — you will eat at my table. Every day. For the rest of your life. Like one of my own sons."

Mephibosheth couldn't understand it. He bowed his head low, his forehead against the cool stone. "What am I," he whispered, "that you would even notice me? I am nothing — like a dead dog."

A dead dog. That's what he called himself. The lowest, most worthless thing he could think of. He truly believed that about himself — that he was broken, unimportant, someone the world had thrown away.

But David just looked at him and repeated what he had said. The king's word stood.

So Mephibosheth moved to Jerusalem. Servants were assigned to farm his land and bring in food for his household. But Mephibosheth himself ate at the king's table — the same table where David's own sons sat, where the most important people in the kingdom gathered.

Every evening, the servants set out the food — bread and roasted meat, bowls of lentils, cups of wine, dishes of figs and honey. The wooden platters steamed. The oil lamps flickered along the walls. The princes took their seats. The officials sat down. And Mephibosheth was carried in and placed at his chair.

A man who couldn't walk. A man from a family that had lost everything. A man who had called himself a dead dog.

Sitting at the king's table. Eating the king's bread. His damaged feet hidden beneath the tablecloth, and his place set right alongside the princes.
