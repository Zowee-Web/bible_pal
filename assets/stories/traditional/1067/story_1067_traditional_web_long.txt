In the year that King Uzziah died, Isaiah came into the temple of the Lord.

The temple was large and still. Stone columns lined the great hall in two rows, rising from the polished floor into the high ceiling where the light barely reached. Each column was thick enough that two men could not have wrapped their arms around it. The surfaces were smooth, cut and fitted by hands long since gone to dust.

The air inside smelled of incense. It was layered — the sharp bite of frankincense on top, the heavier sweetness of something older beneath it, the residue of many offerings burned on the altar over many days. The smell clung to the stone and to the fabric hangings along the walls. It settled into clothing and hair. It did not leave when you left.

The floor was cool underfoot. The stones had been worn smooth by years of sandaled feet, their edges softened, their surfaces carrying the faintest sheen of oil from the skin of those who had knelt upon them.

Light came in from high above, through narrow openings near the roofline. The beams fell at angles through the dim air, catching the dust and the faint haze of incense smoke that never fully cleared. The corners of the temple sat in shadow. The center held a muted glow.

Then Isaiah saw the Lord.

He was seated on a throne, high and lifted up, far above the floor of the temple. The throne was immense. Its height reached into the upper darkness where the ceiling beams crossed. The Lord sat upon it, and the train of his robe fell from the seat of the throne downward, spreading out in vast folds of heavy fabric that covered the steps and cascaded across the floor. The cloth spread from wall to wall. It covered the polished stones entirely. The folds of it lay deep and still, like water that has stopped moving.

The train of his robe filled the temple.

Above the throne stood seraphim.

Each one had six wings. With two wings each covered its face — folded close, the feathers layered tight, hiding whatever was behind them. With two wings each covered its feet — drawn down and crossed, the tips touching. With the remaining two, each one flew. They hovered above the throne in steady motion, their wings beating in a rhythm that was slow and constant, the sound of them like wind through a narrow mountain pass — low, unbroken, pressing against the ears.

The seraphim moved in the space above the throne. Their forms were partially hidden by the smoke that hung in the upper reaches of the temple. Their wings caught the faint light and released it. They turned and hovered and turned again.

Then one called to another. The voice filled the temple.

"Holy, holy, holy is the Lord of hosts."

The sound struck the stone walls and came back. It pressed against the columns. It vibrated in the floor beneath Isaiah's feet. The air itself seemed to tighten and hold.

"The whole earth is full of his glory."

The doorposts of the temple shook. The massive stone thresholds trembled in their fittings, grinding against the floor with a low sound that rose through the soles of the feet and into the bones. Dust fell from the crevices between the stones overhead. Small fragments of mortar dropped and shattered silently against the folds of the robe that covered the floor. The foundations themselves seemed to shift beneath the weight of the cry, as though the temple had been struck from below.

Smoke filled the temple.

It rolled in from the altar in thick white clouds, pouring between the columns and rising toward the ceiling. It swallowed the far walls. It blurred the edges of the columns nearest the altar first, then spread outward until the entire hall was thick with it. The seraphim above moved in and out of the haze, their wings appearing and disappearing as the smoke shifted. The outline of the throne softened. The folds of the robe on the floor became pale shapes in a white sea.

The smoke carried the smell of the altar — charred wood, burned fat, the mineral sharpness of heated stone. It pressed against the eyes and throat. It dimmed the light from the high windows until only a faint glow remained, diffused and directionless, as though the temple were lit from within the smoke itself.

Isaiah cried out.

His voice broke sharp against the stone and the heavy air.

"Woe is me!"

He stood in the middle of the temple, small beneath the throne, surrounded by smoke. His hands were open at his sides. His face was turned upward toward the seraphim and the throne above. The smoke moved around him in slow currents.

"I am undone!"

The word echoed off the columns and returned. The seraphim continued to hover above, their wings beating, their voices silent now. The smoke continued to pour from the altar.

"I am a man of unclean lips," Isaiah said. His voice was raw and unsteady. "And I dwell in the midst of a people of unclean lips — for my eyes have seen the King, the Lord of hosts."

The words hung in the smoky air. The temple was quiet except for the low sound of the seraphim's wings and the faint grinding of the doorposts, which had not fully stilled.

Then one of the seraphim moved.

It left its place above the throne. It descended through the smoke with its wings spread wide — two still covering its face, two still covering its feet. The remaining two carried it forward and downward, beating the heavy air with slow, deliberate strokes. The smoke scattered in trailing wisps where the wings passed through it, curling and re-forming behind the seraph as it flew.

In its hand it held a live coal.

The coal glowed bright against the dimness of the temple. At its center it burned orange and white, pulsing with heat, the edges dark red and crumbling. The seraph had taken it from the altar with tongs — lifted it from among the burning embers where the sacrifices were consumed, where the wood had been reduced to white ash and the stones of the altar were black with soot. The tongs held the coal firmly. The heat of it pushed against the air ahead, and the smoke nearest the coal thinned and pulled away, as though the fire were clearing a path for itself.

The seraph descended until it was before Isaiah. Its wings moved slowly, holding it in place. The glow of the coal lit the space between them — casting orange light across Isaiah's face, across his robes, across the smoke that drifted between his body and the hovering seraph.

The seraph extended the coal toward Isaiah's face.

The heat came first. Isaiah could feel it against his chin and cheeks before the coal arrived. The glow brightened as it neared. The light of it filled his eyes.

Then the seraph pressed the coal against his mouth.

The burning touched his lips. The live coal sat against the skin of his mouth, held there by the hand of the seraph. The heat was immediate and total. The glow lit his face from below — his closed eyes, the bridge of his nose, the creases at the corners of his mouth.

The seraph spoke.

"Behold. This has touched your lips. Your iniquity is taken away, and your sin is purged."

The seraph withdrew the coal. The glow pulled back from Isaiah's face. The orange light receded, and the dimness of the smoke-filled temple returned. The seraph rose again on steady wings, climbing through the layers of smoke, passing through the haze until its form blurred and joined the other seraphim above the throne.

Isaiah stood where he was. The place on his lips where the coal had pressed was still warm. The taste of fire and ash sat on his tongue. His hands hung at his sides. The smoke continued to drift through the temple, moving between the columns in slow currents, thinning near the floor and thickening near the ceiling. The train of the robe still covered the stones beneath his feet. The doorposts had stopped shaking, but the memory of the trembling still seemed to live in the walls, in the columns, in the floor.

The temple was quiet.

The seraphim hovered above. The smoke drifted. The coal on the altar continued to glow, smaller now, dimmer, settling into its bed of ash.

Then a voice came through the smoke.

It was the voice of the Lord, speaking from the throne above. The words filled the temple the way the seraphim's cry had filled it — pressing into every corner, settling into the stone, vibrating in the columns and the floor. The smoke swirled faintly in the stillness that followed each word.

"Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?"

The question hung in the air. The smoke moved. The seraphim's wings beat their slow rhythm above. The glow of the altar pulsed once in the haze.

Isaiah stood in the temple with his face turned upward, the mark of the coal still on his lips, the smoke curling around his feet and rising past his shoulders. He opened his mouth and spoke.

"Here am I. Send me."
