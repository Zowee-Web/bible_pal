In the year that king Uzziah died, Isaiah came into the temple of the Lord.

The temple was great and still. Pillars of stone stood in two rows along the great hall, rising from the polished floor into the high ceiling where the light scarce reached. Each pillar was of such girth that two men could not have compassed it about with their arms. The faces thereof were smooth, hewn and fitted by hands long since returned unto dust.

The air within smelled of incense. It was as layers — the sharp bite of frankincense upon the top, the heavier sweetness of somewhat more ancient beneath it, the remnant of many offerings burnt upon the altar over many days. The smell clung unto the stone and unto the hangings of fabric along the walls. It settled into raiment and into the hair. It departed not when thou didst depart.

The floor was cool beneath the feet. The stones had been worn smooth by years of sandaled feet, their edges softened, their faces bearing the faintest sheen of oil from the skin of them that had kneeled upon them.

Light entered from on high, through narrow openings nigh unto the roofline. The beams fell at angles through the dim air, catching the dust and the faint haze of incense smoke that never fully cleared. The corners of the temple sat in shadow. The midst thereof held a muted glow.

Then Isaiah saw the Lord.

He was seated upon a throne, high and lifted up, far above the floor of the temple. The throne was of great immensity. The height thereof reached into the upper darkness where the ceiling beams crossed. The Lord sat upon it, and the train of his robe fell from the seat of the throne downward, spreading out in vast folds of heavy fabric that covered the steps and poured forth across the floor. The cloth spread from wall to wall. It covered the polished stones entirely. The folds thereof lay deep and still, as waters that have ceased from moving.

The train of his robe filled the temple.

Above the throne stood seraphim.

Each one had six wings. With twain each covered his face — folded close, the feathers layered fast, hiding whatsoever was behind them. With twain each covered his feet — drawn down and crossed, the tips thereof touching. With the twain that remained, each one did fly. They hovered above the throne in steady motion, their wings beating in a measure that was slow and constant, the sound thereof like wind through a narrow pass of the mountains — low, unbroken, pressing against the ears.

The seraphim moved in the space above the throne. Their forms were in part hidden by the smoke that hung in the upper reaches of the temple. Their wings caught the faint light and gave it forth again. They turned and hovered and turned again.

Then one cried unto another. The voice filled the temple.

"Holy, holy, holy, is the Lord of hosts."

The sound smote the walls of stone and returned. It pressed against the pillars. It did shake in the floor beneath the feet of Isaiah. The air itself seemed to draw tight and hold.

"The whole earth is full of his glory."

The posts of the door did shake. The great thresholds of stone trembled in their fittings, grinding against the floor with a low sound that rose through the soles of the feet and into the bones. Dust fell from the crevices between the stones overhead. Small fragments of mortar dropped and brake silently against the folds of the robe that covered the floor. The very foundations seemed to shift beneath the weight of the cry, as though the temple had been smitten from below.

Smoke filled the temple.

It rolled in from the altar in thick white clouds, pouring between the pillars and rising toward the ceiling. It swallowed up the far walls. It blurred the edges of the pillars nearest the altar first, then spread outward until the whole of the hall was thick with it. The seraphim above moved in and out of the haze, their wings appearing and vanishing as the smoke shifted. The form of the throne grew soft. The folds of the robe upon the floor became pale shapes in a white sea.

The smoke bare with it the smell of the altar — charred wood, burnt fat, the sharp bitterness of heated stone. It pressed against the eyes and the throat. It dimmed the light from the high windows until only a faint glow remained, spread abroad and without direction, as though the temple were lit from within the smoke itself.

Isaiah cried out.

His voice brake sharp against the stone and the heavy air.

"Woe is me!"

He stood in the midst of the temple, small beneath the throne, compassed about with smoke. His hands were open at his sides. His face was turned upward toward the seraphim and the throne above. The smoke moved about him in slow currents.

"I am undone!"

The word echoed from the pillars and returned. The seraphim continued to hover above, their wings beating, their voices silent now. The smoke continued to pour forth from the altar.

"I am a man of unclean lips," Isaiah said. His voice was raw and unsteady. "And I dwell in the midst of a people of unclean lips — for mine eyes have seen the King, the Lord of hosts."

The words hung in the smoky air. The temple was quiet save for the low sound of the wings of the seraphim and the faint grinding of the doorposts, which had not fully stilled.

Then one of the seraphim moved.

It left its place above the throne. It descended through the smoke with its wings spread wide — twain still covering its face, twain still covering its feet. The twain that remained bare it forward and downward, beating the heavy air with slow and deliberate strokes. The smoke scattered in trailing wisps where the wings passed through it, curling and gathering again behind the seraph as it flew.

In its hand it held a live coal.

The coal glowed bright against the dimness of the temple. At the heart thereof it burned orange and white, throbbing with heat, the edges dark red and crumbling. The seraph had taken it from the altar with the tongs — lifted it from among the burning embers where the sacrifices were consumed, where the wood had been brought to white ash and the stones of the altar were black with soot. The tongs held the coal fast. The heat thereof pressed against the air before it, and the smoke nearest the coal grew thin and drew back, as though the fire were clearing a way for itself.

The seraph descended until it was before Isaiah. Its wings moved slowly, holding it in its place. The glow of the coal gave light unto the space between them — casting orange light across the face of Isaiah, across his robes, across the smoke that drifted between his body and the seraph that hovered before him.

The seraph put forth the coal toward the face of Isaiah.

The heat came first. Isaiah could feel it against his chin and his cheeks ere the coal arrived. The glow brightened as it drew nigh. The light thereof filled his eyes.

Then the seraph laid the coal upon his mouth.

The burning touched his lips. The live coal sat against the skin of his mouth, held there by the hand of the seraph. The heat was immediate and whole. The glow lit his face from beneath — his closed eyes, the bridge of his nose, the creases at the corners of his mouth.

The seraph spake.

"Lo, this hath touched thy lips; and thine iniquity is taken away, and thy sin purged."

The seraph withdrew the coal. The glow drew back from the face of Isaiah. The orange light receded, and the dimness of the smoke-filled temple returned. The seraph rose again upon steady wings, climbing through the layers of smoke, passing through the haze until its form grew dim and joined the other seraphim above the throne.

Isaiah stood where he was. The place upon his lips where the coal had pressed was yet warm. The taste of fire and ash sat upon his tongue. His hands hung at his sides. The smoke continued to drift through the temple, moving between the pillars in slow currents, thinning nigh the floor and thickening nigh the ceiling. The train of the robe still covered the stones beneath his feet. The doorposts had ceased from shaking, but the memory of the trembling seemed yet to abide in the walls, in the pillars, in the floor.

The temple was quiet.

The seraphim hovered above. The smoke drifted. The coal upon the altar continued to glow, smaller now, dimmer, settling into its bed of ash.

Then a voice came through the smoke.

It was the voice of the Lord, speaking from the throne above. The words filled the temple as the cry of the seraphim had filled it — pressing into every corner, settling into the stone, shaking in the pillars and in the floor. The smoke swirled faintly in the stillness that followed each word.

"Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?"

The question hung in the air. The smoke moved. The wings of the seraphim beat their slow measure above. The glow of the altar throbbed once in the haze.

Isaiah stood in the temple with his face turned upward, the mark of the coal yet upon his lips, the smoke curling about his feet and rising past his shoulders. He opened his mouth and spake.

"Here am I; send me."
