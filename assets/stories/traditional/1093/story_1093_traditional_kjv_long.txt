The house of the Pharisee stood upon one of the better streets of the town. The walls were thick and white. The doorway was wide, and the chamber within was lit with oil lamps set in niches along the walls. A low table ran the length of the room, laid about with bread, olives, roasted grain, herbs, and cups of wine. Cushions were set along both sides, and the guests reclined in the accustomed manner — upon their left sides, leaning upon their elbows, their feet stretched behind them.

Simon the Pharisee had bidden Jesus to sup with him. It was not altogether an act of warmth. The Pharisees were given to testing teachers — watching their conduct, hearkening for errors, weighing whether their authority was sound. Simon had set the table, but he had not shown the common courtesies of a generous host. No water for the feet. No kiss of greeting. No oil for the head. The invitation was an observation, not a welcome.

Jesus reclined at the table. The talk moved in careful, measured tones. The other guests leaned in, listening, assessing.

Then a woman came in from the street.

She was known in the city. Known as a sinner — the word bore its full weight, and none needed ask its meaning. She had not been bidden. She had no place at this table, no right to be within this house. Yet she came — moving through the doorway into the lamplight, bearing in her hands a small alabaster box of ointment.

She made her way to where Jesus lay reclined, and stood behind him, nigh unto his feet.

She was weeping. Not softly — deep, shaking sobs that she could not stay. The tears fell from her face and landed upon his feet, wetting the dust that covered the skin. She kneeled. Her hair, loosed about her shoulders — a sign of intimacy that would have shocked every soul in the room — fell forward as she bent over his feet. She began to wipe the tears with the hairs of her head. She kissed his feet. She kissed them again. Then she opened the box and poured the ointment upon them.

The smell filled the room. It was rich and costly — the manner of perfume that a woman might save for many years. It spread through the air as a wave, reaching every corner, impossible to ignore.

The room was silent. The other guests stared. Some turned away their faces. Simon watched with sharp, reckoning attention. He spake nothing aloud, but his thoughts were plain upon his face.

He said within himself: "This man, if he were a prophet, would have known who and what manner of woman this is that toucheth him: for she is a sinner."

Jesus turned unto him.

"Simon," said he, "I have somewhat to say unto thee."

Simon stiffened. "Master, say on."

"There was a certain creditor which had two debtors: the one owed five hundred pence, and the other fifty. And when they had nothing to pay, he frankly forgave them both." Jesus paused. "Tell me therefore, which of them will love him most?"

Simon weighed the question. He answered carefully, as a man doth who suspecteth the answer to be a snare. "I suppose that he, to whom he forgave most."

"Thou hast rightly judged," said Jesus.

Then he turned toward the woman. She was yet kneeling at his feet, her hair damp, her hands clasped about the flask, her face wet with tears. Jesus turned not his eyes from her, though he spake unto Simon.

"Seest thou this woman?" said he. "I entered into thine house, thou gavest me no water for my feet: but she hath washed my feet with tears, and wiped them with the hairs of her head. Thou gavest me no kiss: but this woman since the time I came in hath not ceased to kiss my feet. My head with oil thou didst not anoint: but this woman hath anointed my feet with ointment."

Each comparison fell upon the silent room as a stone cast into still water. Simon said nothing. His mouth was shut. His hands were still.

"Wherefore I say unto thee," said Jesus, "Her sins, which are many, are forgiven; for she loved much: but to whom little is forgiven, the same loveth little."

Then he turned unto the woman. He looked upon her directly — not past her, not through her, but at her. Into her face.

"Thy sins are forgiven," he said.

The others at the table stirred. Whispers moved about the room. "Who is this," they murmured, "that forgiveth sins also?"

But Jesus turned not unto them. He kept his eyes upon the woman. His voice was gentle and steady.

"Thy faith hath saved thee," he said. "Go in peace."

The woman rose. She gathered the flask. She stood a moment in the lamplight, her face yet wet, her hair disheveled, the room full of the fragrance she had poured forth. Then she turned and went back through the doorway, out into the street, into the evening air. The door closed softly behind her.

The smell of the ointment lingered in the room long after she was gone.
