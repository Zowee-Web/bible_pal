For forty years the hand of the Philistines had been heavy on the land. The children of Israel had again done evil in the sight of the Lord, and the Lord had given them over. The Philistine garrisons sat in the lowlands. The roads carried their taxes. The villages along the hill country bent under it and went on bending. In the town of Zorah, in the tribe of Dan, a man named Manoah lived in a small house on the edge of the fields. His wife was barren. She had carried that quiet weight for years.

She was alone when the angel of the Lord first came to her.

There was no fanfare. There was no warning. She had gone about her work as she did each morning, and then a man was standing where no man had been, and the air around him was different. He spoke, and his voice was even.

"Behold, you shall conceive and bear a son. Drink no wine or strong drink. Eat no unclean thing. The child shall be a Nazirite to God from the womb. No razor shall come on his head. He shall begin to save Israel out of the hand of the Philistines."

She did not ask his name. She did not ask where he was from. She listened until he was done. Then he was no longer there.

She ran.

She went up across the field and through the gate of the house, and the words came out of her in one breath when she found Manoah. "A man of God came to me. His face was like the face of the angel of God, very awesome. I did not ask him where he was from. He did not tell me his name. He said I should conceive and bear a son, and that the child shall be a Nazirite to God from the womb to the day of his death."

Manoah set down what he was holding. He looked at his wife. He had heard her voice steady and her voice tired and her voice low, but he had not heard it like this. He went out into the open and he prayed.

"Oh, Lord, please let the man of God whom you sent come again to us, and teach us what we should do for the child to come."

He waited there a long time. The wind moved across the field. The flocks of the village went out and came back. The sun lowered. He went back into the house. He waited the next day, and the day after.

The Lord heard the voice of Manoah.

And the angel of God came again. He came again to the woman, and again she was alone. She was sitting in the field when she lifted her eyes and saw him standing there. Her breath caught. She rose to her feet and turned and ran a second time across the same ground, and her sandals beat the dirt on the path.

She found Manoah and pulled him by the sleeve. "Behold, the man who came to me that day has appeared to me." She did not wait for an answer. She turned again and began to go back, and Manoah went after her.

He came up to the man where he stood waiting in the field. He stopped a few paces off. He studied his face. "Are you the man who spoke to my wife?"

"I am."

Manoah came closer. The words he had prepared faltered. "Now let your words come to pass. What shall be the child's way of life? What shall be his mission?"

The angel did not lengthen the answer. "Let her observe all that I commanded the woman. She may not eat of anything that comes of the vine. Let her drink no wine or strong drink. Let her eat no unclean thing. Let her observe all that I commanded her."

Manoah heard it. He nodded, slowly. He had asked, and he had been told that what was for the woman was for the woman. He felt the duty of a host rise in him. He could not let the man go without a meal.

"Please stay with us," he said. "We will prepare a young goat for you."

The angel's answer came back without delay. "Though you detain me, I will not eat of your bread. If you prepare a burnt offering, you must offer it to the Lord."

Manoah did not yet understand who stood before him. He pressed once more. "What is your name? When your words come to pass, we will honor you."

The angel looked at him. The look was not unkind. "Why do you ask my name? It is wonderful."

So Manoah went and took the young goat with the meal offering, and he laid them on the rock there in the field. He set the fire beneath them. He stepped back. His wife stood beside him. The wood caught. The flame rose up from the rock toward the sky.

And the angel of the Lord ascended in the flame.

Manoah saw it before he understood it. The figure that had stood beside them rose into the rising fire — lit from within it, lifting with it, going up as the flame went up. The flame climbed. The angel climbed with it. The light reached high above the rock. Then the flame thinned. Then the flame was gone. And the rock stood empty, smoking, in the bare field.

Manoah and his wife fell on their faces to the ground.

They stayed there a long time. The earth was warm against them. The smell of the burning wood drifted on the air and faded. Somewhere on the hill a single bird called twice and was still. Neither of them moved.

The angel did not appear to them again.

When at last Manoah lifted his face, the light had changed. He looked at the empty rock and then at his wife. His voice came out thin. "We shall surely die, because we have seen God."

But his wife was already sitting up. She did not look at the rock. She looked at him. Her face was wet with the dust of the ground, and she was not afraid.

"If the Lord were pleased to kill us," she said, "he would not have received a burnt offering and a meal offering at our hand. He would not have shown us all these things. He would not have told us such things at this time."

She said it the way a person says what is plainly true. She did not raise her voice. She did not soften it. She set the words down between them and let them sit.

Manoah listened. The fear in his chest did not leave at once, but his wife's words moved through it and held still inside it. He looked at her across the cooling ash on the rock and saw that she had heard the angel before he had, and she had heard him better. He nodded, once. He helped her to her feet. They walked together back across the field as the evening came on.

The child came in its season. She bore a son and named him Samson. The boy grew in the small house at Zorah. He grew strong in the hills above it. The Lord blessed him.

And in time the Spirit of the Lord began to move on him, in the camp of Dan, between Zorah and Eshtaol, where the long evenings settled over the open hills and the wind ran low across the ground his mother had once crossed at a run to tell her husband of a voice she could not name.
