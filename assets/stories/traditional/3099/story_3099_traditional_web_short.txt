After five days, the high priest Ananias arrived with elders and an orator named Tertullus. They brought their case against Paul before Governor Felix.

Tertullus opened with praise for Felix, then accused Paul of being a plague, stirring insurrections among Jews throughout the world, leading the sect of the Nazarenes, and attempting to profane the temple. The Jews joined the accusation and affirmed his claims.

Felix beckoned to Paul. Paul answered that only twelve days had passed since he went to Jerusalem to worship. His accusers had not found him disputing in the temple or stirring up a crowd in the synagogues or the city, and they could not prove their charges.

Paul did confess that he served the God of his fathers according to the Way, which they called a sect. He believed the law and the prophets and held the hope of a resurrection of both the just and the unjust. He practiced keeping a conscience void of offense toward God and men.

Paul explained that he had returned after some years to bring gifts for the needy and offerings. Jews from Asia had found him purified in the temple, without a mob or turmoil. Those men were not present to accuse him. The men before Felix could name only Paul’s declaration before the council: “Concerning the resurrection of the dead I am being judged before you today!”

Felix knew the Way more exactly and delayed the case until Lysias came. He kept Paul in custody but granted privileges and allowed his friends to serve and visit him.

Later Felix and his Jewish wife, Drusilla, heard Paul concerning faith in Christ Jesus. As Paul reasoned about righteousness, self-control, and judgment to come, Felix was terrified and dismissed him until a convenient time. Felix also hoped Paul would give him money, so he summoned him often and talked with him.

Two years passed. Porcius Festus succeeded Felix, but Felix, wanting favor with the Jews, left Paul in bonds.
