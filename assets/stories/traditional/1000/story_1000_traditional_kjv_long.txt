The road stretched long and pale beneath the open sky, a track of beaten earth worn smooth by the feet of many travelers. Dust lay upon it in a fine, dry film, and where the wind stirred, it rose in thin ribbons and settled again upon the hems of garments, upon sandals cracked with age, upon the bare ankles of those who walked without covering for their feet. The hour was late in the day, and the heat had begun its slow retreat, though the ground still held warmth enough to press upward through leather soles. Along the edges of the road, dry grasses bent under their own weight, pale and brittle, and a few scattered stones caught the declining light.

A crowd had gathered. They came from the surrounding towns and from the countryside beyond — men whose hands were rough from labor, women carrying burdens upon their shoulders, some leading children by the hand. They pressed together along the roadside, standing in clusters of three and four, some crouching upon their heels in the dust. The sound of their gathering was the low murmur of many voices, the shuffling of feet upon packed earth, the occasional sharp cry of a young child lifted and quieted against a shoulder. Sweat marked the fabric at their necks and across their backs. Some carried tools — a staff, a coil of rope, a folded net. Others had brought nothing but themselves, their garments stained from the day's work, their faces lined and weathered by sun.

Jesus stood among them. He stood upon the road itself, not upon any raised place, and those nearest to him could see the dust upon his own garments, the wear upon his own sandals. His voice, when he spoke, carried across the gathered company with a plainness that required no amplification, no elevation of stone or wood beneath his feet. The crowd grew still. Those at the edges leaned forward. A man near the front shifted his weight from one foot to the other and was quiet.

He spoke to them of the weary. He called out to all who labored and were heavy laden, and bade them come unto him. He told them plainly that he would give them rest.

The words moved through the crowd the way a stone cast into standing water sends its disturbance outward in widening rings. A woman standing near the middle of the gathering adjusted the bundle she carried upon her back — a wrapped load of cloth or grain, bound with cord that had worn a groove into her shoulder through the fabric of her garment. She did not set it down, but her hand moved to the place where the cord pressed deepest, and she stood very still. Beside her, an older man with a staff planted firmly in the ground before him leaned upon it with both hands and lowered his head, listening.

Jesus spoke again. He told them to take his yoke upon them, and to learn of him. He said that he was meek and lowly in heart, and that they would find rest unto their souls.

A murmur passed through the assembly, brief and low. A young man near the back, whose arms were bare and whose skin bore the marks of rope-burn across both forearms, turned to the man beside him and repeated the words in a whisper. The man beside him said nothing but lifted his chin and watched. Somewhere in the crowd, a child sat upon the ground between the feet of those standing, drawing lines in the dust with one finger, unhurried and patient.

The air had begun to cool. A faint wind moved across the open ground, carrying with it the dry scent of grass and the distant smell of woodsmoke from a village hearth beyond the nearest ridge. The sky had shifted from the white brightness of full afternoon toward a deeper, more amber hue along the western edge. Shadows lengthened from the bodies of those standing, stretching eastward across the road in long, thin shapes.

Jesus continued. He told them that his yoke was easy and his burden was light.

A man at the front of the crowd — thick through the shoulders, his hands broad and scarred, his tunic darkened with sweat beneath both arms — stood with his arms hanging at his sides. His fingers were open, not clenched. He did not move. The woman beside him, shorter by a head, had set down a clay jar she had been carrying and placed both hands flat upon her knees where she crouched. The jar rested upright in the dust, its mouth uncovered, empty.

The words hung in the air above the road, above the gathered company, spoken once and not repeated. No one called out a question. No one turned to leave. The crowd remained as it was, standing and crouching and sitting upon the ground, their faces turned toward the one who had spoken. A donkey tethered to a post some distance behind the crowd stamped once and was still. Flies circled in the warm air above the animal's back.

Near the edge of the gathering, a woman who had been standing with one hand pressed against the small of her back — her posture bent slightly forward, her other hand resting upon the arm of a girl beside her — straightened slowly. She drew herself upright, her hand still at her back, and looked toward the place where Jesus stood. The girl beside her looked up at the woman's face and then followed her gaze forward.

The road continued beyond the crowd in both directions, disappearing into the folds of the landscape where low hills rose and fell against the sky. Olive trees stood in scattered groves upon the nearest hillside, their leaves pale and silver-green, turning in the wind so that the whole grove seemed to shift and flicker. The trunks of the trees were gnarled and thick, split and twisted with age, their bark rough and deeply furrowed.

Among those gathered, a man who had been carrying a wooden frame upon his back — a piece of equipment for labor, heavy and awkward, lashed together with leather straps — lowered it carefully to the ground. The frame settled into the dust with a dull, solid sound. The leather straps, darkened and supple with use, hung loose from the crossbar. The man rolled his shoulders back, one and then the other, and stood with his arms at his sides, facing forward.

No one spoke. The wind moved again across the road, lifting a thin veil of dust that drifted and fell. The sound of it was barely a whisper against the dry grasses at the road's edge. A bird passed overhead, moving swiftly toward the groves upon the hill, and its shadow crossed the ground for an instant and was gone.

Jesus remained where he stood. The crowd remained where they were. The late light lay across the road and across the faces of those gathered, warm and low, casting long shadows behind every figure. The woman with the bundle upon her back had not set it down, but she had shifted its weight, and the cord no longer pressed so deeply into the groove it had worn. The older man with the staff had lifted his head. The young man at the back with the rope-burns upon his arms had moved forward several paces and now stood closer to the center of the assembly.

The empty clay jar rested in the dust where the woman had placed it. The wooden frame lay upon the ground where the laborer had set it. The tools and burdens carried by those who had come — the ropes, the staffs, the bundles of cloth and grain — remained among them, held or set aside, present and visible in the fading light.

The air grew cooler. The amber along the western sky deepened. The crowd stood upon the road, and the road stretched out before them and behind them, and the one who had spoken stood among them still, near enough to touch, near enough to hear the breath drawn by those closest to him. The dust settled. The grasses bent. The wind passed over the road and moved on toward the hills, and the grove of olive trees upon the hillside turned its pale leaves once more, catching the last of the low, quiet light.
