A worn wooden yoke, smoothed by years of use, fits differently than one hastily carved and ill-matched. The difference lies not in the absence of weight but in how the weight is distributed — shared across shoulders that were measured and known before the yoke was ever shaped.

Weariness has a particular quality when it accumulates slowly. It settles into the bones like winter settling into the ground, so gradually that a soul may forget what it felt like before the heaviness came. The ordinary burdens of living — grief carried quietly, responsibilities shouldered without complaint, the steady erosion of hope deferred — these gather like stones in a traveler's pack until the road itself seems impossible.

What stands out in these ancient words is the peculiar invitation: not to set the burden down entirely, but to exchange it. Rest and a yoke offered in the same breath. There is a gentleness woven into that paradox, as though the one speaking understood that human hands need something to hold, that purpose and peace are not opposites.

Some exchanges happen not in dramatic moments but in the quiet turning of an exhausted heart toward a voice that says, simply, *come.*
