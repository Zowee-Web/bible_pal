The road climbed gradually through dry, stony ground where thistles grew between the rocks and the dust rose in small puffs with every footfall. The afternoon was well advanced. Heat still pressed down from above and radiated up from the pale earth below, and the people walking along the road moved slowly, their sandals scraping against the packed dirt. Some carried bundles on their shoulders — rolled cloth, baskets of dried fish, jars of oil wrapped in rough fabric. Others walked with nothing but the clothes on their backs, their tunics stained dark with sweat along the collar and under the arms.

Jesus walked among them. His robe was the plain, undyed wool common to the region, and dust had gathered along its hem and in the creases near his sandals. He moved at an unhurried pace, and the crowd around him thickened as the road widened near a clearing where a few broad-canopied terebinth trees threw patches of shade across the ground. The shade was imperfect — dappled, shifting when the dry wind stirred the branches — but people moved toward it the way water moves downhill, without hesitation.

They gathered. Dozens of them, then more. Some sat on the ground, pulling their knees up or stretching their legs out in front of them. A woman lowered a heavy clay jar from her head and set it down carefully, pressing her palms against the small of her back and arching slightly. Near the edge of the group, a man with thick, calloused hands — a laborer, by the look of him — crouched on his heels and rested his forearms across his knees. His shoulders were uneven, the left one sitting lower than the right, as if shaped over years by carrying loads on one side. A younger man beside him unwound a length of rope from around his waist and coiled it loosely on the ground.

The crowd settled. Conversations dropped to murmurs and then to silence as people turned their faces toward Jesus. He stood at the base of one of the trees, and the moving shade crossed his face and arms in shifting patterns of light and dark. A few children sat near the front, cross-legged on the bare ground, their small hands fidgeting with pebbles and dry grass stems. Behind them, an older woman leaned against a companion's shoulder, her head covering slightly askew, her breathing audible — a faint wheeze on each exhale.

Jesus opened his mouth and spoke. His voice carried clearly across the group, not shouted but projected, the way a man speaks when he wants to be heard by those standing at the back of a crowd without straining.

"Come to me, all you who labor and are heavily burdened."

The words moved through the gathered people like a stone dropped into still water. The laborer with the uneven shoulders shifted his weight. The woman who had set down the clay jar turned her head slightly, as though listening more carefully. A man near the back of the crowd, older, with deep lines carved around his mouth and eyes, lifted his chin from where it had been resting against his chest.

"And I will give you rest."

The breeze picked up for a moment, rustling the dry leaves overhead and carrying with it the faint smell of wild thyme from the hillside. A few loose strands of hair blew across the face of a young woman sitting near the middle of the group, and she tucked them behind her ear without looking away from Jesus. The older woman's wheezing continued, a thin, reedy sound beneath the silence.

Jesus paused. He looked across the faces in front of him — weathered faces, lined faces, young faces with dust in their eyebrows. A child near the front dropped a pebble and it clicked against another stone, a small, sharp sound.

"Take my yoke upon you and learn from me," he said.

Several people in the crowd shifted at the word. A farmer near the right side of the gathering glanced down at his own hands, turning them over slowly. The skin across his palms was thick and pale where calluses had built up, cracked in places. He closed his hands and looked back up.

"For I am gentle and humble in heart."

The words came out evenly, without strain. Jesus stood with his arms at his sides, his posture open, his weight settled. A bird — a small brown thing, perhaps a sparrow — landed on a branch above him and hopped twice before going still. The shadow of the leaves continued to move across the ground and across the people sitting there, and the dry wind came again, carrying dust and the faint scent of baked earth.

"And you will find rest for your souls."

The laborer with the uneven shoulders lowered his head slightly. The young man who had been coiling rope went still, his hands resting on the loose coils in his lap. The older man at the back of the crowd, the one with the deep lines around his mouth, stood very straight now, his arms hanging at his sides.

A long moment passed. The only sounds were the wind in the terebinth branches, the distant bleating of a goat somewhere on the hillside, and the faint rustle of clothing as people breathed and shifted where they sat or stood.

Jesus spoke again.

"For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light."

The farmer looked at his hands once more, then let them rest on his thighs, palms down. The woman who had carried the clay jar glanced at it where it sat on the ground beside her, its weight no longer on her head, and she straightened her back. One of the children in the front row picked up the pebble that had fallen and turned it over between small fingers, examining it as children do, with complete attention.

The words hung in the warm air. No one spoke immediately. The crowd remained gathered under the shifting shade, and the sparrow on the branch above Jesus tilted its head and then flew off, a quick blur of brown against the pale sky. Its wingbeats were audible for a moment — a rapid, papery flutter — and then it was gone, and the branch it had perched on bobbed gently before going still.

The older woman with the wheeze drew a long, slow breath, and the sound was slightly clearer than before, or perhaps the silence around her had simply deepened enough to hear it more distinctly. Her companion adjusted the covering on the older woman's head, pulling it gently back into place with practiced hands.

People began to stir. Not to leave — not yet — but in the way that a group stirs when something has been said that requires sitting with. A man near the middle of the crowd rubbed the back of his neck. A woman adjusted the bundle she had set beside her, pushing it slightly farther away as if making more room to sit. The young man with the rope picked up one end of the coil and let it drop again, a small, absent gesture.

The heat had not broken. The road beyond the clearing still shimmered faintly where the sun struck it full. The rocks along the hillside were pale and dry, and the thistles between them had gone brittle in the long summer. But beneath the terebinth trees, the shade held, and the people remained where they were, and the dust settled slowly back to the ground around their feet.

Jesus stood among them. The wind moved through the branches once more, and the leaves turned their silvered undersides to the light, and the sound they made was dry and continuous, like the sound of grain being poured from one vessel into another. The laborer with the uneven shoulders uncrossed his arms and let them hang loose. The farmer with the calloused hands sat with his palms still resting open on his thighs.

The road stretched away in both directions, pale and dusty, climbing toward the next ridge to the north and descending toward the valley floor to the south. But no one on that road had yet risen to walk it. They sat or stood in the clearing beneath the trees, and the afternoon moved slowly forward around them, and the dry wind carried the scent of thyme and dust and sun-warmed stone.
