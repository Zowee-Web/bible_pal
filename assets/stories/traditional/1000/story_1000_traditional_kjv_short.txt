The road climbed slowly toward the hillside where he sat. Dust hung in the still air, stirred by the feet of those who came — men and women walking from the villages below, their sandals worn thin, their garments stained with the labor of many days. Some carried tools upon their shoulders. Others bore bundles of grain or led animals by frayed rope. The sun stood high and the heat pressed down upon the open ground, and there was no shade save a single broad fig tree near the place where the teacher had settled himself upon a low stone.

They gathered before him in an uneven half-circle, lowering their burdens to the earth. A woman set down a clay jar and straightened her back slowly. A man released the rope of a young ox and stood with both hands hanging at his sides. Children crouched near the edges of the crowd, and the sound of their restless movement mixed with the buzzing of insects in the dry grass.

He looked upon them — the sweat upon their brows, the dust in the creases of their hands, the way their shoulders bent forward beneath the weight of what they carried and what they had carried for years. He opened his mouth and spoke, and his voice moved across the gathered company like water poured out upon flat stone.

He called unto them — all that labored, all that were heavy laden. He bade them come unto him. He would give them rest.

The crowd grew still. A man near the front shifted his weight from one foot to the other. The woman beside the clay jar lifted her head.

He spoke again. They were to take his yoke upon them, he said, and learn of him. He declared himself meek and lowly in heart. They would find rest unto their souls.

No one moved. The ox behind the crowd stamped once and was quiet. A dry wind passed through the branches of the fig tree and the leaves turned, showing their pale undersides.

Then he said it plainly: his yoke was easy, and his burden was light.

The words hung in the open air. A man near the back of the gathering looked down at the bundle he had set upon the ground — a heavy thing, wrapped in rough cloth, the cord biting into the fabric where it had been tied. He stood over it and did not pick it up again.

Others remained where they were, their faces turned toward the one who sat upon the stone. The sun moved westward by a degree. Somewhere below the hill, a cock crowed, and the sound carried upward through the warm and heavy air. The teacher sat among them, and the dust settled slowly around his feet, and no one departed from that place.
