The whole of it began with a father's failing sight and a mother who overheard. Isaac was old, his eyes too dim to see, and knowing not the day of his death he called his elder son and sent him to the field with his bow, to hunt and to dress the savoury meat he loved, that he might eat and bless him before he died. But Rebekah had heard the charge, and while Esau was still away she came to Jacob and told him what his father had promised his brother, and set him to a work of her own. Fetch me two kids from the flock, she said, and I will make of them the meat thy father loveth; carry it in to him, and let him bless thee. Two kids fetched, a savoury meat dressed by her own hand, Esau's garments drawn over Jacob, and goatskins bound upon his smooth hands and neck — so she made him into the likeness of his brother, over his own protest that a touch would betray him for a deceiver and bring him a curse instead of a blessing. Let the curse fall on me, was all she answered him; only obey. And she put the meat and the bread into his hands and sent him in.

What followed was a blind man tested at every turn and deceived at every turn. Who was it that came near, Isaac asked, and the answer was Esau, his firstborn, come with venison so his father might rise and bless him. The old man wondered that the hunt had gone so quickly; the Lord sped it to my hand, said the voice that was not Esau's. Still doubting, he bade the son come near that he might feel him, and know for certain whether this were Esau or no. Jacob went near, and the old hands passed over the goatskins and found them rough. The voice is Jacob's voice, he said, but the hands are the hands of Esau. There the deceit held fast; the hairy hands undid the truth the voice had told, and he knew him not. He asked once more whether this were truly his son Esau, and once more was answered that it was, and so he ate the venison, and drank the wine that was brought to him.

Then came the blessing itself. Isaac called the young man near to kiss him, and when he caught the smell of the field in his garments, the words came. See, he said, the smell of my son is as the smell of a field the Lord hath blessed. And he asked of God for him the dew of heaven and the fatness of the earth, and plenty of corn and wine; that peoples would serve him and nations bow before him; that he would be lord over his brethren, and his mother's sons bow down to him; and that every one who cursed him would be cursed, and every one who blessed him would be blessed. So the blessing meant for the firstborn was laid upon the younger, and it could not afterward be called back.

The words were scarcely spoken when the true Esau came in from his hunting, bearing his own meat, asking his father to rise and eat and bless him. Who art thou? said Isaac. I am thy son, thy firstborn, Esau. And the old man trembled, and trembled the more, and asked who it was then who had already come with venison and taken the blessing before ever Esau arrived — for he had eaten of it, and blessed the man, and blessed that one must remain.

Here everything turned on a single cry, great and exceeding bitter. Esau lifted up his voice and begged to be blessed as well. His father could give him only the truth of it: his brother had come with subtilty and taken the blessing. Was he not rightly named Jacob, the elder son answered — the supplanter — who had supplanted him now twice over, first at the birthright and now at the blessing; had his father truly kept back no blessing at all for him? But there was nothing left. Isaac had already made Jacob his lord, given him all his brethren for servants, and sustained him with corn and wine; what then was there to hand the firstborn now? Hast thou but one blessing, my father? Esau pleaded. Bless me, even me also, O my father. And he lifted up his voice, and wept.

What his father found for him at the last was no undoing of the first word. His dwelling too would know the earth's fatness and heaven's dew; yet he would live by his sword and serve his brother, until the day he grew strong enough to shake that yoke from his neck. From that hour Esau hated his brother, and promised himself Jacob's life once the days of mourning for their father were past. His vow reached Rebekah, and she moved a second time for her younger son — warning Jacob, and sending him far off to her brother Laban in Haran, to wait there until his brother's fury spent itself and the wrong was forgotten, when she would send and bring him home. Why should she be bereaved of them both in one day? And to Isaac she said only that the daughters of Heth had wearied her of her very life, and that if Jacob too should take his wife from among them, her life would be worth nothing to her at all.
