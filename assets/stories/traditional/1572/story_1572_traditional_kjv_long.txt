Blind now, and certain his days were few, old Isaac sent for Esau his eldest son. Let him take his quiver and his bow, the father said, and hunt in the field, and dress the savoury meat he loved; then would Isaac eat of it and bless his son before he died.

Rebekah was near, and heard it. And no sooner had Esau gone out with his bow than she came to Jacob, her younger son, with a purpose of her own. She had heard the whole of the father's charge — the venison, the meat, the blessing to be spoken before the Lord — and she would not have that blessing fall where it was meant to fall. Let Jacob go to the flock and fetch two good kids of the goats; her own hands would make of them the very meat Isaac loved, and Jacob should carry it in and take the blessing to himself.

He was afraid of it. Esau was a hairy man, he reminded her, and his own skin was smooth; should his father reach out to feel him, the deceit would lie bare, and he would earn a curse where he had hoped for a blessing. But his mother took the danger upon herself. Upon me be thy curse, my son, she told him — only obey. So he fetched the kids, and she dressed them into the savoury meat. Then she took the goodly raiment of Esau, which was with her in the house, and clothed the younger son in it; and over his smooth hands and neck she bound the skins of the kids; and into his hands she gave the meat and the bread she had made.

So Jacob went in to his blind father, and named himself by his brother's name. I am Esau thy firstborn, he said. Let his father rise and eat of the venison, that his soul might bless him. Isaac wondered aloud that the hunt had been so quickly done, and Jacob answered that the Lord his God had sped it to his hand. Still the old man was uneasy. He called the son near to feel him, and his fingers closed upon the goatskins where he looked for his elder's rough hands. The voice is Jacob's voice, he said, but the hands are the hands of Esau. And there the disguise held; he knew him not. Once more he asked whether this were truly Esau, and once more Jacob answered that it was.

Then Isaac ate the meat, and drank the wine his son poured for him, and drew the young man close to kiss him. The smell of the open field clung to Esau's garments, and the old man was moved, and blessed him out of a full heart. He gave him the dew of heaven and the fatness of the earth, corn and wine in plenty, nations to serve him and brothers to bow before him, a curse upon all who cursed him and a blessing upon all who blessed.

The blessing was scarcely spoken, and Jacob scarcely gone from the tent, when Esau came in from his hunting with the meat he had made. Let my father arise and eat, he said, that thy soul may bless me. Who art thou? asked Isaac. I am thy son, thy firstborn, Esau.

And the old man trembled — trembled very exceedingly — as the whole of it came clear to him. Who was it, then, he cried, that had taken venison and brought it to him? For he had eaten of it, and blessed the one who brought it, before ever Esau came. And blessed that one would remain. The word, once spoken, could not be called back.

When Esau heard it, he broke into a great and exceeding bitter cry. Bless me, even me also, O my father! But his father could give him only the truth of the matter: his brother had come with subtilty and carried the blessing away. Then the grief poured out of him. Was he not rightly named Jacob — the supplanter? For twice now his brother had supplanted him: first the birthright, and now the blessing. Had his father not so much as one blessing kept back for him?

But there was nothing left to give. Isaac had set Jacob as lord over his brethren, had given him his kinsmen for servants and sustained him with corn and wine; what remained for the elder son now? Hast thou but one blessing, my father? Esau pleaded. Bless me, even me also. And he lifted up his voice, and wept.

At the last his father answered him, though it undid nothing of the first word. Esau's dwelling too should know the earth's fatness and the dew of heaven; yet he would live by his sword and serve his brother — until the day he grew strong enough to break that yoke from his neck.

So Esau hated Jacob for the stolen blessing, and said within himself that when the days of mourning for his father were come, he would kill his brother. But the muttered threat did not stay hidden. Word of it reached Rebekah, and once again she moved to save her younger son. Esau comforts himself with the thought of thy death, she warned Jacob; therefore obey me this once more. Let him flee to Laban her brother, away in Haran, and stay there until his brother's fury was spent and the wrong forgotten. Then would she send and fetch him home again. Why, she asked, should she lose them both in a single day?

And to Isaac she said only that she was weary of her life for the daughters of Heth; and if Jacob too should take a wife from the women of that land, what good would her life be to her at all?
