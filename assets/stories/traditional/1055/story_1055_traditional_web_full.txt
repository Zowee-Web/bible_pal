There was a man in the land of Uz whose name was Job. He was blameless and upright, one who feared God and turned away from evil.

He had seven sons and three daughters. He owned seven thousand sheep — their bleating filled the hills around his land, the white of their wool visible on every slope. Three thousand camels. Five hundred yoke of oxen. Five hundred female donkeys. And very many servants who tended it all, their cook fires dotting the landscape at dusk. He was the greatest of all the people of the east. The wealthiest, the most respected, a man whose name was spoken with weight in every market and at every gate.

His sons used to hold feasts in their homes, each on his appointed day, and they would send and invite their three sisters to eat and drink with them. The sound of music and laughter carried from the houses on feast nights, lamplight spilling through the doorways into the dark. And when the days of the feast had run their course, Job would rise early in the morning and offer burnt offerings according to the number of them all. For Job said, "It may be that my children have sinned, and cursed God in their hearts." This Job did continually. Before the sun was fully risen, while the dew still clung to the grass, the smoke of the offering would climb into the sky above his house.

Then one day the messengers came.

The first arrived running. His feet pounded the dirt of the road, his chest heaving, his face gray with dust and terror. He stumbled through the gate and nearly fell in the courtyard.

"The oxen were plowing and the donkeys feeding beside them, and the Sabeans fell upon them and took them and struck down the servants with the edge of the sword. I alone have escaped to tell you."

The words were still in the air when another appeared at the gate. Still running. Still breathless. Still gray with dust. His tunic was torn at the shoulder.

"The fire of God fell from heaven and burned up the sheep and the servants and consumed them. I alone have escaped to tell you."

Seven thousand sheep. Gone. The hillsides that had been full of bleating were silent. The servants who had tended them were ash on the ground.

While he was still speaking, another came through the gate. His garments were torn, his sandals worn through, the leather split at the sole. He braced himself against the doorpost.

"The Chaldeans formed three companies and made a raid on the camels and took them and struck down the servants with the edge of the sword. I alone have escaped to tell you."

Three thousand camels. The wealth that had taken a lifetime to build was being dismantled in minutes, report by report, messenger by messenger, the blows coming faster than a man could absorb them. The courtyard was filling with breathless, dust-covered men, each bearing worse news than the last.

While he was still speaking, another came. This one could barely stand. His knees buckled as he crossed the threshold. His voice shook.

"Your sons and daughters were eating and drinking wine in their eldest brother's house, and behold, a great wind came across the wilderness and struck the four corners of the house, and it fell upon the young people, and they are dead. I alone have escaped to tell you."

The room was silent. The dust of the road still hung in the air where the messengers had come in, lit by the afternoon light from the windows. Four men. Four reports. The oxen, the sheep, the camels, the servants — and now the children. All ten of them. In one day. In the space of a single afternoon the wealthiest man in the east had lost everything he had except the ground beneath his feet and the breath in his lungs.

Then Job arose.

He tore his robe. The fabric ripped in his hands — the sound sharp and ragged in the quiet room. He shaved his head. The razor scraped against his scalp and the hair fell to the floor around him in dark clumps. Then he fell to the ground and worshiped.

He said, "Naked I came from my mother's womb, and naked shall I return there. The Lord gave, and the Lord has taken away. Blessed be the name of the Lord."

The torn robe lay across the floor, its edges frayed. The shorn hair lay in the dust beside his knees. The four messengers stood in the doorway, breathing hard, the light behind them. In all this Job did not sin or charge God with wrong.
