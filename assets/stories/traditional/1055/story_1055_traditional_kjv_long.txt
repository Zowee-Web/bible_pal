There was a man in the land of Uz, whose name was Job; and that man was perfect and upright, and one that feared God, and eschewed evil.

And there were born unto him seven sons and three daughters. His substance also was seven thousand sheep — their bleating filled the hills round about his land, the white of their wool upon every slope. Three thousand camels moved in long lines across the pasture, their shadows stretching at eventide. Five hundred yoke of oxen wrought in his fields. Five hundred she asses grazed beside the streams. And a very great household of servants tended it all, their cook fires dotting the land at dusk like a scattered constellation. So that this man was the greatest of all the men of the east. The wealthiest, the most honoured, a man whose name was spoken with weight in every market and at every gate.

And his sons went and feasted in their houses, every one his day; and sent and called for their three sisters to eat and to drink with them. The houses lay scattered across the family land, the eldest brother's the largest, set upon the slow rise above the others. The sound of music and laughter carried from the houses on the nights of feasting, the light of lamps spilling through the doorways into the darkness. The cycle ran on through the year — each son's house in its turn, the lamps lit, the tables spread, the seven brothers and three sisters gathered, then a new house the next week.

And it was so, when the days of their feasting were gone about, that Job sent and sanctified them, and rose up early in the morning, and offered burnt offerings according to the number of them all. Ten offerings. One for each child. Ere the sun was fully risen, whilst the dew yet clung to the grass, the smoke of the offering climbed into the sky above his dwelling. For Job said within himself, "It may be that my sons have sinned, and cursed God in their hearts." Thus did Job continually. Round after round, feast after feast, year after year.

Then, in a place not seen by him, on a day not measured by his hours, the sons of God came to present themselves before the LORD. And Satan came also among them.

And the LORD said unto Satan, "Whence comest thou?"

Satan answered the LORD, and said, "From going to and fro in the earth, and from walking up and down in it."

And the LORD said unto Satan, "Hast thou considered my servant Job, that there is none like him in the earth, a perfect and an upright man, one that feareth God, and escheweth evil?"

Then Satan answered the LORD, and said, "Doth Job fear God for nought? Hast not thou made an hedge about him, and about his house, and about all that he hath on every side? Thou hast blessed the work of his hands, and his substance is increased in the land. But put forth thine hand now, and touch all that he hath, and he will curse thee to thy face."

And the LORD said unto Satan, "Behold, all that he hath is in thy power; only upon himself put not forth thine hand."

So Satan went forth from the presence of the LORD. And in the land of Uz the morning brake as it ever brake, and the servants went forth into the fields, and the herds moved into the pastures, and the houses of the children stood open for another feast. None of them knew.

It fell on a day when the sons and the daughters were eating and drinking wine in their eldest brother's house.

A messenger came running. His feet smote the dust of the road, his breast heaving, his countenance grey with dust and terror. He stumbled through the gate and near fell in the court. Job rose up from where he sat. The man caught his breath in ragged pulls and forced the words out.

"The oxen were plowing, and the asses feeding beside them, and the Sabeans fell upon them, and took them away; yea, they have slain the servants with the edge of the sword; and I only am escaped alone to tell thee."

The words yet hung in the air when another appeared at the gate. Yet running. Yet breathless. Yet grey with dust. His garment was rent at the shoulder.

"The fire of God is fallen from heaven, and hath burned up the sheep, and the servants, and consumed them; and I only am escaped alone to tell thee."

Seven thousand sheep. Gone. The hillsides that had been full of bleating were silent now. The servants that had tended them were but ashes upon the ground. Job had not yet spoken. He stood very still.

While he was yet speaking, there came also another through the gate. His garments were rent, his sandals worn through, the leather split at the sole. He set his hand against the doorpost and gripped it as one afraid he would fall.

"The Chaldeans made out three bands, and fell upon the camels, and have carried them away, yea, and slain the servants with the edge of the sword; and I only am escaped alone to tell thee."

Three thousand camels. The wealth which had taken a lifetime to build was being pulled asunder in minutes, report upon report, messenger upon messenger, the blows falling faster than a man could bear. The court was filling with breathless, dust-covered men, each bearing tidings worse than the last. Job had not moved. His hands hung at his sides. His countenance was set.

While he was yet speaking, there came also another. This one could scarce stand upon his feet. His knees gave way as he crossed the threshold. His voice trembled. He looked upon Job and then could not look upon him.

"Thy sons and thy daughters were eating and drinking wine in their eldest brother's house: and, behold, there came a great wind from the wilderness, and smote the four corners of the house, and it fell upon the young men, and they are dead; and I only am escaped alone to tell thee."

The chamber was still.

The dust of the road yet hung in the air where the messengers had entered in, lit by the afternoon light from the windows. Four men in the doorway. Four reports. The oxen, the sheep, the camels, the servants — and now the children. All ten of them. The seven sons and the three sisters. The houses on the rise above the others. The lamplight in the doorways. The feast that had been still going an hour before.

In the space of a single afternoon the wealthiest man in all the east had lost everything he had, save the ground beneath his feet and the breath within his body.

Then Job arose.

He moved slowly, the weight of his body heavy beneath him, as if the air itself had thickened. He reached up unto the collar of his mantle, and rent it. The cloth tore in his hands — the sound sharp and ragged in the stillness, the long seam splitting from the throat to the waist. He set the razor unto his head. The blade scraped against his scalp, and the hair fell upon the ground about him in dark clumps, until the crown of his head was bare.

Then he fell down upon the ground.

Not toppled. Not collapsed in a heap. He went down upon his knees, and from his knees upon his face, and he lay there in the dust beside the shorn hair. And he worshipped.

"Naked came I out of my mother's womb, and naked shall I return thither: the LORD gave, and the LORD hath taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD."

The words came slow. They came from a place beneath the grief, beneath the breath. The torn mantle lay across the floor, its edges frayed. The shorn hair lay in the dust beside his knees. The four messengers stood in the doorway, their breath yet heavy, the light behind them in the open air.

In all this Job sinned not, nor charged God foolishly.
