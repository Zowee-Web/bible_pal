There was a man in the land of Uz whose name was Job. He was blameless and upright, one who feared God and turned away from evil.

He had seven sons and three daughters. He owned seven thousand sheep — their bleating filled the hills around his land, the white of their wool visible on every slope. Three thousand camels moved in long lines across the pasture, their shadows stretching at evening. Five hundred yoke of oxen worked his fields. Five hundred female donkeys grazed beside the streams. And very many servants tended it all, their cook fires dotting the landscape at dusk like a scattered constellation. He was the greatest of all the people of the east. The wealthiest, the most respected, a man whose name was spoken with weight in every market and at every gate.

His sons used to hold feasts in their homes, each on his appointed day, and they would send and invite their three sisters to eat and drink with them. The houses lay scattered across the family land, the eldest brother's the largest, set on the slow rise above the others. The sound of music and laughter carried from the houses on feast nights, lamplight spilling through the doorways into the dark. The cycle ran on through the year — each son's house in its turn, the lamps lit, the tables spread, the seven brothers and three sisters gathered, then a new house the next week.

When the days of the feast had run their course, Job would rise early in the morning and offer burnt offerings according to the number of them all. Ten offerings. One for each child. Before the sun was fully risen, while the dew still clung to the grass, the smoke of the offering would climb into the sky above his house. He said within himself, "It may be that my children have sinned, and cursed God in their hearts." This Job did continually. Round after round, feast after feast, year after year.

Then, in a place not seen by him, on a day not measured by his hours, the sons of God came to present themselves before the Lord. And Satan also came among them.

The Lord said to Satan, "Where have you come from?"

Satan answered, "From going back and forth in the earth, and from walking up and down in it."

The Lord said, "Have you considered my servant Job? There is no one like him in the earth, a blameless and upright man, one who fears God and turns away from evil."

Satan answered, "Does Job fear God for nothing? Have you not made a hedge around him, and around his house, and around all that he has, on every side? You have blessed the work of his hands, and his substance is increased in the land. But stretch out your hand now, and touch all that he has, and he will renounce you to your face."

The Lord said to Satan, "Behold, all that he has is in your power. Only on himself do not stretch out your hand."

So Satan went out from the presence of the Lord. And in the land of Uz the morning broke as it always broke, and the servants went out to the fields, and the herds moved into the pastures, and the houses of the children stood open for another feast. None of them knew.

It fell on a day when the sons and the daughters were eating and drinking wine in their oldest brother's house.

A messenger came running. His feet pounded the dirt of the road, his chest heaving, his face gray with dust and terror. He stumbled through the gate and nearly fell in the courtyard. Job rose from where he sat. The man caught his breath in ragged pulls and forced the words out.

"The oxen were plowing and the donkeys feeding beside them, and the Sabeans fell upon them and took them and struck down the servants with the edge of the sword. I alone have escaped to tell you."

The words were still in the air when another appeared at the gate. Still running. Still breathless. Still gray with dust. His tunic was torn at the shoulder.

"The fire of God fell from heaven and burned up the sheep and the servants and consumed them. I alone have escaped to tell you."

Seven thousand sheep. Gone. The hillsides that had been full of bleating were silent now. The servants who had tended them were ash on the ground. Job had not yet spoken. He stood very still.

While that man was still speaking, another came through the gate. His garments were torn, his sandals worn through, the leather split at the sole. He braced himself against the doorpost and gripped it as if afraid he would fall.

"The Chaldeans formed three companies and made a raid on the camels and took them and struck down the servants with the edge of the sword. I alone have escaped to tell you."

Three thousand camels. The wealth that had taken a lifetime to build was being dismantled in minutes, report by report, messenger by messenger, the blows coming faster than a man could absorb them. The courtyard was filling with breathless, dust-covered men, each bearing worse news than the last. Job had not moved. His hands hung at his sides. His face was set.

While he was still speaking, another came. This one could barely stand. His knees buckled as he crossed the threshold. His voice shook. He looked at Job and then could not look at him.

"Your sons and daughters were eating and drinking wine in their eldest brother's house, and behold, a great wind came across the wilderness and struck the four corners of the house, and it fell upon the young people, and they are dead. I alone have escaped to tell you."

The room was silent.

The dust of the road still hung in the air where the messengers had come in, lit by the afternoon light from the windows. Four men in the doorway. Four reports. The oxen, the sheep, the camels, the servants — and now the children. All ten of them. The seven sons and the three sisters. The houses on the rise above the others. The lamplight in the doorways. The feast that had been still going an hour ago.

In the space of a single afternoon the wealthiest man in the east had lost everything he had except the ground beneath his feet and the breath in his lungs.

Then Job arose.

He moved slowly, the weight of his body heavy beneath him, as if the air itself had thickened. He reached up to the collar of his robe and tore it. The fabric ripped in his hands — the sound sharp and ragged in the quiet room, the long seam splitting from the throat to the waist. He set the razor to his head. The blade scraped against his scalp, and the hair fell to the floor around him in dark clumps, until the crown of his head was bare.

Then he fell to the ground.

Not toppled. Not collapsed in a heap. He went down to his knees, and from his knees to his face, and he lay there in the dust beside the shorn hair. And he worshiped.

"Naked I came from my mother's womb, and naked shall I return there. The Lord gave, and the Lord has taken away. Blessed be the name of the Lord."

The words came slow. They came from a place beneath the grief, beneath the breath. The torn robe lay across the floor, its edges frayed. The shorn hair lay in the dust beside his knees. The four messengers stood in the doorway, breathing hard, the light behind them in the open air.

In all this Job did not sin or charge God with wrong.
