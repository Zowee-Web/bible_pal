The chosen men came in from the tribes. Thirty thousand of them. They came on foot down the white roads of Judah, in linen and in wool, with dust on their sandals and bright cloth at their shoulders, and they gathered at Baale Judah. They had come for the ark.

The ark of God had sat for years in the house of Abinadab on the hill. It had been quiet there under its covering, kept by men who tended it carefully and did not speak of it loudly. Now David had said the time had come. The ark, which is called by the Name, the name of the Lord of Armies who sits above the cherubim, was to come home to Jerusalem.

They brought it out of the house. They set it on a new cart. The wood was pale and clean. The yoke was new. Uzzah and Ahio, the sons of Abinadab, walked beside the oxen, and Ahio went ahead, leading the way down the hill toward the road.

David walked with the people. And the people made music.

There were harps. There were stringed instruments and tambourines. There were castanets and the bright clash of cymbals. The whole house of Israel played before the Lord with all kinds of instruments made of cypress wood, and the sound of them rose along the column and out across the fields. Children ran ahead and circled back. Women clapped in time. Men sang phrases that drifted in and out of one another. The oxen plodded under the weight of the cart, and the cart wheels turned in the dust, and the long line of Israel followed.

It was a procession that felt like a festival. The air over the column shimmered with the noise of joy. The road wound between the threshing floors of the harvest country, and the smell of grain dust hung in the heat, and the players kept playing.

They came to the threshing floor of Nacon.

The oxen stumbled. One of them slipped on the trodden grain — a hoof going sideways, then forward, then catching itself — and the cart pitched. The ark moved. Just a finger's width. Just enough.

Uzzah put out his hand. He took hold of it.

The music kept going for one heartbeat longer than the man. Then the line behind him saw, and the line behind that saw, and the harps fell silent in waves down the road. A tambourine struck the ground. A cymbal was lowered. The shouting tapered into a single horrified word and then into nothing at all.

Uzzah lay where he had stood. The Lord's anger had burned against him, and God had struck him there for his error. He had died by the ark of God.

David came forward through the crowd. He looked. He did not speak for a long time. The thirty thousand stood in the sun and waited.

David was displeased. The breach in the procession was a breach in him. He called that place Perez Uzzah, the breaking-out, and the name held — and it has held to this day.

David was afraid of the Lord that day. He stood on the road in front of thirty thousand men and felt the thing he had not felt until that moment. The ark was holy. The ark was not a thing he could carry on a cart with castanets. He said, aloud, so that the men nearest to him heard it, "How could the Lord's ark come to me?"

He would not move it further. He turned the cart aside, off the road, into the house of Obed-Edom the Gittite. The Gittite stood in his doorway, a foreigner in his own household, and watched the king of Israel back away from the thing he had carried in song. David left the ark with him. He went up to Jerusalem without it.

Three months passed.

In those three months the ark sat in Obed-Edom's house. And the house was blessed. The wells did not fail. The fields produced. The flocks bore. The household, all of it, prospered under the presence that had killed Uzzah. The Gittite walked through his rooms each day and lived. He passed the ark coming and going. He slept in a house with the Name in it, and he woke, and his children woke, and his herds were strong.

Word reached David. Men came to him in his palace and said, "The Lord has blessed the house of Obed-Edom, and all that belongs to him, because of God's ark."

David heard it. He was quiet for a moment. Then he stood up.

He went back.

This time it was different. This time the bearers carried the ark on their shoulders, in the old way, as it had been carried in the wilderness. The poles rested on men. The oxen were no longer in front of it. The cart was not in the story anymore.

When those who bore the ark had gone six paces, he sacrificed an ox and a fattened calf. The blood ran in the dust. They walked, and after a while he made them stop, and he sacrificed again. The procession crawled. The procession was reverent. Smoke rose along the road behind them.

And then, slowly, as the road climbed toward Jerusalem, the joy came back into it.

David put off his royal robes. He set aside the colored cloth and the woven belt and the marks of his kingship, and he girded himself with a linen ephod, the plain garment of a priest. He stepped out from the front of the column. And he danced.

He danced before the Lord with all his might.

He leaped. He spun. His hair came loose. The ephod swung at his knees. He was barefoot or near to it. He was a man and not a king, in the dust of the road, before the ark.

The trumpet sounded. The people shouted. The whole house of Israel brought up the ark of the Lord with shouting and with the sound of the trumpet, and the road into the city of David was loud with it. The instruments came back, not as a parade now but as praise, and the king who had been afraid was the king who was leaping, and the men who had carried Uzzah's body to its grave were lifting their voices in song.

As the ark came in through the gate of the city, Michal the daughter of Saul looked out through the window. She saw the king. She saw him leaping. She saw him dancing before the Lord, half-clothed, undignified, ridiculous in her eyes — the king of Israel cavorting like a common man in the street. And she despised him in her heart.

The procession did not see her. The crowd carried the ark inward, past the houses, into the place that had been prepared. They brought it in and set it in its place, in the middle of the tent that David had pitched for it. David offered burnt offerings and peace offerings before the Lord. The smoke went up.

When he had finished offering the burnt offering and the peace offerings, he stood before the people. He raised his hands. He blessed them in the name of the Lord of Armies.

And he gave to all the people, even among the whole multitude of Israel, both to men and to women, to every one of them, a cake of bread and a portion of meat and a cake of raisins. They came forward in lines. They received with both hands. They went home with food in their arms and the music still in their ears.

So all the people departed, each to his own house. The streets of the city quieted. The sun lowered over the rooftops, and lamps began to come on in the windows along the climb.

In an upper window in the city, Michal stood watching them go.
