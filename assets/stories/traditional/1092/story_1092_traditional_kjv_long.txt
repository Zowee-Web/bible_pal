The garden was still and dark. It was early upon the first day of the week — so early that the sun had not yet risen. The air was cold and damp, heavy with the smell of earth and stone and the faint sweetness of flowers growing along the garden paths. A thin mist hung close upon the ground. The trees were black shapes against a grey sky.

Mary Magdalene made her way through the garden alone. Her sandals were wet with the dew. She bore spices wrapped in cloth — oils and fragrant herbs prepared for the body. Her face was drawn. Her eyes were swollen with days of weeping. She had watched him die. She had stood by the cross and seen the life depart from him. She had watched them take him down and bear him unto this tomb and roll the heavy stone across the entrance. And now, two days hence, she had come to do the last thing remaining unto her — to anoint the body, to prepare it fitly, to honour what was left.

When she came unto the sepulchre, she stayed her steps.

The stone had been rolled away. The entrance stood open. The dark mouth of the cave gaped wide.

Her heart fell. She turned and ran.

She found Simon Peter and the other disciple — him whom Jesus loved — and the words poured forth. "They have taken away the Lord out of the sepulchre," she said, "and we know not where they have laid him."

The two disciples ran. The other disciple, being younger and swifter, came first unto the tomb. He stooped down and looked in. He could see the linen clothes lying upon the stone — but something held him, and he went not in. Peter came after, out of breath, and went straightway into the sepulchre. He saw the linen clothes. He saw the napkin that had been about the head — not lying with the rest but folded carefully and set apart by itself.

Then went the other disciple in also. He saw. And he believed — though as yet they understood not the scripture, that he must rise again from the dead.

The two disciples looked one upon another. They knew not what to say. They went away unto their own homes, walking quickly through the empty streets, bearing a thing they could not yet put into words.

But Mary remained.

She stood without at the sepulchre in the dim grey light, weeping. She had come to tend a body, and now even that was taken from her. The grief was manifold — loss upon loss. She wept as one weepeth when there is naught left to do.

After a while she stooped down and looked into the tomb.

Two figures sat within — one at the head and one at the foot of the place where the body had lain. They were clad in white. They were angels, though Mary may not have known it in that hour. They looked upon her with calm and steady eyes.

"Woman, why weepest thou?" they said.

"Because they have taken away my Lord," she said, "and I know not where they have laid him."

She turned about. A man stood behind her in the garden. He was close — but a few feet away. The light was yet dim. Her eyes were blurred with tears. She knew him not.

"Woman, why weepest thou?" the man said. "Whom seekest thou?"

She supposed him to be the gardener. Who else would be here at such an hour, in such a place? "Sir," she said, "if thou have borne him hence, tell me where thou hast laid him, and I will take him away."

Then the man spake her name.

"Mary."

One word. Spoken as only one person had ever spoken it — with a warmth, a knowledge, a manner of saying her name that she had heard an hundred times and never thought to hear again.

She turned fully toward him. Her breath caught. The grey light of the garden fell across a face she knew — knew with a certainty deeper than sight, deeper than thought. All within her broke open — grief splitting apart as the shell of a seed — and what came flooding in was not relief, not understanding, but something beyond both. Recognition. Presence. The impossible, standing before her, speaking her name.

"Rabboni!" she cried.

She reached forth her hands — the way one reacheth for a person one had thought lost for ever.

But Jesus saith unto her, "Touch me not; for I am not yet ascended to my Father: but go to my brethren, and say unto them, I ascend unto my Father, and your Father; and to my God, and your God."

Mary stood in the garden. The first light of morning was touching the tops of the trees. The mist was lifting. A bird sang once from above — the first sound of the new day.

She left the garden. She walked through the streets of Jerusalem as the city was waking — doors opening, voices calling, the smell of bread. She found the disciples.

"I have seen the Lord," she said.

Her face was streaked with tears. But her eyes were clear and bright and certain.

She told them all things which he had spoken unto her.
