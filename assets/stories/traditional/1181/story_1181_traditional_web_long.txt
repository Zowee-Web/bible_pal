On the day the chariots were broken at the river, Deborah and Barak son of Abinoam sang. They sang together on the hillside above the valley, with the dust of the fight still settling in the lowlands beneath them, and the people of Israel sang with them. The song was not a report. It was not a counting of the spoils. It was the day folded into the shape of a song, the events laid down in lines that returned upon themselves, and the people learned it as they listened, and they carried it home.

They sang first of willingness. Because the leaders had taken the lead in Israel, because the people had offered themselves willingly, blessed be the Lord. The song was for the kings to hear. It was for the princes to give ear. Deborah sang, and Barak sang with her, and the song lifted into the open sky. I, even I, will sing to the Lord, Deborah sang. I will sing praise to the Lord, the God of Israel.

Then they sang of the coming of the Lord from Seir. When the Lord went out of Seir, when he marched out of the field of Edom, the earth had trembled. The sky had dropped. The clouds had dropped water. The mountains had quaked at his presence. Even Sinai had quaked, the mountain of fire and of cloud, at the presence of the Lord, the God of Israel. The song remembered an older trembling, set it beside the new, and made them one trembling.

Then it remembered the long quiet before. In the days of Shamgar son of Anath, in the days of Jael, the highways had been unoccupied. Travelers had walked through byways, slipping along the narrow paths through the brush because the open roads were not safe. The rulers had ceased in Israel. They had ceased — until Deborah arose. Until she arose a mother in Israel.

The song lingered on what had been lost. New gods had been chosen. War had come to the gates. A shield or a spear had not been seen among forty thousand in Israel. The song let the count stand. Forty thousand and not a spear. Then it turned, and its turning was its joy. The heart of the singer was toward the governors of Israel who had offered themselves willingly among the people. Bless the Lord.

Deborah sang to herself in the song. Awake, awake, Deborah. Awake, awake, utter a song. The line came twice over, the way songs return, the way a drum beats and beats again. Arise, Barak. Lead away the captives, son of Abinoam.

Then the song named the tribes. It named those who had come down. The princes of Issachar were with Deborah. As Issachar was, so was Barak — they had rushed into the valley at his feet. Zebulun was a people that jeopardized their lives to the death. Naphtali also, on the high places of the field. Ephraim came down, and Benjamin among their peoples, and the governors out of Machir, and those who handled the marshal's staff out of Zebulun.

Then it named those who had not come. By the watercourses of Reuben, there had been great resolves of heart. Great searchings of heart. But Reuben had sat among the sheepfolds, listening for the whistling of the flocks, and had not come down. Gilead had lived beyond the Jordan. Dan had remained in ships. Asher had sat still at the haven of the sea, and had lived by his creeks. The song held both — those who came and those who did not — and let the holding be the judgment. The roll was kept in the song, and the song was sung forever.

Then they sang the battle. The kings came and fought. The kings of Canaan fought at Taanach by the waters of Megiddo. They took no plunder of silver. From the sky the stars fought. From their courses, the stars fought against Sisera. The night sky had taken a side, and the side of heaven had been the side of Israel. And the river Kishon — that ancient river, the river Kishon — swept the chariots away. The river had risen, and the river had remembered, and the iron had gone under.

Deborah sang into the song's own marching. My soul, march on with strength. Then the horse hoofs had stamped. The prancing of their strong ones had broken in the mud, and the wheels had caught, and the chariots had gone nowhere.

Then the song spoke a curse. The angel of the Lord had said, Curse Meroz. Curse bitterly its inhabitants — because they had not come to the help of the Lord against the mighty. The song laid the curse down, plain, and went on.

Then it turned to Jael. Blessed above women shall Jael be, the wife of Heber the Kenite. Blessed above women in the tent. The song rendered her not as the prose would render her — not as the hand that struck — but as a hand that gave and a hand that took, woven together in a single shape. He asked for water. She gave him milk. She brought him butter in a lordly dish. The lordly dish was the small luxury, the careful kindness, the welcoming gesture of a tent. She had put her hand to the tent peg, and her right hand to the workmen's hammer. With the hammer she struck Sisera. At her feet he bowed. He fell. He lay. At her feet he bowed; he fell. Where he bowed, there he fell down dead. The lines repeated, the way a song repeats. The falling came three times so the listener could feel it.

Then the song did something the prose could not do. It moved north. It moved across the hills to a house that had not appeared in the day's fighting, to a room with a window, to a mother no one in the song had ever seen. Through the window she looked out. Sisera's mother looked through the lattice. The song carried her voice. Why is his chariot so long in coming? Why do the wheels of his chariots wait?

Her wise ladies answered her, and she answered herself, talking quietly to her own waiting heart. They are dividing the plunder. They are gathering the dyed garments — a lady for each man, two ladies to every man, dyed garments embroidered on both sides for the neck of the spoil. He is delayed only because the spoil is great. He will come at evening. He will come tomorrow. He will come.

The hill country heard her. The song let her wait at the window, and the women of Israel on the hillside felt the wait, and the silence in the song held what the song would not say plainly. The chariot would not come. The wheels were under the river. The mother at the lattice would learn it in the slow way mothers learn.

Then the song closed. Deborah and Barak lifted the final words together, and the people on the hillside lifted them with them. "So let all your enemies perish, O Lord. But let those who love him be as the sun when it goes forth in its strength."

The song ended on the hill. The people carried it down into their villages. They sang it through the harvest. They sang it through the winter rains. They sang it to their children, and their children learned the names of the tribes and the name of Jael and the trembling of the mountains and the mother at the lattice. And in the hill country between Ramah and Bethel, Deborah went back to her palm tree, and Barak went back to Kedesh, and the highways were occupied again, and the wells were safe again, and the land had rest forty years.
