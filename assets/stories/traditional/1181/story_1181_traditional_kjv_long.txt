On the day the chariots were broken at the river, Deborah and Barak the son of Abinoam sang. They sang together upon the hillside above the valley, with the dust of the fight still settling in the lowlands beneath them, and the people of Israel sang with them. The song was not a report. It was not a counting of the spoils. It was the day folded into the shape of a song, the events laid down in lines that returned upon themselves, and the people learned it as they listened, and they carried it home.

They sang first of willingness. Because the leaders had taken the lead in Israel, because the people had offered themselves willingly, blessed be the LORD. The song was for the kings to hear. It was for the princes to give ear. Deborah sang, and Barak sang with her, and the song lifted into the open sky. I, even I, will sing unto the LORD, Deborah sang. I will sing praise unto the LORD, the God of Israel.

Then they sang of the coming of the LORD from Seir. When the LORD went out of Seir, when he marched out of the field of Edom, the earth had trembled. The heavens had dropped. The clouds had dropped water. The mountains had melted at his presence. Even Sinai had quaked, the mountain of fire and of cloud, at the presence of the LORD, the God of Israel. The song remembered an older trembling, set it beside the new, and made them one trembling.

Then it remembered the long quiet that had gone before. In the days of Shamgar the son of Anath, in the days of Jael, the highways had been unoccupied. Travellers had walked through byways, slipping along the narrow paths through the brush because the open roads were not safe. The inhabitants of the villages had ceased. They had ceased in Israel — until Deborah arose. Until she arose a mother in Israel.

The song lingered on what had been lost. They had chosen new gods; then was war in the gates. Was there a shield or spear seen among forty thousand in Israel? The song let the count stand. Forty thousand and not a spear. Then it turned, and its turning was its joy. The heart of the singer was toward the governors of Israel that had offered themselves willingly among the people. Bless ye the LORD.

Deborah sang unto herself in the song. Awake, awake, Deborah. Awake, awake, utter a song. The line came twice over, the way songs return, the way a drum beats and beats again. Arise, Barak, and lead thy captivity captive, thou son of Abinoam.

Then the song named the tribes. It named those that had come down. The princes of Issachar were with Deborah. As Issachar was, so was Barak — they had rushed into the valley at his feet. Zebulun was a people that jeoparded their lives unto the death. Naphtali also, upon the high places of the field. Out of Ephraim came down a root against Amalek, and Benjamin among their peoples, and the governors out of Machir, and they that handle the pen of the writer out of Zebulun.

Then it named those that had not come. By the watercourses of Reuben there had been great thoughts of heart. Great searchings of heart. But Reuben had abode among the sheepfolds, to hear the bleatings of the flocks, and had not come down. Gilead had abode beyond Jordan. Dan had remained in ships. Asher had continued on the sea shore, and had abode in his breaches. The song held both — them that came and them that came not — and let the holding be the judgment. The roll was kept in the song, and the song was sung for ever.

Then they sang the battle. The kings came and fought. The kings of Canaan fought in Taanach by the waters of Megiddo. They took no gain of money. They fought from heaven; the stars in their courses fought against Sisera. The night sky had taken a side, and the side of heaven had been the side of Israel. And the river of Kishon — that ancient river, the river Kishon — swept them away. The river had risen, and the river had remembered, and the iron had gone under.

Deborah sang into the song's own marching. O my soul, thou hast trodden down strength. Then were the horsehoofs broken by the means of the pransings, the pransings of their mighty ones, and the wheels had caught in the mire, and the chariots had gone nowhere.

Then the song spake a curse. Curse ye Meroz, said the angel of the LORD, curse ye bitterly the inhabitants thereof — because they came not to the help of the LORD, to the help of the LORD against the mighty. The song laid the curse down, plain, and went on.

Then it turned unto Jael. Blessed above women shall Jael be, the wife of Heber the Kenite. Blessed shall she be above women in the tent. The song rendered her not as the prose would render her — not as the hand that struck — but as a hand that gave and a hand that took, woven together in a single shape. He asked water, and she gave him milk. She brought forth butter in a lordly dish. The lordly dish was the small luxury, the careful kindness, the welcoming gesture of a tent. She had put her hand to the nail, and her right hand to the workmen's hammer. With the hammer she smote Sisera. At her feet he bowed, he fell, he lay down. At her feet he bowed, he fell. Where he bowed, there he fell down dead. The lines repeated, the way a song repeats. The falling came three times so the listener could feel it.

Then the song did somewhat the prose could not do. It moved north. It moved across the hills unto a house that had not appeared in the day's fighting, unto a room with a window, unto a mother no man in the song had ever seen. Through the window she looked out. The mother of Sisera looked out at a window, and cried through the lattice. The song carried her voice. Why is his chariot so long in coming? Why tarry the wheels of his chariots?

Her wise ladies answered her, yea, she returned answer to herself, talking softly unto her own waiting heart. They are dividing the spoil. They are gathering the dyed garments — to every man a damsel or two, to Sisera a prey of divers colours, a prey of divers colours of needlework, of divers colours of needlework on both sides, meet for the necks of them that take the spoil. He is delayed only because the spoil is great. He will come at evening. He will come on the morrow. He will come.

The hill country heard her. The song let her wait at the window, and the women of Israel upon the hillside felt the wait, and the silence in the song held what the song would not say plainly. The chariot would not come. The wheels were under the river. The mother at the lattice would learn it in the slow way mothers learn.

Then the song closed. Deborah and Barak lifted the final words together, and the people on the hillside lifted them with them. "So let all thine enemies perish, O LORD. But let them that love him be as the sun when he goeth forth in his might."

The song ended upon the hill. The people carried it down into their villages. They sang it through the harvest. They sang it through the winter rains. They sang it unto their children, and their children learned the names of the tribes and the name of Jael and the trembling of the mountains and the mother at the lattice. And in the hill country between Ramah and Beth-el, Deborah went back unto her palm tree, and Barak went back unto Kedesh, and the highways were occupied again, and the wells were safe again, and the land had rest forty years.
