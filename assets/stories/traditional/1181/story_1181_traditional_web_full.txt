On the day the chariots were broken at the river, Deborah and Barak son of Abinoam lifted their voices together. They stood on the slope above the valley where the iron had been undone, and they sang. The people of Israel gathered on the hillside around them, and the song carried over the brown grass and out into the air.

They sang first of willingness. Because the leaders had taken the lead in Israel, because the people had offered themselves willingly, blessed be the Lord. The song was for the kings to hear. It was for the princes to give ear. Deborah sang, and Barak sang with her, and the song lifted into the open sky.

Then they sang of the coming of the Lord from Seir. When the Lord went out of Seir, when he marched out of the field of Edom, the earth had trembled. The sky had dropped. The clouds had dropped water. The mountains had quaked at his presence. Even Sinai had quaked, the mountain of fire and of cloud, at the presence of the Lord, the God of Israel.

The song remembered the long quiet before. In the days of Shamgar son of Anath, in the days of Jael, the highways had been unoccupied. Travelers had walked through byways, slipping along narrow paths through the brush because the open roads were not safe. The rulers had ceased in Israel. They had ceased — until Deborah arose. Until she arose a mother in Israel.

She sang to herself in the song. Awake, awake, Deborah. Awake, awake, utter a song. Arise, Barak. Lead away the captives, son of Abinoam. The words rose and circled back, parallel and patterned, the way songs return upon themselves.

Then the song named the tribes. It named those who had come down. The princes of Issachar were with Deborah; Zebulun jeopardized their lives to the death; Naphtali also, on the high places of the field. Then it named those who had not come. By the watercourses of Reuben, there were great searchings of heart. Gilead lived beyond the Jordan. Dan remained in ships. Asher sat still at the haven of the sea. The song held both — those who came and those who did not — and let the holding be the judgment.

Then they sang the battle. The kings came and fought. The kings of Canaan fought at Taanach by the waters of Megiddo. They took no plunder of silver. From the sky the stars fought. From their courses, the stars fought against Sisera. And the river Kishon — that ancient river, the river Kishon — swept the chariots away.

Deborah sang into the song's own marching, "My soul, march on with strength."

Then the song turned to Jael. Blessed above women shall Jael be, blessed above women in the tent. He had asked for water. She had given him milk. She had brought him butter in a lordly dish. She had put her hand to the tent peg, and her right hand to the workmen's hammer. With the hammer she struck Sisera. At her feet he bowed. He fell. He lay still.

The song slowed. It moved across the valley and came to a window — a window in a house far to the north, the home of a mother no one in the song had ever seen. Through the window she looked out. Sisera's mother looked through the lattice. The song carried her voice. "Why is his chariot so long in coming? Why do the wheels of his chariots wait?" Her wise ladies answered her, and she answered herself, talking quietly to her own waiting heart. They are dividing the plunder. They are gathering the dyed garments. He is delayed only because the spoil is great.

The hill country heard her. The people heard her. They heard the mother at the lattice asking after a son whose chariot would never come — and the song let the silence hold.

Then it closed. Deborah and Barak lifted the final words together. "So let all your enemies perish, O Lord. But let those who love him be as the sun when it goes forth in its strength."

The song ended on the hill, and the people carried it home into their villages. They sang it through the harvest. They sang it through the winter rains. And the land had rest forty years.
