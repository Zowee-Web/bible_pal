On the day the chariots were broken at the river, Deborah and Barak the son of Abinoam lifted their voices together. They stood upon the slope above the valley where the iron had been undone, and they sang. The people of Israel gathered upon the hillside round about them, and the song carried over the brown grass and out into the air.

They sang first of willingness. Because the leaders had taken the lead in Israel, because the people had offered themselves willingly, blessed be the LORD. The song was for the kings to hear. It was for the princes to give ear. Deborah sang, and Barak sang with her, and the song lifted into the open sky.

Then they sang of the coming of the LORD from Seir. When the LORD went out of Seir, when he marched out of the field of Edom, the earth had trembled. The heavens had dropped. The clouds had dropped water. The mountains had melted at his presence. Even Sinai had quaked, the mountain of fire and of cloud, at the presence of the LORD, the God of Israel.

The song remembered the long quiet that had gone before. In the days of Shamgar the son of Anath, in the days of Jael, the highways had been unoccupied. Travellers had walked through byways, slipping along narrow paths through the brush because the open roads were not safe. The inhabitants of the villages had ceased. They had ceased in Israel — until Deborah arose. Until she arose a mother in Israel.

She sang unto herself in the song. Awake, awake, Deborah. Awake, awake, utter a song. Arise, Barak, and lead thy captivity captive, thou son of Abinoam. The words rose and circled back, parallel and patterned, the way songs return upon themselves.

Then the song named the tribes. It named those that had come down. The princes of Issachar were with Deborah; Zebulun jeoparded their lives unto the death; Naphtali also, upon the high places of the field. Then it named those that had not come. By the watercourses of Reuben there were great searchings of heart. Gilead abode beyond Jordan. Dan remained in ships. Asher continued on the sea shore. The song held both — them that came and them that came not — and let the holding be the judgment.

Then they sang the battle. The kings came and fought. The kings of Canaan fought in Taanach by the waters of Megiddo. They took no gain of money. They fought from heaven; the stars in their courses fought against Sisera. And the river of Kishon — that ancient river, the river Kishon — swept them away.

Deborah sang into the song's own marching, "O my soul, thou hast trodden down strength."

Then the song turned unto Jael. Blessed above women shall Jael be, blessed shall she be above women in the tent. He had asked water, and she had given him milk. She had brought forth butter in a lordly dish. She had put her hand to the nail, and her right hand to the workmen's hammer. With the hammer she smote Sisera. At her feet he bowed. He fell. He lay down.

The song slowed. It moved across the valley and came unto a window — a window in a house far to the north, the home of a mother no man in the song had ever seen. Through the window she looked out. The mother of Sisera looked out at a window, and cried through the lattice. The song carried her voice. "Why is his chariot so long in coming? Why tarry the wheels of his chariots?" Her wise ladies answered her, yea, she returned answer to herself, talking softly unto her own waiting heart. They are dividing the spoil. They are gathering the dyed garments. He is delayed only because the spoil is great.

The hill country heard her. The people heard her. They heard the mother at the lattice asking after a son whose chariot would never come — and the song let the silence hold.

Then it closed. Deborah and Barak lifted the final words together. "So let all thine enemies perish, O LORD. But let them that love him be as the sun when he goeth forth in his might."

The song ended upon the hill, and the people carried it home into their villages. They sang it through the harvest. They sang it through the winter rains. And the land had rest forty years.
