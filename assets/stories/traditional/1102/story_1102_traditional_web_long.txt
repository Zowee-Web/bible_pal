The crowd was already gathered at the shore when the boat appeared on the lake. They had been waiting since morning — fishermen who had pulled their nets onto the sand, women who had set their water jars on the flat rocks, a group of older men sitting in the shade of an overturned hull. Children ran along the water's edge, kicking up spray. When the boat drew close and the bow scraped the sand, the crowd moved forward. Voices rose. Hands reached out. They pressed toward Jesus as he stepped onto the shore, water still dripping from his sandals.

A man came pushing through from the back. He moved with urgency, his elbows working against the bodies on either side, not pausing when someone spoke to him. Jairus, a ruler of the synagogue — known in the town, a man of position. But his robe was wrinkled — he had not slept — and his face was gray. He reached the space before Jesus and fell — knees hitting the wet sand, hands flat on the ground.

"Come to my house," he said. His voice was tight, the words fast. His only daughter — twelve years old — was dying.

Jesus went with him. The crowd came too, pressing close on every side. They moved up from the shore into the narrow street that climbed toward the town. Bodies bumped and shuffled. Sandals scraped on the paving stones. Jesus walked among them with Jairus at his side, and the father's eyes were fixed ahead, his steps quick, his hands opening and closing.

Behind Jesus, deeper in the press of the crowd, a woman moved. She was thin — her robe hung loose at her shoulders. She had been bleeding for twelve years. A constant sickness, draining her day by day. She had spent everything she had on physicians. She had gone from one to another, and none had healed her. She was weaker now than twelve years ago, and poorer, and alone. She pushed through the crowd from behind, working her way close to Jesus. She reached forward and touched the fringe of his garment with the tips of her fingers.

Immediately the bleeding stopped. She felt it — the cessation, sudden and complete. Twelve years of constant loss, and then nothing. Her hand withdrew.

Jesus stopped. The crowd kept pressing forward for a moment, bodies moving against his stillness. He turned.

"Who touched me?" he said.

Peter was beside him. He looked at the crowd — dozens of bodies pushed together. "Master," he said, "the multitudes press and crush you. How can you ask who touched you?"

"Someone touched me," Jesus said. "Power went out from me."

The crowd grew quiet. People stepped back slightly, opening a small space. The woman saw she could not hide. She had thought to touch him and slip away unseen. But he had felt it. She came forward trembling. Her face was drawn. She fell on the ground before him and spoke.

She told him everything. Before all the people gathered there in the street, she told him. How long she had been sick. How she had spent all she had. How she had come through the crowd and touched the fringe of his garment and been healed in that instant. Her voice shook, but she spoke clearly.

Jesus looked down at her.

"Daughter," he said. "Your faith has made you well. Go in peace."

The woman rose and moved back into the crowd. The people parted for her.

Jairus stood three steps away. His face had not changed. Through the whole exchange — the stopping, the question, the woman's confession — his daughter had been dying. The minutes pressed on him. He stood rigid, his hands at his sides, his jaw tight.

While Jesus was still speaking, a man came running from the direction of Jairus' house. He came fast through the crowd, pushing people aside. He was breathing hard. He reached Jairus and spoke directly to him.

"Your daughter is dead," he said. "Do not trouble the Teacher any longer."

The words struck the air. Jairus went still. The crowd around them was silent.

Jesus turned to Jairus. He spoke quietly, the words for him alone.

"Do not be afraid," he said. "Only believe, and she will be made well."

They walked on. The crowd did not follow now — only Jesus, Jairus, and three of the disciples: Peter, John, and James. They reached the house. The sound of mourning reached them before they turned the corner — women wailing, voices raised in grief, the beating of hands against chests. Mourners had gathered in the courtyard already.

Jesus entered the house with the three disciples and the girl's parents. The interior was dim — oil lamps burning, the smell of spices. People crowded the front room, weeping.

"Do not weep," Jesus said. "She is not dead, but sleeping."

The weeping stopped. Several of the mourners looked up. Then they laughed at him. They had seen the girl's body. They had felt for her breath and found none.

Jesus turned from them and walked through the house to the inner room where the girl lay. Her parents followed. Peter, John, and James stood behind them in the doorway.

The room was small. A single lamp burned on a shelf. The girl lay on a low mat against the wall. She was still. Her face was pale, drained of color. Her hands lay at her sides. She looked very small — twelve years old, thin, her hair spread on the mat beneath her.

Jesus crossed the room and knelt beside her. He reached out and took her hand. It was small and cool and motionless in his. He held it.

"Child, arise," he said.

The room was silent. Then the girl's chest moved. A breath came into her, then another. Color returned to her face — a faint warmth spreading beneath the skin. Her eyes opened. She looked at the face above her. Then she sat up — quickly, without hesitation, the way a child rises from sleep. She looked around the room at the faces staring at her.

Her mother made a sound. Her father stepped forward and stopped. The disciples in the doorway said nothing.

The girl was alive. She sat on the edge of the mat, her feet on the floor, looking around with clear eyes. She was breathing.

"Give her something to eat," Jesus said.
