In the first year of Darius the son of Ahasuerus, of the offspring of the Medes, who had been made king over the realm of the Chaldeans, Daniel sat in his chamber with the scroll of Jeremiah open before him.

The lamps had been trimmed. Their light fell on the parchment in a low warm wash, and Daniel followed the line of the prophet's writing with his finger. He counted. He read again, and counted again. Seventy years. The word of the Lord had set a number on the desolation of Jerusalem, and the number was nearing its end.

Daniel looked up from the scroll. He understood what time it was. He understood the way a man understands the turn of a season — not as a thought arriving from outside but as something settling into the bones.

He set his face to the Lord his God.

He laid the scroll down, and he rose, and he made ready in the manner of a man who has read what he has read. He took off the garments of the court. He put on sackcloth. The coarse weave dragged at his skin. He went outside the chamber and gathered ashes from the dead coals of the fire, and he carried them back in his hands, and he let them down upon his head. They marked his hair. They marked his face. They settled on his shoulders in a fine gray layer that did not brush away. He sat. He set his face toward Jerusalem. Jerusalem was far. The city lay in rubble beyond the long road of the exile, but the direction was the direction, and the body knew the way to bow.

He did not eat. The hours moved through the chamber, and the hours moved through his body, and he kept his fast, and he opened his mouth in prayer.

"O Lord," he said. "The great and dreadful God, who keeps covenant and loving kindness with those who love him and keep his commandments."

He paused. He took a breath. The confession came next, and he did not hurry it. He had been chosen by the Lord and lifted up in the courts of foreign kings, but he had also been a son of his people, and he knew the sin of his people the way a man knows the smell of his own house. He named it openly.

"We have sinned," he said. "We have dealt perversely. We have done wickedly. We have rebelled, turning aside from your precepts and from your ordinances."

The cadence of the confession was the cadence of a man laying down stones in a wall. One verb. Then the next verb. Then the next. He did not soften it. He named it four times because the thing was a fourfold thing, and a single naming would not hold what had to be held.

"We have not listened," he said. "We have not listened to your servants the prophets, who spoke in your name to our kings, our princes, our fathers, and to all the people of the land. Lord, righteousness belongs to you, but to us confusion of face."

His voice was steady. The ashes did not move.

"To us belongs confusion of face," he said. "To our kings, to our princes, to our fathers, because we have sinned against you. To the Lord our God belong mercies and forgiveness, for we have rebelled against him."

He held both in his mouth at once. The mercy that belonged to the Lord. The rebellion that belonged to the people. He did not separate them. He did not plead the mercy as if the rebellion had not been. He did not bury the rebellion as if there were no mercy beyond it. He laid them side by side because they belonged side by side, and the Lord knew them both.

"Yes," he said, "all Israel have transgressed your law. The curse and the oath written in the law of Moses the servant of God has been poured out on us. He has confirmed his words, which he spoke against us. As it is written in the law of Moses, all this evil has come on us."

The lamps burned. The ashes lay on his shoulders. The fast had thinned his voice. His voice grew quieter as it went lower into the prayer, but it did not stop. The prayer moved through the long descent of confession, and then it began to turn.

"Now therefore, our God," he said, "listen to the prayer of your servant and to his petitions, and cause your face to shine on your sanctuary that is desolate, for the Lord's sake. My God, turn your ear and hear. Open your eyes and see our desolations, and the city which is called by your name. For we do not present our petitions before you for our righteousness, but for your great mercies' sake."

The pleading had begun. The pleading was not a leaving of the confession. The pleading was the confession turning toward the only direction it could turn — toward the mercy it had named.

Then his voice broke open, and he cried out.

"O Lord, hear. O Lord, forgive. O Lord, listen and do. Don't defer, for your own sake, my God, because your city and your people are called by your name."

The four cries went up. Hear. Forgive. Listen. Do. Each one short. Each one carrying the weight of seventy years. They were the prayer of a man who had read the scroll, and known what time it was, and laid down everything but the asking.

While Daniel was speaking — while he was praying and confessing his sin and the sin of his people Israel and presenting his supplication before the Lord his God for the holy mountain of his God — yes, while he was still speaking in prayer — the angel Gabriel came.

He came in swift flight. The text says he was caused to fly swiftly, and the words mean what they mean. The messenger moved. The distance between heaven and the chamber of an old man in Babylon collapsed at the speed of the Lord's intention. Gabriel came at the time of the evening offering, which was the hour the priests in another country, in a temple that no longer stood, had once lifted the lamb upon the altar. The hour had not been forgotten. Daniel had kept it in his body, and the Lord had kept it in heaven, and the two had met.

Gabriel touched him.

Daniel felt the touch upon his shoulder. He felt the hand of the messenger come down where the ashes lay, and he stopped his words, and he bowed his face to the ground. The floor was cool against his forehead. His hands lay open at his sides. The sackcloth pressed against his ribs.

"Daniel," Gabriel said. "I have now come forth to give you wisdom and understanding. At the beginning of your petitions the commandment went out, and I have come to tell you, for you are greatly beloved."

The answer had been already on the way. While Daniel was still naming the sin, the word had already been spoken. While Daniel was still listing the four verbs, the messenger had already been moving. The prayer had not been a wedge to pry open a closed door. The door had not been closed.

The lamps burned low in the chamber, and the evening had come, and Daniel kept his face to the ground, and he heard the words of the messenger settle around him the way the ashes had settled upon his hair, and he listened, and he held still.
