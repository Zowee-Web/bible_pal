In the first year of Darius the son of Ahasuerus, of the seed of the Medes, which was made king over the realm of the Chaldeans, Daniel sat in his chamber with the scroll of Jeremiah open before him.

The lamps had been trimmed. Their light fell upon the parchment in a low warm wash, and Daniel followed the line of the prophet's writing with his finger. He counted. He read again, and counted again. Seventy years. The word of the Lord had set a number unto the desolation of Jerusalem, and the number was nigh unto its end.

Daniel looked up from the scroll. He understood what time it was. He understood the way a man understandeth the turn of a season — not as a thought arriving from without but as something settling into the bones.

He set his face unto the Lord his God.

He laid the scroll down, and he rose, and he made ready in the manner of a man who hath read what he hath read. He took off the garments of the court. He put on sackcloth. The coarse weave dragged at his skin. He went forth from the chamber and gathered ashes from the dead coals of the fire, and he carried them back in his hands, and he let them down upon his head. They marked his hair. They marked his face. They settled upon his shoulders in a fine gray layer that did not brush away. He sat. He set his face toward Jerusalem. Jerusalem was far. The city lay in rubble beyond the long road of the exile, but the direction was the direction, and the body knew the way to bow.

He did not eat. The hours moved through the chamber, and the hours moved through his body, and he kept his fast, and he opened his mouth in prayer.

"O Lord," he said. "The great and dreadful God, keeping the covenant and mercy to them that love him and keep his commandments."

He paused. He took a breath. The confession came next, and he did not hurry it. He had been chosen of the Lord and lifted up in the courts of foreign kings, but he had also been a son of his people, and he knew the sin of his people the way a man knoweth the smell of his own house. He named it openly.

"We have sinned," he said. "We have committed iniquity. We have done wickedly. We have rebelled, even by departing from thy precepts and from thy judgments."

The cadence of the confession was the cadence of a man laying down stones in a wall. One verb. Then the next verb. Then the next. He did not soften it. He named it four times because the thing was a fourfold thing, and a single naming would not hold what had to be held.

"Neither have we hearkened," he said. "Neither have we hearkened unto thy servants the prophets, which spake in thy name to our kings, our princes, our fathers, and to all the people of the land. O Lord, righteousness belongeth unto thee, but unto us confusion of face."

His voice was steady. The ashes did not move.

"Unto us belongeth confusion of face," he said. "To our kings, to our princes, to our fathers, because we have sinned against thee. To the Lord our God belong mercies and forgivenesses, though we have rebelled against him."

He held both in his mouth at once. The mercy that belonged unto the Lord. The rebellion that belonged unto the people. He did not separate them. He did not plead the mercy as if the rebellion had not been. He did not bury the rebellion as if there were no mercy beyond it. He laid them side by side because they belonged side by side, and the Lord knew them both.

"Yea," he said, "all Israel have transgressed thy law. The curse and the oath written in the law of Moses the servant of God hath been poured out upon us. He hath confirmed his words, which he spake against us. As it is written in the law of Moses, all this evil is come upon us."

The lamps burned. The ashes lay upon his shoulders. The fast had thinned his voice. His voice grew quieter as it went lower into the prayer, but it did not cease. The prayer moved through the long descent of confession, and then it began to turn.

"Now therefore, O our God," he said, "hear the prayer of thy servant and his supplications, and cause thy face to shine upon thy sanctuary that is desolate, for the Lord's sake. O my God, incline thine ear, and hear. Open thine eyes, and behold our desolations, and the city which is called by thy name. For we do not present our supplications before thee for our righteousnesses, but for thy great mercies."

The pleading was begun. The pleading was no leaving of the confession. The pleading was the confession turning unto the only direction it could turn — unto the mercy it had named.

Then his voice broke open, and he cried out.

"O Lord, hear; O Lord, forgive; O Lord, hearken and do; defer not, for thine own sake, O my God: for thy city and thy people are called by thy name."

The four cries went up. Hear. Forgive. Hearken. Do. Each one short. Each one carrying the weight of seventy years. They were the prayer of a man who had read the scroll, and known what time it was, and laid down everything but the asking.

Whiles Daniel was speaking — whiles he was praying and confessing his sin and the sin of his people Israel and presenting his supplication before the Lord his God for the holy mountain of his God — yea, whiles he was yet speaking in prayer — the angel Gabriel came.

He came in swift flight. The text saith he was caused to fly swiftly, and the words mean what they mean. The messenger moved. The distance between heaven and the chamber of an old man in Babylon collapsed at the speed of the Lord's intention. Gabriel came about the time of the evening oblation, which was the hour the priests in another country, in a temple that no longer stood, had once lifted the lamb upon the altar. The hour had not been forgotten. Daniel had kept it in his body, and the Lord had kept it in heaven, and the two had met.

Gabriel touched him.

Daniel felt the touch upon his shoulder. He felt the hand of the messenger come down where the ashes lay, and he stopped his words, and he bowed his face to the ground. The floor was cool against his forehead. His hands lay open at his sides. The sackcloth pressed against his ribs.

"O Daniel," Gabriel said. "I am now come forth to give thee skill and understanding. At the beginning of thy supplications the commandment came forth, and I am come to shew thee; for thou art greatly beloved."

The answer had been already on the way. Whiles Daniel was yet naming the sin, the word had already been spoken. Whiles Daniel was yet listing the four verbs, the messenger had already been moving. The prayer had not been a wedge to pry open a closed door. The door had not been closed.

The lamps burned low in the chamber, and the evening was come, and Daniel kept his face to the ground, and he heard the words of the messenger settle around him the way the ashes had settled upon his hair, and he hearkened, and he held still.
