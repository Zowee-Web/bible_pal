Yahweh sent Nathan to David.

The king was in his house in Jerusalem. It was in the second year of his reign over all Israel — the year in which the kingdom had been at peace for a long enough time that the king had not gone out with the armies in the spring, as the other kings of the earth went out. He had stayed in Jerusalem. The army had gone to Rabbah without him. While the army was at Rabbah, the matter of Bathsheba had happened on the roof of the king's house.

The matter of Uriah the Hittite had followed. The order to Joab. The placement of Uriah at the hottest part of the battle. The word that had come back to the king that Uriah was dead among the slain.

Bathsheba had mourned for her husband, in the days that were appointed.

When the mourning days had ended, David had sent for her. He had brought her into his house. He had taken her to be his wife. The child she had conceived on the roof was growing in her by the time of these days.

David had returned to the business of being king. He had heard reports from the messengers. He had received those who had come to him with petitions. He had ruled in the gate of his city. The days had passed.

Then Nathan came in to David and stood before him.

He had not been sent for. He had not been summoned by the king's officers. He had come because Yahweh had sent him. He stood before David and did not bow himself low. He stood as a man who carried something in his mouth that he had not chosen to carry.

He said —

"There were two men in one city. The one was rich. The other was poor.

The rich man had very many flocks and herds.

But the poor man had nothing — except one little ewe lamb, which he had bought and raised. It grew up together with him, and with his children. It ate of his own food. It drank of his own cup. It lay in his bosom. It was like a daughter to him.

A traveler came to the rich man. The rich man spared to take of his own flock and of his own herd, to prepare for the wayfaring man who had come to him. He took the poor man's lamb, and prepared it for the man who had come to him."

The parable had been clean and simple, the way a parable from a prophet is. The rich man. The poor man. The lamb that was loved like a daughter. The traveler. The rich man's grasping of what was not his to take.

David heard it.

David's anger burned hot against the man of the story.

He sat up straighter in his seat. The heat of the matter came into his voice. He felt the judgment forming in his mouth before he had spoken it.

He said to Nathan, "As Yahweh lives, the man who has done this deserves to die."

He went further. He pronounced the restoration.

"He must restore the lamb fourfold, because he did this thing, and because he had no pity."

The words hung in the air of the chamber where he had spoken them.

Nathan looked at David.

He did not raise his voice. He did not point his hand. He did not move toward the king. He stood where he had been standing when he had come in.

He said, "You are the man."

The three words were very small in the room.

He waited a moment. Then he gave the rest of Yahweh's word to him.

He said, "This is what Yahweh, the God of Israel, says: I anointed you king over Israel, and I delivered you out of the hand of Saul. I gave you your master's house, and your master's wives into your bosom, and gave you the house of Israel and of Judah. And if that had been too little, I would have added to you many more such things."

The king did not interrupt him. The king did not rise from his seat. The king did not lift his hand to the men who stood at the door of his chamber. The king did not look away from the prophet who had spoken to him.

Nathan finished what he had been sent to say. Then he stood quiet before the king. He waited for the king to answer him.

The room was very quiet.

The judgment David had pronounced against the man of the parable — the death deserved, the fourfold restoration, the pity that had been lacking — was still hanging in the air where he had said it. The words could not be unsaid. They had been said by his own mouth, in the hearing of the prophet who had brought him the story.

The poor man's lamb of the parable was still in the room with them, in the listener's ear, lying in the bosom of the man who had loved it like a daughter. The lamb that had been taken. The lamb that had been prepared for the wayfaring man who had come.

The prophet was still standing before him. He had not moved from the place where he had stood when he had pointed nothing and raised nothing and said the three words that had named the king.

The king was still seated where he had been seated when Nathan came in. His hands rested on the arms of his seat. His eyes did not lift from the prophet. His breath went out, and came back in, and went out again before he moved.

The king did not speak. The chamber held its silence around him, and the silence held him there, for a long while, before he was ready to make the answer that was forming in his own chest.
