And the Lord sent Nathan unto David.

The king was in his house in Jerusalem. It was in the second year of his reign over all Israel — the year in which the kingdom had been at peace for a long enough time that the king had not gone out with the armies in the spring, as the other kings of the earth went out. He had tarried in Jerusalem. The army had gone unto Rabbah without him. While the army was at Rabbah, the matter of Bathsheba had happened upon the roof of the king's house.

The matter of Uriah the Hittite had followed. The order unto Joab. The placement of Uriah at the hottest part of the battle. The word that had come back unto the king that Uriah was dead among the slain.

Bathsheba had mourned for her husband, in the days that were appointed.

When the mourning days had ended, David had sent for her. He had brought her into his house. He had taken her to be his wife. The child she had conceived upon the roof was growing in her by the time of these days.

David had returned to the business of being king. He had heard reports from the messengers. He had received them that came unto him with petitions. He had ruled in the gate of his city. The days had passed.

And Nathan came unto him and stood before him.

He had not been sent for. He had not been summoned by the king's officers. He had come because the Lord had sent him. He stood before David and bowed not himself low. He stood as a man who carried something in his mouth that he had not chosen to carry.

He said —

"There were two men in one city. The one rich, and the other poor.

The rich man had exceeding many flocks and herds.

But the poor man had nothing, save one little ewe lamb, which he had bought and nourished up. And it grew up together with him, and with his children. It did eat of his own meat, and drank of his own cup, and lay in his bosom, and was unto him as a daughter.

And there came a traveller unto the rich man. And he spared to take of his own flock and of his own herd, to dress for the wayfaring man that was come unto him. But he took the poor man's lamb, and dressed it for the man that was come to him."

The parable had been clean and simple, the way a parable from a prophet is. The rich man. The poor man. The lamb that was loved as a daughter. The traveller. The rich man's grasping of that which was not his to take.

David heard it.

And David's anger was greatly kindled against the man of the story.

He sat up straighter in his seat. The heat of the matter came into his voice. He felt the judgment forming in his mouth before he had spoken it.

He said unto Nathan, "As the Lord liveth, the man that hath done this thing shall surely die."

He went further. He pronounced the restoration.

"And he shall restore the lamb fourfold, because he did this thing, and because he had no pity."

The words hung in the air of the chamber where he had spoken them.

And Nathan looked at David.

He raised not his voice. He pointed not his hand. He moved not toward the king. He stood where he had been standing when he had come in.

He said, "Thou art the man."

The three words were very small in the chamber.

He waited a moment. Then he gave the rest of the word of the Lord unto him.

He said, "Thus saith the Lord God of Israel: I anointed thee king over Israel, and I delivered thee out of the hand of Saul. And I gave thee thy master's house, and thy master's wives into thy bosom, and gave thee the house of Israel and of Judah; and if that had been too little, I would moreover have given unto thee such and such things."

The king interrupted him not. The king rose not from his seat. The king lifted not his hand unto the men that stood at the door of his chamber. The king looked not away from the prophet that had spoken unto him.

Nathan finished that which he had been sent to say. Then he stood quiet before the king. He waited for the king to answer him.

The chamber was very quiet.

The judgment David had pronounced against the man of the parable — the death deserved, the fourfold restoration, the pity that had been lacking — was yet hanging in the air where he had said it. The words could not be unsaid. They had been said by his own mouth, in the hearing of the prophet who had brought him the story.

The poor man's lamb of the parable was yet in the chamber with them, in the listener's ear, lying in the bosom of the man that had loved it as a daughter. The lamb that had been taken. The lamb that had been dressed for the wayfaring man that was come.

The prophet was yet standing before him. He had not moved from the place where he had stood when he had pointed nothing and raised nothing and spake the three words that had named the king.

The king was yet seated where he had been seated when Nathan came in. His hands rested upon the arms of his seat. His eyes lifted not from the prophet. His breath went out, and came back in, and went out again before he moved.

The king spake not. The chamber held its silence round about him, and the silence held him there, for a long while, before he was ready to make the answer that was forming within his own chest.
