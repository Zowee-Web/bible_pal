The Lord appeared to Abraham by the oaks of Mamre. It was the middle of the day, the hottest hour. The sun blazed white in a cloudless sky. Heat shimmered above the dry ground. Abraham sat in the entrance of his tent, looking out at the open land. The oak trees nearby cast dark patches of shade on the dusty earth.

Abraham looked up. Three men stood a short distance away. He had not heard them approach. They simply appeared, standing in the bright sunlight.

As soon as he saw them, Abraham sprang to his feet. He was an old man, but he ran — actually ran — toward the three visitors. When he reached them, he bowed low, all the way to the ground.

"My lord," Abraham said, "if I have found favor in your sight, please do not pass by your servant." His voice was eager and welcoming. "Let a little water be brought. Wash your feet and rest yourselves under the tree."

He did not stop there. "I will bring a piece of bread so that you may refresh your heart. After that you may go on your way, since you have come to your servant."

The three men looked at Abraham and said, "Do as you have said."

Abraham turned and hurried. He moved faster than you would expect for a man his age. He ducked into the tent where Sarah was and spoke quickly. "Quickly! Take three measures of fine flour, knead it, and make cakes."

Sarah began working right away. Flour, water, kneading the dough with strong hands. The bread cakes would need to bake on hot stones.

But Abraham was already running again. He went to the herd and looked over the animals. He chose a calf — young and tender, the very best he had. He gave it to a servant, who hurried to prepare it. The meat was cooked carefully.

When everything was ready, Abraham gathered the meal together. He brought curds and milk and the tender calf that had been prepared. He carried it all out to where the three visitors sat under the great oak tree. He set the food before them, spread out on cloths — a proper feast. Not the "little piece of bread" he had modestly offered.

Abraham did not sit down with them. He stood nearby under the tree while they ate. He watched, ready to bring anything else they might need.

When the visitors had eaten, one of them spoke. "Where is Sarah your wife?"

Abraham answered, "There, in the tent."

Then the visitor said something remarkable. "I will certainly return to you about this time next year, and Sarah your wife will have a son."

A son. Sarah was very old. Abraham was very old. They had waited for a child for more years than most people live. They had been promised a child before, long ago, and it had never happened. Year after year had passed.

Sarah was listening at the tent door, just behind where the visitors sat. She heard every word. And she laughed.

It was not a happy laugh or a joyful laugh. It was the kind of laugh that comes when something sounds impossible. Sarah thought to herself, "After I am worn out and my lord is old, will I have this pleasure?"

But the Lord — for it was the Lord speaking through the visitor — said to Abraham, "Why did Sarah laugh? Why did she say, 'Will I really bear a child since I am old?'"

Then the Lord asked a question that hung in the warm air under the oak tree. "Is anything too hard for the Lord?"

The words settled over them like shade on a hot day. Is anything too hard? Anything at all?

"At the appointed time I will return to you," the Lord said, "about this time next year, and Sarah will have a son."

Sarah heard the question and the promise. And she was afraid. "I did not laugh," she said from the tent entrance.

But the Lord said gently, "No, but you did laugh."

The oak leaves rustled in a small breeze. The remains of the meal lay on the cloths under the tree. The three visitors sat in the shade, and Abraham stood nearby. The promise had been spoken again — clear and certain. A son. Next year. Nothing too hard for the Lord.
