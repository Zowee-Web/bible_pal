The Lord appeared unto Abraham in the plains of Mamre. It was the middle of the day, the hottest hour. The sun blazed white in a cloudless sky. Heat shimmered above the dry ground. Abraham sat in the tent door, looking out upon the open land. The oaks nigh at hand cast dark patches of shade upon the dusty earth.

Abraham lifted up his eyes. And lo, three men stood a little way off. He had not heard them approach. They simply appeared, standing in the bright sunlight.

When he saw them, Abraham sprang to his feet. He was an old man, but he ran — verily he ran — to meet the three visitors. When he came unto them, he bowed himself toward the ground.

"My Lord," Abraham said, "if now I have found favour in thy sight, pass not away, I pray thee, from thy servant." His voice was eager and welcoming. "Let a little water, I pray you, be fetched. Wash your feet, and rest yourselves under the tree."

He did not stop there. "And I will fetch a morsel of bread, and comfort ye your hearts. After that ye shall pass on, for therefore are ye come to your servant."

The three men looked upon Abraham and said, "So do, as thou hast said."

Abraham turned and made haste. He moved faster than ye would look for in a man of his years. He went into the tent where Sarah was and spake quickly. "Make ready quickly three measures of fine meal, knead it, and make cakes upon the hearth."

Sarah began to work straightway. Meal, water, kneading the dough with strong hands. The cakes would need to bake upon hot stones.

But Abraham was already running again. He ran unto the herd and looked over the cattle. He chose a calf — tender and good, the very best he had. He gave it unto a servant, who hasted to dress it. The meat was prepared with care.

When all was ready, Abraham gathered the meal together. He brought butter and milk, and the calf which he had dressed. He carried it all out to where the three visitors sat under the great oak. He set it before them, spread out upon cloths — a proper feast. Not the "morsel of bread" he had modestly offered.

Abraham did not sit down with them. He stood by them under the tree while they did eat. He watched, ready to fetch any thing else they might have need of.

When the visitors had eaten, one of them spake. "Where is Sarah thy wife?"

Abraham answered, "Behold, in the tent."

Then the visitor said something wondrous. "I will certainly return unto thee according to the time of life, and lo, Sarah thy wife shall have a son."

A son. Sarah was well stricken in years. Abraham was well stricken in years. They had waited for a child more years than most folk live. They had been promised a child before, long ago, and it had never come to pass. Year after year had passed away.

Sarah was hearkening at the tent door, just behind where the visitors sat. She heard every word. And she laughed.

It was not a happy laugh nor a joyful laugh. It was the manner of laugh that cometh when a thing soundeth impossible. Sarah said within herself, "After I am waxed old shall I have pleasure, my lord being old also?"

But the Lord — for it was the Lord speaking through the visitor — said unto Abraham, "Wherefore did Sarah laugh, saying, 'Shall I of a surety bear a child, which am old?'"

Then the Lord asked a question that hung in the warm air beneath the oak. "Is any thing too hard for the Lord?"

The words settled over them like shade in the heat of the day. Is any thing too hard? Any thing at all?

"At the time appointed I will return unto thee," the Lord said, "according to the time of life, and Sarah shall have a son."

Sarah heard the question and the promise. And she was afraid. "I laughed not," she said from the tent door.

But the Lord said gently, "Nay; but thou didst laugh."

The oak leaves rustled in a small wind. The remains of the meal lay upon the cloths under the tree. The three visitors sat in the shade, and Abraham stood by them. The promise had been spoken again — clear and certain. A son. Next year. Nothing too hard for the Lord.
