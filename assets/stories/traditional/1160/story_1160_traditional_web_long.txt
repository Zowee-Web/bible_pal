When Mordecai found out all that was done, he tore his clothes in the street where he stood. He took ashes from the nearest hearth and poured them over his head, and he bound sackcloth around his body in place of the robes he had let fall, and he went out into the middle of the city of Susa. He cried a loud and bitter cry as he walked. The cry rose above the rooftops. People stepped aside in their doorways. Merchants stopped their counting and looked up. Children stilled where they were playing. A man in sackcloth and ashes was a man whose grief had broken the small order of the day, and the day made room for him.

He came as far as the king's gate. He did not pass through it. None clothed in sackcloth was permitted past that threshold, and Mordecai knew it. So he stood in the open square in front of the gate, in plain sight of every official and runner and merchant who came and went, and there he waited, and there he wept.

He was not alone in his grief. In every province of the kingdom, wherever the king's commandment and his decree had come, the same scene was unfolding. There was great mourning among the Jews. There was fasting. There was weeping and wailing. Many lay in sackcloth and ashes on the floors of their houses, and the houses themselves were silent except for that sound.

Inside the palace, in the quiet of the inner rooms, Esther knew none of it yet. The walls of the palace were thick. The voice of the city did not pass through them. Then her maids came in. Her eunuchs came after her maids. They told her what was happening at the gate, and the queen was greatly grieved.

Her first response was practical. She sent garments out to Mordecai. Fresh robes folded over the arms of a servant, robes such as a man of the court might wear, to clothe him and to take away the sackcloth from him. The servant carried them out through the gate to the square. He held them out to Mordecai. Mordecai did not receive them. The servant came back with the cloth still folded as it had been.

Then Esther called for Hatach. He was one of the king's eunuchs, whom the king himself had appointed to attend her, and he stood ready in her presence. She gave him a command. Go to Mordecai. Find out what this is, and why it is.

Hatach went out.

He passed through the inner corridors of the palace. He passed the guards at the king's gate and went out into the city square that lay before it. He came to Mordecai where he stood among the ashes. He came close. Mordecai told him everything. He told him what Haman had done. He told him the exact sum of the money that Haman had promised to pay into the king's treasuries for the destruction of the Jews. He drew out a copy of the writing of the decree itself, the one that had been given out at Susa, and he placed it into Hatach's hands. Show this to Esther, he said. Declare it to her. Charge her to go in to the king. To make supplication to him. To make request before him for her people.

Hatach took the writing and went back the way he had come. He passed again through the king's gate. He came to Esther in her chamber and he told her the words of Mordecai, and he gave into her hands the copy of the decree.

She read it. She did not answer at once.

Then she spoke to Hatach, and through him she sent her answer back. All the king's servants and the people of the king's provinces know what happens to anyone, whether man or woman, who comes to the king into the inner court without being called. There is one law for them. They are put to death. There is one exception. The king may hold out the golden scepter, and the one to whom he holds it out may live. I have not been called to come in to the king these thirty days.

Hatach received her words. He turned and went out.

He passed the guards. He crossed the gate. He came into the square and he came to Mordecai where Mordecai still stood, and he told him what the queen had said.

Mordecai listened. He looked toward the gate. He looked away from it. Then he gave Hatach his answer to bring back.

"Don't think to yourself," he said, "that you will escape in the king's house any more than all the Jews. For if you remain silent now, relief and deliverance will come to the Jews from another place. But you and your father's house will perish. And who knows whether you have come to the kingdom for such a time as this."

Hatach turned again. He went back across the square. He passed the guards. He came into the palace, and he came to the chamber where Esther waited, and he spoke the words into the room.

She was quiet for a long while. The words sat in the room with her. She did not rise. She did not pace. Outside her window, the cry that had been the sound of the city all day was now also the sound of her own house. The light through the lattice fell on the floor in narrow bars. A maiden somewhere in an outer room set down a jar.

After a long time, Esther spoke. Her voice was low, and Hatach leaned to hear it. Then it steadied.

"Go," she said. "Gather together all the Jews who are present in Susa. Fast for me. Don't eat or drink three days, night or day. I and my maidens will also fast the same way. Then I will go in to the king, which is against the law."

She paused. Then she finished.

"And if I perish, I perish."

Hatach bowed his head. He received her words. He turned and went out of the chamber. He crossed the gate one last time. He came into the square where Mordecai still stood among the ashes, and he gave him the message that the queen had sent.

Mordecai listened. He nodded. He went his way, and he did according to all that Esther had commanded him.
