Jesus left Judea and departed again into Galilee. He had to pass through Samaria.

The road through Samaria climbed and fell across pale hills. The disciples walked behind him, their sandals raising small puffs of dust. The sun rose to the top of the sky and stayed there. By the time they came to a city of Samaria called Sychar, near the parcel of ground that Jacob had given to his son Joseph, the heat was full upon the day.

Jacob's well was there. The well was deep. The stone rim was worn smooth by the hands of generations.

Jesus, being wearied with his journey, sat down by the well. He set his hands on the warm stone. He let his head bow for a moment, then lifted it. It was about the sixth hour. The sun was directly overhead. The shadows were short. The disciples had gone away into the city to buy food, and he was alone by the well.

The plain around him was still. A few goats moved in the distance. The city showed its low walls and white roofs through the heat shimmer. No one was out on the path. At the sixth hour, the women of the town came at dawn or in the cool of the evening. The well stood quiet under the sun.

Then footsteps came along the path.

A woman of Samaria came to draw water. She walked alone. Her water jar rode on her shoulder, balanced with one hand. Her sandals scuffed the dust. She came at noon because at noon no one else came, and she had learned, year by year, to choose the heat over the eyes of other women. She did not lift her face until she reached the well.

When she looked up, she saw him.

Jesus said to her, "Give me a drink."

The woman went still. She set the jar down on the rim with both hands. The clay tapped against the stone. She looked at him, then past him, then at him again.

"How is it that you, being a Jew, ask a drink from me, a Samaritan woman?"

For Jews have no dealings with Samaritans.

Jesus answered her, "If you knew the gift of God, and who it is who says to you, 'Give me a drink,' you would have asked him, and he would have given you living water."

The woman looked at his hands. They were empty. She looked at the dark mouth of the well.

"Sir, you have nothing to draw with, and the well is deep. Where then do you get that living water? Are you greater than our father Jacob, who gave us the well, and drank from it himself, and his children, and his cattle?"

Jesus did not move from the rim. He spoke quietly, and his voice carried in the still air.

"Everyone who drinks of this water will thirst again, but whoever drinks of the water that I will give him will never thirst again. The water that I will give him will become in him a well of water springing up to eternal life."

The woman's hands rested on the lip of the jar. She did not look away from him.

"Sir, give me this water, so that I will not be thirsty and will not have to come all the way here to draw."

There was a small pause. The light fell white on the stones. A breath of wind moved across the open ground and was gone.

Jesus said to her, "Go, call your husband, and come here."

The woman's eyes lowered.

"I have no husband."

Jesus said to her, "You have well said, 'I have no husband,' for you have had five husbands, and the one you now have is not your husband. What you have said is true."

The wind did not move. Her hand on the jar tightened, then loosened. She did not step back. She did not gather the jar and leave. She stood in the place she had stood, and she looked at the man on the rim, and her face changed.

"Sir, I perceive that you are a prophet."

Her voice was lower now. She turned her face toward the hills behind him, where Mount Gerizim rose above the plain.

"Our fathers worshiped on this mountain, and you Jews say that Jerusalem is the place where people ought to worship."

Jesus said to her, "Woman, believe me, the hour comes when neither on this mountain nor in Jerusalem will you worship the Father. You worship what you do not know. We worship what we know, for salvation is from the Jews. But the hour comes, and now is, when the true worshipers will worship the Father in spirit and truth, for the Father seeks such to worship him. God is spirit, and those who worship him must worship in spirit and truth."

The words settled into the noon. The woman did not answer at once. She looked at the well. She looked at him. The light on the stones was very bright.

"I know that Messiah comes, he who is called Christ. When he comes, he will declare to us all things."

Jesus said to her, "I who speak to you am he."

At that moment his disciples came back along the path. They had bread in cloth and a skin of food slung between them, and they slowed as they neared the well. They marveled that he was speaking with a woman. They did not break the silence. No one said, "What do you seek?" No one said, "Why are you speaking with her?" They stood at a small distance and waited.

The woman left her water jar.

She did not pick it up. She did not draw the water she had come for. She turned from the well and walked back along the path she had come, and her steps were quicker now, and she did not look back. The jar sat where she had set it, full of nothing, balanced on the worn stone.

She went away into the city. The first people she met were in the street near the gate, and her voice carried.

"Come, see a man who told me everything that I ever did. Can this be the Christ?"

Heads turned. A man stopped where he was. Another set down what he was carrying. The woman who had walked to the well alone at noon now spoke in the open street, and the people listened.

They went out of the city. They were coming to him. A line of men and women began along the path toward Jacob's well, the city behind them, the noon sun still high, the stones of the well still warm.

The water jar sat on the stone rim of the well.
