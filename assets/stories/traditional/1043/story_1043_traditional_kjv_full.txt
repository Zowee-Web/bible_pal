The Midianites were terrible unto the people of Israel. Year by year, they swarmed over the land like locusts — stealing grain, taking sheep and oxen and asses, trampling the crops flat. When they came, the Israelites fled. They hid themselves in dens and in caves and in strongholds. They were afraid of all things.

In the little town of Ophrah, a young man named Gideon was threshing wheat. But he was not threshing it upon a threshing floor out in the open, as one would commonly do. He was threshing by the winepress — a low stone pit hid behind walls. He was hiding. If the Midianites should see his wheat, they would take it.

Gideon beat the stalks against the stone. Dust floated in the warm air. Sweat ran down his face. He wrought with haste, glancing up every moment to make sure none was coming.

Then someone was there.

The angel of the Lord appeared, and sat under an oak which grew nearby. Its shade was cool and dark. Gideon perceived him not at first. Then the angel spake.

"The Lord is with thee, thou mighty man of valour."

Gideon stayed his hand. He beheld this stranger sitting under the tree. Mighty man of valour? Gideon was hiding in a winepress. He felt not mighty at all.

"Oh my lord," said Gideon, "if the Lord be with us, wherefore then is all this befallen us? And where be all his miracles which our fathers told us of, saying, 'Did not the Lord bring us up from Egypt?' But now the Lord hath forsaken us, and delivered us into the hand of the Midianites."

Gideon's voice had an edge unto it. He was sore vexed. He had heard the old tales of God doing wondrous things. But all he could see was his people hiding in caves.

The Lord turned and looked straight upon Gideon. "Go in this thy might, and thou shalt save Israel from the hand of the Midianites. Have not I sent thee?"

Gideon blinked. Save Israel? He? "Oh my Lord, wherewith shall I save Israel?" said he. "Behold, my family is poor in Manasseh, and I am the least in my father's house."

The youngest. The least family. The most unlikely man any would choose.

"Surely I will be with thee," said the Lord. His voice was calm and sure. "And thou shalt smite the Midianites as one man."

Gideon stood there with wheat dust upon his hands. He would fain believe it. But he had need to know that this was real.

"If now I have found grace in thy sight," said Gideon, "then shew me a sign that thou talkest with me. Depart not hence, I pray thee, until I come unto thee, and bring forth my present, and set it before thee."

The Lord said, "I will tarry until thou come again."

Gideon went in unto his house. He took a kid and slew it. He seethed the flesh. He took flour and made unleavened bread — flat cakes baked with haste over the fire. He put the flesh in a basket. He poured the broth into a pot. He bare it all forth, under the oak, and set it before the angel.

The angel of God said, "Take the flesh and the unleavened cakes, and lay them upon this rock. And pour out the broth."

Gideon did so. He laid the flesh and the unleavened cakes upon the great rock. He poured the warm broth thereon. Then he stepped back.

The angel of the Lord put forth the end of the staff that was in his hand — a long walking rod — and touched the tip thereof to the flesh and the unleavened cakes.

There rose up fire out of the rock. It came forth from within the stone itself, hot and bright. The flames consumed the flesh and the unleavened cakes in moments. Gideon felt the heat upon his face. The broth hissed and steamed. All was consumed.

When Gideon looked up, the angel of the Lord was departed out of his sight. The branches of the oak swayed in the breeze. The rock was scorched black. The food was gone.

Gideon's heart was sore amazed. "Alas, O Lord God!" he cried. "For because I have seen an angel of the Lord face to face!"

But the Lord spake unto him again — gently this time, like a father quieting a frightened child. "Peace be unto thee. Fear not. Thou shalt not die."

Gideon stood still. His breathing slowed. The fear drained away, and somewhat else took its place — a deep, quiet peace.

He built an altar there, upon that very spot, with the stones round about him. He stacked them with care and called the altar "The Lord is Peace." For that was what God had given him. Not only a charge, not only a sign. Peace.

The young man that had been hiding in a winepress, afraid of all things — God had called him a mighty warrior. And God meant it.
