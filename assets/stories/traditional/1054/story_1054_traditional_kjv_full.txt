And David said, "Is there yet any that is left of the house of Saul, that I may shew him kindness for Jonathan's sake?"

The question arose in a chamber where none expected it. David was king. Saul was dead. Jonathan was dead. The wars betwixt the two houses had ended. The throne stood sure. There was no cause of policy to seek out the remnant of a rival's blood. Kings sought not the heirs of those that went before them to shew them kindness. Kings sought them to put them away.

But David remembered Jonathan. The robe. The sword. The bow. The girdle. The covenant made in the court of Saul when they were both young and the days to come were yet unwritten.

Now there was of the house of Saul a servant whose name was Ziba. And when they had called him unto David, he entered the throne room and crossed the wide stone floor, his sandals scuffing upon the polished surface. The king said, "Art thou Ziba?" He said, "Thy servant is he."

And the king said, "Is there not yet any of the house of Saul, that I may shew the kindness of God unto him?"

The kindness of God. Not the mercy of a conqueror nor the favour of politics. The kindness of God — a thing altogether different.

And Ziba said, "Jonathan hath yet a son, which is lame on his feet."

The child had been five years of age when the tidings came from Jezreel — Saul and Jonathan fallen in battle, the Philistines at hand. His nurse had caught him up to flee. In her haste, running down the stone passages of the palace with the child pressed against her breast, she stumbled or the child slipped, and he fell. The sound of his body striking the floor. Both his feet were broken. He grew up lame, unable to walk without help, dragging himself along walls and leaning upon the arms of servants. His name was Mephibosheth.

David said, "Where is he?" "He is in the house of Machir, the son of Ammiel, in Lodebar."

Lodebar. A small place beyond the Jordan, far from Jerusalem, far from power, far from aught that mattered. The grandson of a king dwelling in obscurity in a place whose name signified no pasture. Hidden. Forgotten. Lame.

Then king David sent, and fetched him out of the house of Machir in Lodebar. The journey was long — days upon the road, the litter jolting over stony ground, the dust rising and settling upon all things. Mephibosheth came borne upon the shoulders of others — a grown man who could not go of his own strength, called by a king whom he had every reason to fear. When new houses rose to power, the remnant of the old house was slain. Such was the way of kingdoms. Mephibosheth would have known this well. Every mile of the journey toward Jerusalem would have been a mile nearer to what he expected was his death.

Now when Mephibosheth, the son of Jonathan, the son of Saul, was come unto David, he fell on his face and did reverence. His body lay flat upon the stone of the palace floor, his broken feet behind him. The cold of the stone pressed against his brow and his palms.

And David said, "Mephibosheth." And he answered, "Behold thy servant."

And David said unto him, "Fear not: for I will surely shew thee kindness for Jonathan thy father's sake, and will restore thee all the land of Saul thy father; and thou shalt eat bread at my table continually."

The words hung in the chamber. The guards stood still along the walls. Mephibosheth bowed low his head, his brow touching the stone once more.

And he bowed himself, and said, "What is thy servant, that thou shouldest look upon such a dead dog as I am?"

A dead dog. The meanest thing a man could name himself — worthless, unclean, beneath regard. And yet the king had sent for him, borne him across the country, set him in the throne room, and spoken those words.

Then the king called to Ziba, and said unto him, "I have given unto thy master's son all that pertained to Saul and to all his house. Thou therefore, and thy sons, and thy servants, shall till the land for him, and thou shalt bring in the fruits, that thy master's son may have food to eat." The fields and orchards and vineyards that Saul had once possessed — all of it restored.

But Mephibosheth — the lame grandson of a dead king, the son of David's beloved friend — he should eat bread at the king's table always. As one of the king's own sons.

So Mephibosheth dwelt in Jerusalem, and he did eat continually at the king's table, a man lame on both his feet, seated among the princes and the captains and the men of valour. His place was set at the long board where the lamplight caught the bronze vessels and the bread was broken fresh. His feet rested beneath the same table where the king's own sons sat down to eat, hidden by the cloth, and none beheld them.
