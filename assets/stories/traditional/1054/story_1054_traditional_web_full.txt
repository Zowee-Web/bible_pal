David said, "Is there still anyone left of the house of Saul, that I may show him kindness for Jonathan's sake?"

The throne room was quiet. David sat on the seat where Saul had once sat. The stone walls were hung with woven cloth. Guards stood along the edges of the room. The light came through the high windows and fell across the floor in long rectangles.

There was a servant of Saul's house named Ziba. They brought him before the king. He entered the throne room and crossed the wide stone floor, his sandals scuffing against the polished surface. David said, "Are you Ziba?" He said, "At your service."

The king said, "Is there not still someone of the house of Saul, that I may show the kindness of God to him?"

Ziba said, "There is still a son of Jonathan. He is lame in both feet."

The boy had been five years old when the news came from Jezreel — Saul and Jonathan fallen in battle, the Philistines advancing. His nurse had snatched him up and fled. In the panic and the hurry, running down the stone corridors of the palace with the child pressed to her chest, she stumbled or he slipped, and the child fell. The sound of his body striking the floor. Both his feet were injured. He grew up lame, unable to walk without support, dragging himself along walls and leaning on the arms of servants. His name was Mephibosheth.

David said, "Where is he?"

"In the house of Machir, the son of Ammiel, in Lo-debar."

Lo-debar was a small settlement east of the Jordan. Dry ground, low stone houses, goats on the hillside. Far from Jerusalem.

King David sent and brought Mephibosheth from Lo-debar. The journey was long — days on the road, the litter jolting over rocky ground, the dust rising and settling on everything. Mephibosheth came carried or supported, a grown man who could not walk on his own.

He came before David. He fell on his face and paid homage, his body flat against the stone floor of the palace, his damaged feet behind him. The cold of the stone pressed against his forehead and his palms.

David said, "Mephibosheth."

He said, "Behold, I am your servant."

David said to him, "Do not fear, for I will show you kindness for the sake of your father Jonathan, and I will restore to you all the land of Saul your grandfather, and you shall eat at my table always."

The words hung in the room. The guards stood still along the walls. Mephibosheth bowed his head, his forehead touching the stone again.

"What is your servant," he said, "that you should show regard for a dead dog such as I?"

David summoned Ziba. He told him that everything that had belonged to Saul and his house was now given to Mephibosheth. Ziba and his fifteen sons and twenty servants would till the land and bring in the harvest, and the produce would provide for Mephibosheth's household. The fields and orchards and vineyards that Saul had once owned — all of it restored.

But Mephibosheth himself would eat at the king's table. Like one of the king's own sons.

So Mephibosheth lived in Jerusalem. He ate at the king's table regularly, a man lame in both feet, seated among the princes and the officials and the warriors. His place was set at the long table where the lamplight caught the bronze cups and the bread was broken fresh. His feet rested beneath the same table where the king's own sons sat down to eat, hidden by the cloth, and no one saw them.
