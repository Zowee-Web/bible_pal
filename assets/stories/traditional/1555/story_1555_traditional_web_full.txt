Amos came up from Tekoa. He was a herdsman and a dresser of sycamore trees, and he had not been trained in the schools of the prophets. He had not been the son of a priest. He had been a man with a flock and a small grove south of Bethlehem, and the word of Yahweh had come to him in the field one afternoon, and he had carried it north.

He stood now at Bethel, in the days of Jeroboam the son of Joash, king of Israel. The kingdom was rich. It had not been this rich in a long time. The borders had been pushed out. The trade routes ran east and west across the high country. The altars were many. The festivals filled the calendar from new moon to new moon. There were silver vessels in the houses of the merchants and ivory in the beds of the wealthy and feasts that went on for days at the high places. The priests at Bethel were paid well, and the songs at Bethel were beautiful, and the people of Bethel believed they were standing in the favor of the Lord.

And in the alleys of the same city the poor were sold for a pair of sandals. And the gleanings were not left at the corners of the field. And the judges at the gate took the bribe. And the small farmer's land was taken when his harvest failed, and the woman whose husband died had no one to plead her cause. And the prophet who came up from Tekoa had walked through those alleys before he walked into the festival, and he had seen.

He lifted up his voice in the open court of the altar.

"Woe to you who desire the day of Yahweh," he said. "Why do you long for the day of Yahweh? It is darkness, and not light. It is as when a man flees from a lion and a bear meets him. Or as when he goes into the house and leans his hand on the wall, and a snake bites him.

"Shall not the day of Yahweh be darkness, and not light? Even very dark, with no brightness in it?"

Some in the crowd turned to listen. Most kept moving. The musicians on the steps of the altar did not stop playing at first. Then they did. The voice of the herdsman from Tekoa was carrying farther than the priests would have liked.

"I hate, I despise your feasts," he said, "and I cannot stand your solemn assemblies. Though you offer me your burnt offerings and your meal offerings, I will not accept them. Neither will I regard the peace offerings of your fat animals. Take away from me the noise of your songs. I will not listen to the music of your harps."

A few of the merchants in the courtyard began to draw away. The chief priest at Bethel sent a runner to find Amaziah, the king's man at the sanctuary.

"But let justice roll on like rivers," the prophet said, "and righteousness like a mighty stream."

The words struck the steps of the altar at Bethel and ran back out into the streets of the city. The river he was calling for was not running yet. He knew that. The crowd knew that. The poor in the alleys knew it best.

Amaziah came. The king's man at Bethel sent word south to the palace that the prophet from Tekoa was speaking against the king's house. He told Amos to go back to Judah, to eat his bread there, and to prophesy there, and not at Bethel — for Bethel was the king's sanctuary and the king's house.

Amos did not go home yet. He had not finished saying what he had been sent to say. He answered Amaziah, briefly and without raising his voice, that he had not been a prophet by trade. The Lord had taken him from following the flock. The Lord had said: go, prophesy to my people Israel.

And so it was in those days, in the city of Bethel, that the herdsman from Tekoa stood in the place of the king's altar and called for the river of justice, and the noise of the festival went on around him, and the river had not yet begun to run.
