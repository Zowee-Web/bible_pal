Amos came up from Tekoa. He was a herdman and a gatherer of sycamore fruit, and he had not been trained in the schools of the prophets. He had not been the son of a priest. He had been a man with a flock and a small grove south of Bethlehem, and the word of the Lord had come unto him in the field one afternoon, and he had carried it north.

He stood now at Bethel, in the days of Jeroboam the son of Joash, king of Israel. The kingdom was rich. It had not been so rich in a long time. The borders had been pushed out. The trade routes ran east and west across the high country. The altars were many. The feast days filled the calendar from new moon to new moon. There were silver vessels in the houses of the merchants and ivory in the beds of the wealthy and feasts that went on for many days at the high places. The priests at Bethel were paid well, and the songs at Bethel were beautiful, and the people of Bethel believed they stood in the favour of the Lord.

And in the streets of the same city the poor were sold for a pair of shoes. And the gleanings were not left at the corners of the field. And the judges at the gate took the bribe. And the small farmer's land was taken when his harvest failed, and the woman whose husband had died had no one to plead her cause. And the prophet who came up from Tekoa had walked through those streets before he walked into the feast, and he had seen.

He lifted up his voice in the open court of the altar.

"Woe unto you that desire the day of the Lord," he said. "To what end is it for you? The day of the Lord is darkness, and not light. As if a man did flee from a lion, and a bear met him. Or went into the house, and leaned his hand on the wall, and a serpent bit him.

"Shall not the day of the Lord be darkness, and not light? Even very dark, and no brightness in it?"

Some in the crowd turned to listen. Most kept moving. The musicians on the steps of the altar did not stop playing at first. Then they did. The voice of the herdman from Tekoa was carrying farther than the priests would have desired.

"I hate, I despise your feast days," he said, "and I will not smell in your solemn assemblies. Though ye offer me burnt offerings and your meat offerings, I will not accept them. Neither will I regard the peace offerings of your fat beasts. Take thou away from me the noise of thy songs. I will not hear the melody of thy viols."

A few of the merchants in the courtyard began to draw away. The chief priest at Bethel sent a runner to find Amaziah, the king's man at the sanctuary.

"But let judgment run down as waters," the prophet said, "and righteousness as a mighty stream."

The words struck the steps of the altar at Bethel and ran back out into the streets of the city. The river he was calling for was not running yet. He knew that. The crowd knew that. The poor in the streets knew it best.

Amaziah came. The king's man at Bethel sent word south to the palace that the prophet from Tekoa was speaking against the king's house. He told Amos to go back to Judah, to eat his bread there, and to prophesy there, and not at Bethel — for Bethel was the king's chapel and the king's court.

Amos went not home yet. He had not finished saying what he had been sent to say. He answered Amaziah, briefly and without raising his voice, that he had not been a prophet by trade. The Lord had taken him as he followed the flock. The Lord had said: go, prophesy unto my people Israel.

And so it was in those days, in the city of Bethel, that the herdman from Tekoa stood in the place of the king's altar and called for the river of judgment, and the noise of the feast went on around him, and the river had not yet begun to run.
