Amos came up from Tekoa. He was a herdman and a gatherer of sycamore fruit, and he had not been trained in the schools of the prophets. He had not been the son of a priest. He had been a man with a flock and a small grove south of Bethlehem, walking the dry country between the salt sea and the wilderness of Judah, and the word of the Lord had come unto him in the field one afternoon, and he had carried it north.

The road from Tekoa unto Bethel is not long. He had walked it in a few days, sleeping under the stars, eating bread from the bag he carried. He had passed through the cities upon the way. He had seen the merchants in the gates of Samaria. He had heard the songs in the high places. He had stood in the streets of the towns where the poor sat with their faces unto the wall, and he had counted the houses of the rich.

He stood now at Bethel, in the days of Jeroboam the son of Joash, king of Israel. The kingdom was rich. It had not been so rich in a long time. The borders had been pushed out, north into Hamath and south past the brook of the wilderness. The trade routes ran east and west across the high country. The altars were many. The feast days filled the calendar from new moon to new moon, and the priests at Bethel were paid well, and the songs at Bethel were beautiful.

There were silver vessels in the houses of the merchants and ivory in the beds of the wealthy. There were summer houses and winter houses. There were feasts that went on for many days at the high places. There were songs unto David sung upon stringed instruments. The people of Bethel believed they stood in the favour of the Lord, and they brought their offerings, and they did eat the flesh of the offerings, and they laughed in the courtyards of the sanctuary, and they slept well.

And in the streets of the same city the poor were sold for a pair of shoes.

The gleanings were not left at the corners of the field. The judges at the gate took the bribe. The small farmer's land was taken when his harvest failed. The woman whose husband had died had no one to plead her cause. The prophet who came up from Tekoa had walked through those streets before he walked into the feast, and he had seen all of it. He had not come north to give a speech. He had come north because he could not abide where he was.

He lifted up his voice in the open court of the altar at midday. The pilgrims were thick. The music was loud. He needed not to ask for audience. The Lord had given him the voice he required.

"Woe unto you that desire the day of the Lord," he said. "To what end is it for you? The day of the Lord is darkness, and not light. As if a man did flee from a lion, and a bear met him. Or went into the house, and leaned his hand on the wall, and a serpent bit him.

"Shall not the day of the Lord be darkness, and not light? Even very dark, and no brightness in it?"

The musicians upon the steps of the altar did not cease playing at first. Then they did, one by one, beginning with the harpist who had heard prophets before. Some in the crowd turned to listen. Most kept moving. The chief priest at Bethel sent a runner to find Amaziah, the king's man at the sanctuary.

The prophet slowed not.

"I hate, I despise your feast days," he said, "and I will not smell in your solemn assemblies. Though ye offer me burnt offerings and your meat offerings, I will not accept them. Neither will I regard the peace offerings of your fat beasts."

He looked across the court at the choir.

"Take thou away from me the noise of thy songs. I will not hear the melody of thy viols."

A few of the merchants in the courtyard began to draw away. The pilgrims who had come for the feast looked at one another. This was not how the feast was meant to go. The herdman from Tekoa was speaking against the king, and against the priests, and against the music, and against the food.

He had not yet come unto the line he had been carrying since Tekoa.

"But let judgment run down as waters," he said, "and righteousness as a mighty stream."

The words struck the steps of the altar at Bethel and ran back out into the streets of the city. The river he was calling for was not running yet. He knew that. The crowd knew that. The poor in the streets knew it best.

Amaziah came. The king's man at Bethel had a soft voice and a careful manner. He told Amos he had heard enough. He sent word south unto the palace of the king that the prophet from Tekoa was speaking against the king's house. He bade Amos go back to Judah, to eat his bread there, and to prophesy there, and not at Bethel — for Bethel was the king's chapel and the king's court.

Amos went not home yet. He had not finished saying what he had been sent to say. He answered Amaziah, briefly and without raising his voice. He had not been a prophet by trade. He had not been the son of a prophet. He had been a herdman, and a gatherer of sycamore fruit. The Lord had taken him as he followed the flock. The Lord had said unto him: go, prophesy unto my people Israel.

He said that, and he said a few more things, and then he turned and walked out of the courtyard of the altar, and the feast closed up behind him as a body of water closeth up behind a stone that hath fallen into it.

And so it was in those days, in the city of Bethel, that the herdman from Tekoa stood in the place of the king's altar and called for the river of judgment, and the noise of the feast went on around him, and the river had not yet begun to run.
