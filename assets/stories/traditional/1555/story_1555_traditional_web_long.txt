Amos came up from Tekoa. He was a herdsman and a dresser of sycamore trees, and he had not been trained in the schools of the prophets. He had not been the son of a priest. He had been a man with a flock and a small grove south of Bethlehem, walking the dry country between the Dead Sea and the Judean wilderness, and the word of Yahweh had come to him in the field one afternoon, and he had carried it north.

The road from Tekoa to Bethel is not long. He had walked it in a few days, sleeping under the stars, eating bread from the bag he carried. He had passed through the cities on the way. He had seen the merchants in the gates of Samaria. He had heard the songs in the high places. He had stood in the alleys of the towns where the poor sat with their faces to the wall, and he had counted the houses of the rich.

He stood now at Bethel, in the days of Jeroboam the son of Joash, king of Israel. The kingdom was rich. It had not been this rich in a long time. The borders had been pushed out, north into Hamath and south past the Brook of the Arabah. The trade routes ran east and west across the high country. The altars were many. The festivals filled the calendar from new moon to new moon, and the priests at Bethel were paid well, and the songs at Bethel were beautiful.

There were silver vessels in the houses of the merchants and ivory in the beds of the wealthy. There were summer houses and winter houses. There were feasts that went on for days at the high places. There were songs to David sung on stringed instruments. The people of Bethel believed they were standing in the favor of the Lord, and they brought their offerings, and they ate the meat of the offerings, and they laughed in the courtyards of the sanctuary, and they slept well.

And in the alleys of the same city the poor were sold for a pair of sandals.

The gleanings were not left at the corners of the field. The judges at the gate took the bribe. The small farmer's land was taken when his harvest failed. The woman whose husband died had no one to plead her cause. The prophet who came up from Tekoa had walked through those alleys before he walked into the festival, and he had seen all of it. He had not come north to give a speech. He had come north because he could not stay where he was.

He lifted up his voice in the open court of the altar at midday. The pilgrims were thick. The music was loud. He did not need to ask for attention. The Lord had given him the voice he needed.

"Woe to you who desire the day of Yahweh," he said. "Why do you long for the day of Yahweh? It is darkness, and not light. It is as when a man flees from a lion and a bear meets him. Or as when he goes into the house and leans his hand on the wall, and a snake bites him.

"Shall not the day of Yahweh be darkness, and not light? Even very dark, with no brightness in it?"

The musicians on the steps of the altar did not stop playing at first. Then they did, one by one, beginning with the harpist who had heard prophets before. Some in the crowd turned to listen. Most kept moving. The chief priest at Bethel sent a runner to find Amaziah, the king's man at the sanctuary.

The prophet did not slow down.

"I hate, I despise your feasts," he said, "and I cannot stand your solemn assemblies. Though you offer me your burnt offerings and your meal offerings, I will not accept them. Neither will I regard the peace offerings of your fat animals."

He looked across the court at the choir.

"Take away from me the noise of your songs. I will not listen to the music of your harps."

A few of the merchants in the courtyard began to draw away. The pilgrims who had come for the festival looked at one another. This was not how the festival was supposed to go. The herdsman from Tekoa was speaking against the king, and against the priests, and against the music, and against the food.

He had not yet come to the line he had been carrying since Tekoa.

"But let justice roll on like rivers," he said, "and righteousness like a mighty stream."

The words struck the steps of the altar at Bethel and ran back out into the streets of the city. The river he was calling for was not running yet. He knew that. The crowd knew that. The poor in the alleys knew it best.

Amaziah came. The king's man at Bethel had a soft voice and a careful manner. He told Amos that he had heard enough. He sent word south to the palace of the king that the prophet from Tekoa was speaking against the king's house. He told Amos to go back to Judah, to eat his bread there, and to prophesy there, and not at Bethel — for Bethel was the king's sanctuary and the king's house.

Amos did not go home yet. He had not finished saying what he had been sent to say. He answered Amaziah briefly and without raising his voice. He had not been a prophet by trade. He had not been the son of a prophet. He had been a herdsman, and a dresser of sycamore trees. The Lord had taken him from following the flock. The Lord had said: go, prophesy to my people Israel.

He said that, and he said a few more things, and then he turned and walked out of the courtyard of the altar, and the festival closed up behind him as a body of water closes up behind a stone that has fallen into it.

And so it was in those days, in the city of Bethel, that the herdsman from Tekoa stood in the place of the king's altar and called for the river of justice, and the noise of the festival went on around him, and the river had not yet begun to run.
