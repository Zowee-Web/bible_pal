Jacob lived in the land of Canaan with his twelve sons. He had a big family, but one son was his favorite — Joseph. Joseph was seventeen years old and helped tend the flocks with his brothers.

Jacob loved Joseph more than all his other children, because Joseph had been born when Jacob was already old. To show his love, Jacob made Joseph a special gift — a long coat with many colors. The fabric was soft and the colors were bright — reds and blues and purples. None of the other brothers had anything like it.

When the brothers saw the coat, jealousy burned in their chests. They could not speak a kind word to Joseph. Every time he walked past in that bright robe, their faces went hard.

Then Joseph made things worse. "Listen to this dream I had," he said one morning. "We were tying up bundles of grain out in the field. My bundle stood up straight. Then all your bundles gathered around mine and bowed down to it."

The brothers glared at him. "Are you going to reign over us?" they said. "Are you going to rule over us?" They hated him even more.

Joseph had another dream. "The sun and the moon and eleven stars were all bowing down to me," he said.

Even Jacob frowned at that. "What is this dream?" he said. "Shall I and your mother and your brothers actually come and bow down to you on the ground?" But privately, Jacob kept thinking about it. He turned it over in his mind.

Some time later, the brothers took the flocks far away to graze near Shechem. Jacob sent Joseph to check on them. "Go and see whether it is well with your brothers," he said. Joseph put on his colorful coat and set out alone.

He walked a long time. The land was hilly and brown, the paths dusty. When he got to Shechem, his brothers were not there. He wandered in the open field until a man found him. "What are you looking for?"

"My brothers," Joseph said. "Do you know where they are?"

"They went to Dothan," the man said.

So Joseph kept walking. He walked until he could see Dothan in the distance.

His brothers saw him coming from far away. The colorful coat was easy to spot against the brown hillside. "Here comes the dreamer," one of them said. "Come, let us kill him and throw him into a pit. We can say a wild animal ate him. Then we will see what becomes of his dreams."

But Reuben, the oldest brother, said, "Do not shed blood. Do not kill him. Just throw him into the pit out here in the wilderness." Reuben said this because he planned to come back later and rescue Joseph.

When Joseph arrived, smiling, glad to see them, they grabbed him. They ripped off his colorful coat. They dragged him to an empty pit — a deep hole in the rocky ground with no water at the bottom — and threw him in. Joseph landed hard. He looked up at a small circle of blue sky far above him.

The brothers sat down to eat lunch. They ate calmly, as if nothing had happened.

While they were eating, they saw a caravan coming along the road. Camels plodded in a long line, loaded with spices and sweet-smelling myrrh. The traders were Ishmaelites, heading to Egypt.

Judah looked at the caravan. Then he looked at his brothers. "What do we gain if we kill our brother and hide what we did?" he said. "Let us sell him to the Ishmaelites. He is our brother, after all." The brothers agreed. They pulled Joseph out of the pit and sold him to the traders for twenty pieces of silver. The camels started moving south. Joseph went with them.

Joseph looked back at his brothers standing on the hill. They did not wave.

When Reuben returned to the pit later, Joseph was gone. He tore his clothes. "The boy is gone!" he cried. "What am I going to do?"

The brothers took Joseph's coat, killed a goat, and dipped the coat in the blood. The bright colors turned dark and sticky. They brought the bloody coat to their father. "We found this," they said. "Is it your son's coat?"

Jacob recognized it immediately. His face crumpled. "It is my son's coat," he whispered. "A wild animal has devoured him. Joseph is surely torn to pieces!" Jacob tore his own clothes and put on rough sackcloth. He wept for many days. All his children came to comfort him, but no one could. "I will go to my grave mourning for my son," he said.

Far away, the caravan reached Egypt. The traders sold Joseph to a man named Potiphar, an important officer who worked for Pharaoh. Joseph stood in a strange land with no coat, no family, no home. But God was with him, even there.
