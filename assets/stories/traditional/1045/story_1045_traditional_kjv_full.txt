Jacob dwelt in the land of Canaan with his twelve sons. He had a great house, but one son was his beloved — Joseph. Joseph was seventeen years old, and helped to feed the flock with his brethren.

Jacob loved Joseph more than all his children, because he was the son of his old age. To shew his love, Jacob made Joseph a special gift — a long coat of many colours. The cloth was soft and the colours were bright — reds and blues and purples. None of the other brethren had any such thing.

When the brethren saw the coat, envy burned within their breasts. They could not speak peaceably unto Joseph. Every time he walked past in that bright robe, their faces hardened.

Then Joseph made it worse. "Hear, I pray you, this dream which I have dreamed," he said one morning. "We were binding sheaves in the field. Lo, my sheaf arose, and also stood upright. Then your sheaves stood round about, and made obeisance to my sheaf."

The brethren glared upon him. "Shalt thou indeed reign over us?" said they. "Shalt thou indeed have dominion over us?" They hated him yet the more.

Joseph dreamed yet another dream. "Behold, the sun and the moon and the eleven stars made obeisance to me," said he.

Even Jacob frowned thereat. "What is this dream that thou hast dreamed?" said he. "Shall I and thy mother and thy brethren indeed come to bow down ourselves to thee to the earth?" But privately, Jacob kept the saying in mind. He turned it over in his thoughts.

After certain days, the brethren took the flocks far away to feed near unto Shechem. Jacob sent Joseph to see them. "Go, I pray thee, see whether it be well with thy brethren," said he. Joseph put on his coat of many colours and went forth alone.

He walked a long while. The land was hilly and brown, the paths dusty. When he came unto Shechem, his brethren were not there. He wandered in the field until a man found him. "What seekest thou?"

"I seek my brethren," said Joseph. "Tell me, I pray thee, where they feed their flocks?"

"They are departed hence to Dothan," said the man.

So Joseph went forward. He walked till he could behold Dothan in the distance.

His brethren saw him afar off. The coat of many colours was easy to spy against the brown hillside. "Behold, this dreamer cometh," said one of them. "Come now therefore, and let us slay him, and cast him into some pit, and we will say, Some evil beast hath devoured him. And we shall see what will become of his dreams."

But Reuben, the firstborn, said, "Shed no blood, but cast him into this pit that is in the wilderness, and lay no hand upon him." Reuben said this that he might deliver him out of their hands, to bring him to his father again.

When Joseph was come, smiling, glad to see them, they laid hold on him. They stript him out of his coat of many colours. They dragged him unto an empty pit — a deep hole in the rocky ground wherein was no water — and cast him in. Joseph fell hard. He looked up at a small circle of blue sky far above him.

The brethren sat down to eat bread. They did eat calmly, as though nothing were come to pass.

While they did eat, they lifted up their eyes and beheld a company of Ishmeelites coming along the way. The camels plodded in a long line, bearing spicery and balm and myrrh. The merchantmen were journeying down to Egypt.

Judah looked upon the caravan. Then he looked upon his brethren. "What profit is it if we slay our brother, and conceal his blood?" said he. "Come, and let us sell him to the Ishmeelites. He is our brother and our flesh." The brethren were content. They drew Joseph up out of the pit and sold him unto the merchantmen for twenty pieces of silver. The camels began to move southward. Joseph went with them.

Joseph looked back upon his brethren standing on the hill. They waved not.

When Reuben returned unto the pit, behold, Joseph was not in the pit. He rent his clothes. "The child is not!" he cried. "And I, whither shall I go?"

The brethren took Joseph's coat, and killed a kid of the goats, and dipped the coat in the blood. The bright colours turned dark and clotted. They brought the bloody coat unto their father. "This have we found," said they. "Know now whether it be thy son's coat or no."

Jacob knew it straightway. His face fell. "It is my son's coat," he whispered. "An evil beast hath devoured him. Joseph is without doubt rent in pieces!" Jacob rent his own clothes, and put sackcloth upon his loins. He mourned for his son many days. All his children rose up to comfort him, but he refused to be comforted. "I will go down into the grave unto my son mourning," said he.

Far off, the caravan came down into Egypt. The merchantmen sold Joseph unto a man named Potiphar, an officer of Pharaoh. Joseph stood in a strange land with no coat, no kindred, no home. But the Lord was with him, even there.
