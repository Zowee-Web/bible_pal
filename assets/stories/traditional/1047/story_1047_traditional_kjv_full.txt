Elijah the prophet stood before King Ahab. Ahab was a wicked king who worshipped false gods. Elijah feared him not.

"As the Lord, the God of Israel, liveth," Elijah said, "there shall not be dew nor rain these years, but according to my word." Then he turned and went his way.

The Lord spake unto Elijah. "Get thee hence, and turn thee eastward, and hide thyself by the brook Cherith, which is before Jordan. Thou shalt drink of the brook, and I have commanded the ravens to feed thee there."

Elijah did even as the Lord had commanded. He found the brook — a small, clear stream that tumbled over smooth stones. Tall reeds grew along the brink. The water was cool and clean. Elijah bowed down and drank.

That evening, he heard the sound of wings. Great black ravens swooped down out of the sky. In their beaks they bare bread and flesh. They let the food fall before him. Elijah took it up and did eat.

Every morning, the ravens came. Every evening, they came again. Black feathers glistening, sharp beaks bearing the food the Lord had prepared. It was enough.

But no rain fell. The heavens stayed cloudless. The sun scorched the land. Slowly, the brook grew small. The water that had tumbled and splashed now scarce trickled. Elijah beheld it shrink, day by day, until on a morning there was nothing left. The brook was dried up.

The Lord spake again. "Arise, get thee to Zarephath. I have commanded a widow woman there to sustain thee."

Zarephath was far off. Elijah arose and set forth. The land was dry and brown. Dust rose with every step. His throat was parched and his limbs were heavy.

When he came unto the gate of the city, he beheld a woman. She was thin — too thin. Her raiment was worn. She was bowed down, gathering of sticks from the ground. She moved slowly, as one in whom there was little strength left.

"Fetch me, I pray thee, a little water in a vessel, that I may drink," Elijah called unto her.

She nodded and turned to go. Then Elijah called again. "Bring me, I pray thee, a morsel of bread."

The woman stayed her steps. Her shoulders fell. She turned again about. "As the Lord thy God liveth," she said, and her voice was thin and weary, "I have not a cake. I have but an handful of meal in a barrel and a little oil in a cruse. Behold, I am gathering two sticks, that I may go in and dress it for me and my son, that we may eat it, and die."

One last meal. Then nothing. She had given up.

Elijah looked upon this weary, hungry woman. "Fear not," he said. "Go and do as thou hast said. But make me thereof a little cake first, and bring it unto me. Afterward make for thee and for thy son. For thus saith the Lord, the God of Israel: the barrel of meal shall not waste, neither shall the cruse of oil fail, until the day that the Lord sendeth rain upon the earth."

The woman stood there with her handful of sticks. A stranger asked of her the last morsel of her food. Her son tarried at home, hungry.

But somewhat in the words of Elijah made her believe.

She went home. She kindled a fire. She scraped the meal from the barrel and poured the last drops of oil. She mingled and patted and baked a little cake upon the hot stones. She brought it unto Elijah.

Then she made bread for herself and her son. They sat together and did eat.

The next morning, she went unto the barrel. She reached in, expecting nothing. Her fingers touched meal. She scooped it out — a full handful, even as before. She took up the cruse of oil. Oil poured forth, golden and smooth.

She marvelled. They were not empty.

Day after day, the same thing came to pass. She reached into the barrel and there was meal. She took up the cruse and there was oil. Never a feast — only enough. Always enough.

The barrel of meal wasted not. The cruse of oil failed not. It came to pass according to the word of the Lord, which he spake by Elijah.

Without, the drought went on. But within one small house in Zarephath, a widow, her son, and a weary prophet ate bread every day. The Lord had provided — through a quiet barrel that never emptied and a small cruse that never ran dry.
