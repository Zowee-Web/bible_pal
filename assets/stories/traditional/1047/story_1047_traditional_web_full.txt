Elijah the prophet stood before King Ahab. Ahab was a wicked king who worshipped false gods. Elijah was not afraid of him.

"As the Lord, the God of Israel, lives," Elijah said, "there shall not be dew nor rain these years, but according to my word." Then he turned and walked out.

God spoke to Elijah. "Turn east and hide yourself by the brook Cherith, near the Jordan. You shall drink from the brook. I have commanded the ravens to feed you there."

Elijah did what God told him. He found the brook — a small, clear stream that tumbled over smooth stones. Tall reeds grew along the edges. The water was cool and clean. Elijah knelt down and drank.

That evening, he heard wings. Big black ravens swooped down from the sky. In their beaks they carried bread and meat. They dropped the food right in front of Elijah. He picked it up and ate.

Every morning, the ravens came. Every evening, they came again. Black feathers glistening, sharp beaks carrying food God had prepared. It was enough.

But no rain fell. The sky stayed cloudless. The sun baked the land. Slowly, the brook got smaller. The water that had tumbled and splashed now barely trickled. Elijah watched it shrink, day by day, until one morning there was nothing left. The brook had dried up.

God spoke again. "Arise, go to Zarephath. I have commanded a widow there to sustain you."

Zarephath was far away. Elijah got up and started walking. The land was dry and brown. Dust rose with every step. His throat was parched and his legs were heavy.

When he came to the gate of the city, he saw a woman. She was thin — too thin. Her clothes were worn. She was stooped over, picking up sticks from the ground. She moved slowly, like someone who did not have much strength left.

"Please, bring me a little water in a cup," Elijah called to her.

She nodded and turned to go. Then Elijah called again. "Please bring me a piece of bread."

The woman stopped. Her shoulders dropped. She turned back around. "As the Lord your God lives," she said, and her voice was thin and tired, "I do not have a cake. I only have a handful of meal in a jar and a little oil in a jug. I am gathering sticks so I can bake one last meal for me and my son. After we eat it, we will die."

One last meal. Then nothing. She had given up.

Elijah looked at this tired, hungry woman. "Do not be afraid," he said. "Go and do as you have said. But first, make me a small cake and bring it to me. Afterward, make some for yourself and your son. For the Lord, the God of Israel, says: the jar of meal shall not run out, and the jug of oil shall not fail, until the day the Lord sends rain on the earth."

The woman stood there with her handful of sticks. A stranger was asking for her last bite of food. Her son was waiting at home, hungry.

But something in Elijah's words made her believe.

She went home. She built a fire. She scraped the flour from the jar and poured the last drops of oil. She mixed and patted and baked a small cake on the hot stones. She brought it to Elijah.

Then she made food for herself and her son. They sat together and ate.

The next morning, she went to the jar. She reached in, expecting nothing. Her fingers touched flour. She scooped it out — a full handful, just like before. She tilted the jug of oil. Oil poured out, golden and smooth.

She blinked. They were not empty.

Day after day, the same thing happened. She reached into the jar and there was flour. She tipped the jug and there was oil. Never a feast — just enough. Always enough.

The jar of meal was not used up. The jug of oil did not fail. It happened according to the word of the Lord, which he spoke by Elijah.

Outside, the drought went on. But in one small house in Zarephath, a widow, her son, and a tired prophet ate bread every day. God had provided — through a quiet jar that never emptied and a small jug that never ran dry.
