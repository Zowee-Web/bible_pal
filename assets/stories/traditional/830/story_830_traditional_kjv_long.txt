A certain man had two sons. He dwelt upon his estate, compassed about with fields and servants, flocks and a household full of daily labours. The younger of his sons, restless and driven by a longing for far places, came unto him one day. The son's eyes were resolved. Dust clave unto his sandals, and the early sun lit his countenance with hope and impatience.

He looked up unto his father and said, "Father, give me the portion of goods that falleth to me." The father stood silent for a moment, his face quiet but open. Then, without rebuke or delay, he divided unto them his living. The rich land, the cattle, and the gathered wealth — all counted out, measured, and given.

Not many days after, the younger son gathered all together. The morning he departed was clear and bright, the sky stretching above him as he set forth upon the road. He left the world of his father's house behind — the ring of laughter from the servants, the savour of bread from the kitchen, the comforting weight of home. Before him lay the unknown, and his steps quickened with eagerness.

He took his journey far from familiar fields, away from the well-trodden paths of his childhood. In a far country, restless energy moved within him. Liberty felt like gold within his grasp, and he spent his substance without restraint. Each day brought new feasts, new faces, and the lure of pleasure. The coins scattered swiftly — poured out in living that knew no morrow. Wine flowed, musick played, and laughter echoed through crowded halls.

But his treasures dwindled faster than he had supposed. One by one, his friends slipped away; the feasts grew quieter; the food waned. As his riches vanished, there arose a mighty famine in that land. The air grew heavy and still. Hunger crept through the streets and fields. The people grew desperate, their faces drawn and troubled.

He felt the ache in his own belly, his raiment hanging upon thin shoulders. The savour of roasted meat, once familiar, became but a memory. To live, he joined himself to a citizen of that country — one who owned fields without the city. The man sent him into his fields to feed swine.

The work was harsh and humbling. The field was lonely, hot, and foul with the stench of swine. The boy's feet sank in the miry ground, and the snorts of the beasts compassed him about. His fine robes gave way to rags, stained and rent. He carried pails of husks, longing only to fill his aching belly.

He looked upon the husks that the swine did eat. Hunger gnawed at him, stronger every day. The swine chewed, content. No man gave unto him meat. No man remembered him. In the far country, laughter and welcome had faded. Dust clave unto his skin, sweat ran down into his eyes, and the sun burned overhead, relentless and uncaring.

One day, as he squinted against the glare, he stopped. The wind rustled the dry grass, and flies buzzed about the trough. The stench was overwhelming. He pressed a hand to his hollow belly and remembered home — the lamplight falling softly upon bread, servants bustling, his father's table overflowing.

He came to himself. His words formed quietly at first, then clearer: "How many hired servants of my father's have bread enough and to spare, and I perish with hunger! I will arise and go to my father, and will say unto him, 'Father, I have sinned against heaven, and before thee, And am no more worthy to be called thy son: make me as one of thy hired servants.'"

Resolve took root within him. His mind was troubled, yet hope flickered like a lamp in darkness. He arose. His raiment hung loose and filthy. The empty fields fell away behind him as he set forth down the long, dusty road toward home.

His steps faltered at times, weighed down with shame and fear. The road seemed without end. Every bend filled him with anxious dread — Would his father turn him away? Would the doors be shut, the lamps gone out? Yet the thought of bread, shelter, and even a servant's welcome urged him onward.

At the family estate, the father went about his daily labour: surveying the land, walking among the olive trees, glancing oft down the road that led beyond the fields. His heart bare a silent longing for the son who had gone away. Every day, his eyes searched the distance.

On this day, as the sun pressed low against the horizon, something stirred. A faint figure walked in the distance — tattered, hesitant, leaning against the wandering wind. The father's heart leapt. He knew the walk, the shape. When he was yet a great way off, his father saw him, and had compassion, and ran. His feet smote the earth. Dust rose in his wake. Through the gate, across the yard, out unto the road — he ran, with arms open.

Before the son could speak a word, the father fell on his neck, and kissed him. The son's face was streaked with dust and tears, his hands trembling. He began his confession, his voice hoarse: "Father, I have sinned against heaven, and in thy sight, and am no more worthy to be called thy son."

The father would not let him finish. Joy overwhelmed sorrow. He called unto his servants, his voice raised with urgent gladness: "Bring forth the best robe, and put it on him; and put a ring on his hand, and shoes on his feet! Bring hither the fatted calf, and kill it; and let us eat, and be merry — for this my son was dead, and is alive again; he was lost, and is found!"

Straightway, the household stirred. Servants ran to obey. The best robe, soft and clean, was wrapped about the trembling son's shoulders. A ring glittered upon his hand, shoes eased onto cracked and weary feet.

Within, the musick rose. Laughter rang out. Dishes clattered, the savour of roasted calf filled the air. The long table was laid with fruit, bread, wine, and every good thing. The house pulsed with musick and dancing, the sound borne out beyond the walls into the twilight.

While the merriment echoed within, the elder son returned from the field. His raiment was dusted with the labour of the day. He came near unto the house, and heard musick and dancing. Wondering, and somewhat puzzled, he called one of the servants and asked what these things meant.

The servant answered: "Thy brother is come; and thy father hath killed the fatted calf, because he hath received him safe and sound."

A shadow crossed the elder son's face. Anger flared in his breast. He would not go in. His feet stood rooted without while merriment spilled through every window.

His father therefore came out, and intreated him, patient and gentle. The father's eyes pleaded with him to join the joy within. But the elder son answered, pain sharp in his words: "Lo, these many years do I serve thee, neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment: and yet thou never gavest me a kid, that I might make merry with my friends: But as soon as this thy son was come, which hath devoured thy living with harlots, thou hast killed for him the fatted calf."

The father hearkened, his love unwavering. He said unto him, "Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine. It was meet that we should make merry, and be glad: for this thy brother was dead, and is alive again; and was lost, and is found."
