A certain man had two sons. Their home was full of light and work. Fields stretched wide, with vines heavy in the season. Servants moved about. In this house, the younger son grew restless. One day, he spoke to his father. “Father, give me my share of the inheritance,” he said.

The father divided his property between his two sons. Soon after, the younger son gathered everything he had. He set out on a journey to a distant country. Behind him, the familiar fields faded. Ahead, the world beckoned with promise.

In the far country, the young man lived recklessly. He wasted his wealth in wild living. Coins vanished in laughter. Feasts and parties filled his nights. Friends gathered around him when the purse was full. He did not notice his fortune dwindling—until it was gone.

A severe famine struck the land. The fields outside the city dried up and yielded little. Hunger came quickly. His friends disappeared. The younger son had nothing left, not even a coin to buy bread. Desperate, he sought work from a man of that country.

The man sent him into the fields to feed pigs. Morning and evening, the young man trudged through mud and sour air. He was so hungry that even the pods the pigs ate began to look good. No one gave him anything.

His clothes hung in tatters. His skin was smeared with grime. The stench of pigs clung to him. He remembered the warmth of home—the food, the laughter, the songs at mealtimes. Even his father's hired servants had enough bread, and more to spare.

He came to himself. He said, “How many of my father’s hired servants have bread enough to spare, and I perish here with hunger! I will arise and go to my father. I will say to him, ‘Father, I have sinned against heaven and in your sight. I am no more worthy to be called your son. Make me as one of your hired servants.’”

He stood, hope flickering in his chest. Dust clung to his feet as he began the long walk home. Every step echoed uncertainty. Yet, as he came near, the old paths became familiar.

While he was still a long way off, his father saw him. The father’s heart leaped. Without hesitation, he ran out across the field—his robe billowing, feet pounding the earth. Arms wide, he reached his son and drew him close in a tight embrace. Tears and dust mingled.

The son, trembling, began his prepared words: “Father, I have sinned against heaven and in your sight. I am no more worthy to be called your son.”

But before he could finish, the father turned to his servants. “Bring out the best robe and put it on him. Put a ring on his hand, and sandals on his feet. Bring the fattened calf. Kill it, and let us eat and celebrate! For this, my son, was dead and is alive again. He was lost and is found.”

The servants hurried. Soon, laughter filled the house. The aroma of roasted meat drifted into the night. Garlands and music transformed the rooms. The younger son stood amid it all—robed, clean, the ring shining on his hand. Joy and astonishment mingled in his eyes.

Meanwhile, the older son was in the field. As he came near the house, he heard music and dancing. He called one of the servants and asked what these things meant. The servant said, “Your brother has come, and your father has killed the fattened calf because he has received him back safe and healthy.”

The older son grew angry. He would not go in. His father came out and pleaded with him.

The son answered, “Look, these many years I have served you, and I never disobeyed a commandment of yours, but you never gave me a young goat that I might celebrate with my friends. But when this, your son, came—who has devoured your living with prostitutes—you killed the fattened calf for him.”

The father said to him, “Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours. But it was appropriate to celebrate and be glad, for this, your brother, was dead, and is alive again. He was lost, and is found.”
