A certain man had two sons. Their home was full of light and of labour. Fields stretched wide, with vines heavy in the season. Servants moved about. In this house, the younger son grew restless. One day, he spake unto his father. "Father, give me the portion of goods that falleth to me," he said.

The father divided unto them his living. Soon after, the younger son gathered all together. He took his journey into a far country. Behind him, the familiar fields faded. Before him, the world beckoned with promise.

In the far country, the young man lived riotously. He wasted his substance with riotous living. Coins vanished in laughter. Feasts and revellings filled his nights. Friends gathered round him while the purse was full. He marked not his fortune dwindling — until it was spent.

There arose a mighty famine in that land. The fields without the city dried up and yielded little. Hunger came swiftly. His friends were gone. The younger son had nothing left, not even a coin wherewith to buy bread. In his need, he sought work of a citizen of that country.

The man sent him into his fields to feed swine. Morning and evening, the young man trudged through mire and sour air. He was so an hungred that even the husks the swine did eat began to look good unto him. And no man gave unto him.

His raiment hung in tatters. His skin was smeared with grime. The stench of swine clave unto him. He remembered the warmth of home — the food, the laughter, the songs at mealtimes. Even his father's hired servants had bread enough, and to spare.

He came to himself. He said, "How many hired servants of my father's have bread enough and to spare, and I perish with hunger! I will arise and go to my father, and will say unto him, 'Father, I have sinned against heaven, and before thee, And am no more worthy to be called thy son: make me as one of thy hired servants.'"

He arose, hope flickering in his breast. The dust clave unto his feet as he began the long walk homeward. Every step echoed uncertainty. Yet, as he drew nigh, the old paths grew familiar.

But when he was yet a great way off, his father saw him. The father's heart leapt. Without delay, he ran out across the field — his robe billowing, his feet smiting the earth. With arms wide, he reached his son and fell on his neck, and kissed him. Tears and dust mingled together.

The son, trembling, began the words he had prepared: "Father, I have sinned against heaven, and in thy sight, and am no more worthy to be called thy son."

But before he could finish, the father turned unto his servants. "Bring forth the best robe, and put it on him; and put a ring on his hand, and shoes on his feet. Bring hither the fatted calf, and kill it; and let us eat, and be merry! For this my son was dead, and is alive again; he was lost, and is found."

The servants made haste. Soon, laughter filled the house. The savour of roasted meat drifted into the night. Garlands and musick transformed the chambers. The younger son stood amid it all — robed, clean, the ring shining upon his hand. Joy and wonder mingled in his eyes.

Now the elder son was in the field. As he came and drew nigh to the house, he heard musick and dancing. He called one of the servants, and asked what these things meant. The servant said, "Thy brother is come; and thy father hath killed the fatted calf, because he hath received him safe and sound."

The elder son was angry. He would not go in. His father therefore came out, and intreated him.

The son answering said unto his father, "Lo, these many years do I serve thee, neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment: and yet thou never gavest me a kid, that I might make merry with my friends: But as soon as this thy son was come, which hath devoured thy living with harlots, thou hast killed for him the fatted calf."

The father said unto him, "Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine. It was meet that we should make merry, and be glad: for this thy brother was dead, and is alive again; and was lost, and is found."
