There was a man who had two sons. He lived on his estate, surrounded by fields and servants, flocks and a household full of daily routines. The younger of his sons, restless and driven by a longing for distant places, came to him one day. The son's eyes were determined. Dust clung to his sandals, and the early sun lit his features with hope and impatience.

He looked up at his father and said, “Father, give me my share of your property that falls to me.” The father stood silent for a moment, his face quiet but open. Then, without protest or delay, he divided his property between his two sons. The rich land, the livestock, and the gathered wealth — all counted out, measured, and given.

Not many days passed before the younger son gathered all that was his. The morning he left was clear and bright, the sky stretching above as he set out down the road. He left the world of his father’s house behind — the ring of laughter from the servants, the scent of bread from the kitchen, the comforting weight of home. Ahead of him lay the unknown, and his steps quickened with anticipation.

He journeyed far from familiar fields, away from the well-worn paths of childhood. In a distant country, restless energy coursed through his veins. Freedom felt like gold in his grasp, and he spent his inheritance without restraint. Each day brought new feasts, new faces, and the lure of pleasure. The coins scattered quickly — poured out in living that knew no tomorrow. Wine flowed, music played, and laughter echoed through crowded halls.

But the treasures dwindled faster than he had imagined. One by one, friends slipped away; the parties grew quieter; the food waned. As his riches vanished, a famine struck the land. The air grew heavy and still. Hunger crept through the streets and fields. People grew desperate, their faces drawn and worried.

He felt the ache in his own stomach, clothes hanging off thin shoulders. The scent of roasted meat, once familiar, became only a memory. Needing to survive, he joined himself to one of the citizens of that country—someone who owned fields outside the city. The man sent him into the fields to feed swine.

The work was harsh and humbling. The field was lonely, hot, and foul with the stench of pigs. The boy’s feet sank in the muddy ground, and the snorts of the animals surrounded him. His fine robes gave way to rags, stained and torn. He carried pails of husks, longing only to fill his aching belly.

He looked at the pods the swine ate. Hunger gnawed at him, stronger every day. The pigs chewed, content. No one offered him food. No one remembered him. In the far country, laughter and welcome had faded. Dust clung to his skin, sweat ran into his eyes, and the sun burned overhead, relentless and uncaring.

One day, as he squinted against the glare, he stopped. The wind rustled dry grass, and flies buzzed around the trough. The stench was overwhelming. He pressed a hand to his hollow stomach and remembered home—the lamplight falling softly on bread, servants bustling, his father’s table overflowing.

He came to himself. His words formed quietly at first, then clearer: “How many hired servants of my father’s have bread enough and to spare, and I am dying with hunger! I will get up and go to my father, and I will tell him, ‘Father, I have sinned against heaven, and in your sight. I am no more worthy to be called your son. Make me as one of your hired servants.’”

Resolve took root within him. His mind was troubled, yet hope flickered like a lamp in darkness. He got up. His clothes hung loose and filthy. The empty fields fell away behind him as he started down the long, dusty road toward home.

His steps faltered at times, weighed down by shame and fear. The road seemed endless. Each bend filled him with anxious dread — Would his father turn him away? Would the doors be shut, the lights gone out? Yet the thought of bread, shelter, and just a servant’s welcome urged him on.

At the family estate, the father moved about his daily work: surveying the land, walking among the olive trees, glancing often down the road that led beyond the fields. His heart carried silent longing for the son who had gone away. Every day, his eyes searched the distance.

On this day, as the sun pressed low against the horizon, something stirred. A faint figure walked in the distance — tattered, hesitant, leaning against the wandering wind. The father’s heart leapt. He recognized the walk, the shape. Without pause, the father ran. His feet pounded the earth. Dust rose in his wake. Through the gate, across the yard, out onto the road — he ran, arms open.

Before the son could say a word, the father embraced him. The son’s face was streaked with dust and tears, his hands trembling. He began his confession, voice hoarse: “Father, I have sinned against heaven, and in your sight. I am no more worthy to be called your son.”

The father would not let him finish. Joy overwhelmed sorrow. He called to his servants, voice raised with urgent gladness: “Bring out the best robe and put it on him! Put a ring on his hand and sandals on his feet! Bring the fattened calf, kill it, and let us eat and celebrate — for this, my son, was dead, and is alive again. He was lost, and is found.”

At once, the household stirred. Servants rushed to obey. The best robe, soft and clean, was wrapped around the trembling son’s shoulders. A ring glittered on his hand, sandals eased onto cracked and tired feet.

Inside, the music rose. Laughter rang out. Dishes clattered, the scent of roasted calf filled the air. The long table was laid with fruit, bread, wine, and every good thing. The house pulsed with music and dancing, the sound carrying out beyond the walls into the twilight.

While the celebration echoed within, the older son returned from the fields. His clothes were dusted with the labor of the day. He came near the house, and heard music and dancing. Curious, and a little puzzled, he called to one of the servants and asked what was happening.

The servant replied: “Your brother has come! Your father has killed the fattened calf because he has received him back safe and healthy.”

A shadow crossed the older son’s face. Anger flared in his chest. He would not go in. His feet stood rooted outside while celebration spilled through every window.

His father came out to him, patient and gentle. The father’s eyes pleaded for him to join the joy inside. But the older son answered, pain sharp in his words: “Behold, these many years I have served you, and I never disobeyed your commandment. Yet you never gave me a goat, that I might celebrate with my friends. But when this, your son, came, who has devoured your living with prostitutes, you killed the fattened calf for him.”

The father listened, love unwavering. He said, “Son, you are always with me. All that is mine is yours. But it was appropriate to celebrate and be glad, for this, your brother, was dead, and is alive again. He was lost, and is found.”
