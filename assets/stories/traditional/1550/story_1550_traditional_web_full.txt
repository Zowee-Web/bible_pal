The hill was bare rock and dry soil. They called it the Skull. Three upright beams had been set into holes cut in the stone, and three crossbeams had been fixed to them, and three men hung from nails driven through their wrists and feet. The center cross was taller than the other two by a span. A sign had been nailed above the head of the man in the middle. It read: This is the King of the Jews.

Jesus of Nazareth hung there. His body was torn from the scourging. Blood had dried in dark lines across his back and shoulders. The crown of thorns sat pressed into his scalp, and where each point had pierced the skin, a thin trail of red ran down his forehead and into his brows.

On either side of him, a criminal hung from identical wood. Their crimes had earned them this death. Their arms were stretched wide, their chests heaving with the effort of pulling air. Every breath required them to push upward on the nails through their feet, then sag again. The motion was slow and constant, like the rise and fall of something caught in water.

The soldiers had finished their work. They sat below the crosses on the rocky ground, casting lots for the garments they had stripped from the condemned. The click of small stones in a clay cup. The murmur of numbers. One of them spread a robe across his knee to examine the weave.

A crowd stood at a distance. Some watched in silence. Others had already turned away.

The man on the left cross spoke first. His voice was strained, pulled thin by the weight on his lungs. He turned his head toward Jesus and said, "Are you not the Christ? Save yourself and us."

The words crossed the space between the beams. Jesus did not answer. The wind moved across the hilltop and stirred the dust at the base of the crosses.

The man on the right cross heard what was said. He shifted against the wood. The movement sent a visible tremor through his arms, and his face tightened. He drew a breath, pushing up on the iron through his feet, and spoke.

"Do you not fear God?" His voice was rough but carried. "You are under the same condemnation."

The first criminal turned his head but did not speak.

The man on the right continued. Each sentence came between labored breaths. "We are here justly. We receive the due reward of what we have done." He paused. His chest expanded and contracted. Sweat ran from his hairline down the side of his face and dripped from his jaw onto the crossbeam.

"But this man," he said, and he looked toward the center cross, "has done nothing wrong."

Below, one of the soldiers glanced up briefly, then looked away. The lots continued. A gust of wind lifted grit from the ground and scattered it against the base of the wooden beams. The sky had gone dark — not the darkness of evening, but something heavier, as though a thick cloud had settled over the whole land. The light that remained was flat and gray.

The man on the right cross was quiet for a moment. His fingers were curled against the wood behind his palms, and the nails held his hands open and still. Blood had pooled where the iron met flesh and dried in dark rings around each wound.

He turned his head toward Jesus. The effort showed in his neck — the tendons drawn tight beneath the skin, his jaw set against the pain of the movement. He looked at the face of the man on the center cross. The bruises from the beatings had swollen his features. His eyes were partly closed. His lips were cracked and pale.

The criminal spoke.

"Lord," he said. "Remember me when you come into your kingdom."

The words were simple. They crossed the short distance between the two crosses and reached the man they were meant for.

Jesus opened his eyes. He turned his head toward the man on the right. The thorns shifted slightly against his scalp as he moved. A fresh line of blood traced down his temple.

He spoke. His voice was clear — not loud, but distinct enough that the man beside him could hear every word above the low wind.

"Truly I tell you," Jesus said, "today you will be with me in paradise."

The man on the right cross looked at Jesus. His mouth was open slightly, drawing air. His hands stayed where the nails held them, palms up against the rough grain of the crossbeam. The blood on his wrists had gone dark.

Below, the soldiers sat with the divided garments across their laps. The crowd stood scattered on the lower slope, their robes pressed flat against their legs by the wind. The sky stayed dark over the hill. The three crosses stood in a line against that darkness, and the only sound was the wind moving across the bare stone of the Skull.
