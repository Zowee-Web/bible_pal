Moses had sent twelve men to search out the promised land — the land the Lord said he would give unto the people of Israel. The twelve spies had been gone forty days, and the whole camp was waiting. Every morning the people looked toward the north and wondered: What have they found? Is the land good? When shall they return? The little ones asked their parents. Parents asked one another. None had answers. The tents stretched out across the wilderness, and the waiting made all men restless.

Then on an afternoon, they appeared.

Two men came walking slowly, and between them they bare a staff. Hanging from the staff was the greatest cluster of grapes that any man had ever beheld. It was so great, so heavy with fruit, that it took two grown men to bear it. The grapes were dark purple and round, each one fat as a child's fist, and they seemed as though they might burst with juice. The staff bowed in the midst from the weight thereof.

After them came more spies bearing pomegranates — smooth and red and beautiful, their skin tight and shining in the sun — and figs that were soft and ripe, yet warm from the southern hills. The whole camp gathered round, and marvelled. The little ones ran up to behold them more nearly. A girl stretched forth her hand and touched one of the grapes, and her fingers came back sticky with sweetness. Parents pointed and whispered one to another. Old men stood on tiptoe at the back of the crowd. This was the fruit of the promised land!

The spies gave their report unto Moses and all the people. "We came unto the land whither thou sentest us," they said. "Surely it floweth with milk and honey. Behold the fruit of it!" They held up the grapes and the pomegranates and the figs. Juice ran down the staff and dripped into the dust.

For a moment, all were glad. The land was real. The land was good.

But then the spies kept on speaking.

"Nevertheless," they said — and that one word changed all things — "the people that dwell in the land are strong. Their cities are walled and very great, higher than aught we have seen. The gates are of iron. The towers are of stone. And we saw the sons of Anak there." The sons of Anak were giants. Mighty men. The kind of men that made one feel very, very small but to stand near them.

The smiles vanished away. Mothers caught hold of their children. Men looked upon one another with wide eyes. A woman near the front put her hand upon her mouth. The gladness drained out of the camp as water out of a cracked vessel.

But then Caleb stood up.

Caleb was one of the twelve spies. He had seen the same giants. He had seen the same walls. He had walked the same land, felt the same dry wind, stood in the shade of the same stone towers. But Caleb believed somewhat other than the ten.

"Let us go up at once, and possess it!" said Caleb. His voice was strong and sure, cutting through the silence as a trumpet. "For we are well able to overcome it!"

For a moment, all were silent. They looked upon Caleb. They looked upon the fruit. They looked upon one another.

But the other ten spies shook their heads. "We be not able to go up against the people," they said. "For they are stronger than we. The land eateth up the inhabitants thereof. We saw giants — the sons of Anak — and we were in our own sight as grasshoppers. So small did we appear. So small did we feel."

Grasshoppers. The word spread through the camp as smoke. The people began to weep. The people began to murmur. Some sat down upon the ground and would not rise. Others cried out against Moses and Aaron, their voices sharp and wroth. The fear was louder than the courage of Caleb. It drowned out all.

That night the whole camp lifted up their voice and wept. Voices carried in the dark — the sounds of a people who had forgotten all that the Lord had wrought for them. The plagues. The Red Sea. The manna every morning. The water out of the rock. All of it cast aside by one word: grasshoppers.

But Caleb had said it still. Before all the people, with the same things seen and the same giants and the same walls, Caleb had stood up and said: we are well able.

And Joshua stood with him. Two men against ten. Two voices against a whole camp full of weeping. Joshua's countenance was set. Caleb's hands were steady.

The grapes yet hung heavy upon the staff, dripping. The pomegranates yet sat in the dust, their red skin catching the last of the firelight. The proof of the promise was there — sweet, true, and to be handled. And two men of valour stood beside it, feet planted, waiting.
