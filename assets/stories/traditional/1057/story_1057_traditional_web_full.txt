Moses had sent twelve men to go look at the promised land — the land God said he would give to the people of Israel. The twelve spies had been gone for forty days, and the whole camp was waiting. Every morning people looked toward the north and wondered: What did they find? Is the land good? When are they coming back? Kids asked their parents. Parents asked each other. Nobody had answers. The tents stretched out across the desert, and the waiting made everyone restless.

Then one afternoon, they appeared.

Two men came walking slowly, and between them they carried a pole. Hanging from the pole was the biggest cluster of grapes anyone had ever seen. It was so enormous, so heavy with fruit, that it took two grown men to carry it. The grapes were dark purple and round, each one fat as a child's fist, and they looked like they might burst with juice. The pole bowed in the middle from the weight.

Behind them came more spies carrying pomegranates — smooth and red and beautiful, their skin tight and shining in the sun — and figs that were soft and ripe, still warm from the southern hills. The whole camp gathered around, amazed. Kids ran up to get a closer look. A girl reached out and touched one of the grapes, and her fingers came back sticky with sweetness. Parents pointed and whispered to each other. Old men stood on tiptoe at the back of the crowd. This was fruit from the promised land!

The spies gave their report to Moses and all the people. "We went to the land where you sent us," they said. "It really does flow with milk and honey. Look at this fruit!" They held up the grapes and the pomegranates and the figs. Juice ran down the pole and dripped into the dust.

For a moment, everyone was happy. The land was real. The land was good.

But then the spies kept talking.

"However," they said — and that one word changed everything — "the people who live there are very strong. Their cities have thick, tall walls, higher than anything we've seen. The gates are iron. The towers are stone. And we saw the sons of Anak there." The sons of Anak were giants. Huge people. The kind of people who made you feel very, very small just by standing near them.

The smiles disappeared. Mothers reached for their children. Men looked at each other with wide eyes. A woman near the front put her hand over her mouth. The happy feeling drained out of the camp like water out of a cracked jar.

But then Caleb stood up.

Caleb was one of the twelve spies. He had seen the same giants. He had seen the same walls. He had walked the same land, felt the same dry wind, stood in the shade of the same stone towers. But Caleb believed something different from the other ten.

"Let us go up at once and take the land!" Caleb said. His voice was strong and sure, cutting through the silence like a horn. "We can do it. We are well able to overcome!"

For a second, everyone was quiet. They looked at Caleb. They looked at the fruit. They looked at each other.

But the other ten spies shook their heads. "We can't do it," they said. "Those people are way stronger than us. The land eats up the people who live there. We saw giants — the sons of Anak — and we were like grasshoppers compared to them. That's how tiny we looked. That's how tiny we felt."

Grasshoppers. The word spread through the camp like smoke. People started to cry. People started to complain. Some sat down on the ground and refused to move. Others shouted at Moses and Aaron, their voices sharp and angry. The fear was louder than Caleb's courage. It drowned out everything.

That night the whole camp wept. Voices carried in the dark — the sounds of people who had forgotten everything God had done for them. The plagues. The Red Sea. The manna every morning. The water from the rock. All of it pushed aside by one word: grasshoppers.

But Caleb had still said it. In front of everyone, with the same facts and the same giants and the same walls, Caleb had stood up and said: we can do this.

And Joshua stood with him. Two men against ten. Two voices against a whole camp full of crying. Joshua's jaw was set. Caleb's hands were steady.

The grapes hung heavy on the pole, still dripping. The pomegranates sat in the dust, their red skin catching the last of the firelight. The proof of the promise was right there — sweet, real, and touchable. And two brave men stood beside it, feet planted, waiting for the people to believe.
