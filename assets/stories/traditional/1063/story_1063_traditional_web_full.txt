There was a man named Job who lived in a land called Uz, and he was the kind of person everybody respected. The Bible says he was "blameless and upright" — which means he tried to do the right thing every single day. He feared God, which means he took God seriously. And he turned away from evil, which means he chose good even when it was hard.

Job had a big, happy family. Seven sons and three daughters — ten children who loved each other. They would take turns hosting feasts at their houses. One brother would have everyone over one day, another brother the next day. The sisters came too. It was like having a party every week.

And Job was wealthy. Very, very wealthy. He owned seven thousand sheep — imagine a hillside covered with them, white dots as far as you could see. Three thousand camels that carried goods across the desert. Five hundred teams of oxen for plowing the fields. Five hundred donkeys. And a huge number of servants who took care of it all. The Bible says Job was the greatest man in all the east.

But what made Job truly special wasn't his money or his animals. It was his heart.

Every morning, after his children had been at a feast, Job would get up before sunrise. He would offer sacrifices to God — one for each of his children. "Just in case one of them sinned without knowing it," Job said. He prayed for them every single day. He never missed a morning.

That's the kind of man Job was. Good to the bone. Faithful to the core.

Then came one day that changed everything.

Job was at home when a messenger came running. The man was covered in dust and could barely catch his breath.

"The oxen were plowing in the field and the donkeys were grazing nearby," the messenger said, "and the Sabeans attacked. They took all the animals and killed the servants. I'm the only one who escaped to tell you."

Job's eyes went wide. Five hundred oxen. Five hundred donkeys. All his field workers. Gone.

But before that messenger finished talking, another one came running in behind him.

"Fire fell from the sky!" this one said. "It burned up all the sheep and the shepherds. I'm the only one who made it."

Seven thousand sheep. The hillside that had been full of bleating was silent. All those shepherds — gone.

Job opened his mouth to speak but a third messenger was already at the door.

"Three groups of raiders attacked the camels. They took every single one and killed all the servants. I'm the only one left."

Three thousand camels. Gone in an afternoon.

And then the fourth messenger arrived. This one was different. He was shaking. He could barely stand. His face was white.

"Your sons and your daughters," he said, and his voice cracked, "were eating and drinking at your oldest son's house. A terrible wind came across the wilderness and hit all four corners of the house. The house collapsed. Your children — all of them — are dead. I'm the only one who escaped."

The room went completely silent.

Four messengers. Four disasters. In the space of maybe an hour, Job had lost everything — every animal, every worker, and all ten of his children. The man who had been the richest, most blessed person in the entire east had nothing left except the floor beneath his feet and the air in his lungs.

Job stood up.

He reached down and took hold of his robe — the nice outer garment he was wearing — and tore it with both hands. The fabric ripped with a sharp, loud sound. Then he shaved his head. In those days, tearing your clothes and shaving your head were the ways people showed that their grief was deeper than words could hold.

Then Job fell down to the ground. Not to give up. Not in anger. He fell to the ground and he worshiped.

And he said words that people have been repeating for thousands of years, words that still make grownups stop and think:

"I came into this world with nothing. And when I leave, I will take nothing with me. The Lord gave me everything I had. And the Lord has taken it away. Blessed be the name of the Lord."

The room was still. Job knelt on the stone floor, his torn robe hanging from his shoulders, his shaved head bowed low. His hands lay open on the ground in front of him.

Outside, the wind that had taken everything was gone. The sky was empty and quiet. And Job stayed where he was, on his knees, in the silence.
