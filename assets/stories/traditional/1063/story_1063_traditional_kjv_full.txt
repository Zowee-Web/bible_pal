There was a man whose name was Job who dwelt in the land of Uz, and he was the kind of man that all gave honour unto. The scripture saith he was "perfect and upright" — which is to say that he sought to do that which was right every single day. He feared God, which is to say he held God in high reverence. And he eschewed evil, which is to say he chose good even when it was hard.

Job had a great and joyful household. Seven sons and three daughters — ten children that loved one another. They were wont to keep feasts in their houses, every one his day. One brother would have them all unto his house upon a day, and another brother upon the morrow. The sisters came also. It was as a feast every week.

And Job was wealthy. Very, very wealthy. He had seven thousand sheep — imagine a hillside covered over with them, white spots as far as the eye could see. Three thousand camels that bare burdens across the wilderness. Five hundred yoke of oxen to till the fields. Five hundred she-asses. And a very great household of servants that kept them all. The scripture saith Job was the greatest of all the men of the east.

But that which made Job truly noble was not his riches nor his cattle. It was his heart.

Every morning, after his children had kept their feast, Job would rise up early before the sun. He would offer burnt offerings unto God — one for every one of his children. "It may be that my sons have sinned, and cursed God in their hearts," Job said. He prayed for them every single day. He failed not of a single morning.

Such a man was Job. Good unto the bone. Faithful unto the core.

Then came one day that changed all things.

Job was at home when a messenger came running. The man was covered with dust and could scarce draw breath.

"The oxen were plowing, and the asses feeding beside them," the messenger said, "and the Sabeans fell upon them, and took them away; yea, they have slain the servants with the edge of the sword; and I only am escaped alone to tell thee."

Job's eyes opened wide. Five hundred oxen. Five hundred asses. All his men of the field. Gone.

But while he was yet speaking, there came also another, running in behind him.

"The fire of God is fallen from heaven!" said he. "It hath burned up the sheep, and the servants, and consumed them; and I only am escaped alone to tell thee."

Seven thousand sheep. The hillside that had been full of bleating was silent. All those shepherds — gone.

Job opened his mouth to speak, but a third messenger was already at the door.

"The Chaldeans made out three bands, and fell upon the camels, and have carried them away, yea, and slain the servants with the edge of the sword; and I only am escaped alone to tell thee."

Three thousand camels. Gone in an afternoon.

And then came the fourth messenger. This one was other than the rest. He trembled. He could scarce stand. His face was pale.

"Thy sons and thy daughters," he said, and his voice brake, "were eating and drinking wine in their eldest brother's house. And, behold, there came a great wind from the wilderness, and smote the four corners of the house, and it fell upon the young men, and they are dead; and I only am escaped alone to tell thee."

The chamber was wholly silent.

Four messengers. Four ruins. In the space of perhaps an hour, Job had lost all — every beast, every servant, and all ten of his children. The man that had been the richest, the most blessed in all the east had nothing left save the floor beneath his feet and the breath in his lungs.

Then Job arose.

He took hold of his mantle — the goodly outer garment he had on — and rent it with both hands. The cloth tore with a sharp and loud sound. Then he shaved his head. In those days, the rending of the garments and the shaving of the head were the manner whereby a man did shew that his grief was deeper than words could hold.

Then Job fell down upon the ground. Not in despair. Not in anger. He fell upon the ground and worshipped.

He said:

"Naked came I out of my mother's womb, and naked shall I return thither. The Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away; blessed be the name of the Lord."

The chamber was still. Job knelt upon the stone floor, his rent mantle hanging from his shoulders, his shaved head bowed low. His hands lay open upon the ground before him.

Without, the wind that had taken all was gone. The sky was empty and quiet. And Job abode where he was, upon his knees, in the silence.
