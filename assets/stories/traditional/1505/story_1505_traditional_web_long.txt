The king of Syria was warring against Israel. In his tent at the end of the day he took counsel with his servants, and he leaned over a low table where lamps burned in clay dishes, and he set his finger on a place. "My camp will be in such and such a place," he said. The captains nodded. They went out into the dusk and gave the orders. By morning the chosen ground was held.

But the king of Israel did not pass that way. He sent men ahead, and they found the Syrian camp where the king had set his finger. He saved himself there.

It happened again. The king of Syria moved his tent and chose another place, and again the king of Israel did not pass. It happened more than twice. Three times. Four. The Syrian captains began to count the failures on their fingers and look at one another.

The heart of the king of Syria was sore troubled. He called his servants together inside the tent. The lamps had burned low. His voice was low with them. "Won't you show me which of us is for the king of Israel?"

The servants were silent. They knew the question was an accusation. Each man's eye searched the eye of the man next to him. Then one of them spoke. "No, my lord, O king. But Elisha, the prophet who is in Israel, tells the king of Israel the words that you speak in your bedchamber."

The king sat back. The accusation in the room lifted, and another thing settled in its place — a quieter, stranger fear. He had thought he was alone when he made his plans. He had thought the walls of his tent were walls. He took a breath.

"Go and see where he is," he said, "that I may send and get him."

Word came back. "Behold, he is in Dothan."

The king sent horses and chariots and a great army. They came by night. They moved without torches over the last hills. They surrounded the city of Dothan in the dark, and they waited for the dawn to take the prophet.

Inside the city, the servant of the man of God rose early in the morning. The light was still thin. He climbed the stairs to the wall to look out over the country, as servants will do at first light, half awake, expecting nothing. He reached the top. He set his hands on the stones.

He saw them.

The hills around Dothan were no longer hills. They were horses. They were chariots. They were a great host, ringed entirely around the city, banner and shield and spear. The first sun caught their helmets. The servant did not breathe. He turned and went back down the stairs. He did not run; his legs would not let him run; he walked the way a man walks in a dream where the air has gone thick.

He found Elisha. "Alas, my master!" he said. "What shall we do?"

Elisha was already awake. He was standing near the door of the house, looking out at the morning the way a man looks at a morning he has been given many times. He did not go to the wall. He did not ask the servant to describe what he had seen. He answered without turning.

"Don't be afraid. Those who are with us are more than those who are with them."

The servant could not answer. The number he had counted was very large.

Then Elisha prayed. He did not raise his voice. The words were spoken into the open air of the doorway. "Lord, please open his eyes, that he may see."

The servant felt nothing change in his body. But the morning changed.

He looked again at the hills.

The hills were full of fire.

Horses of fire. Chariots of fire. They stood on the ridges and on the slopes, ringed around Elisha, ringed around the city — outside the Syrian ring, around it, above it. They burned without smoke. They did not move. Their light was not the light of the rising sun; the rising sun was beside it, smaller. The servant stood in the doorway and his mouth was open, and Elisha stood beside him with the same settled face he had worn when he had not gone to look.

The servant began, very slowly, to understand what Elisha had been seeing all morning.

The Syrian army came down the slope toward the city. Elisha walked out to meet them. He prayed again, with the same quiet voice. "Please strike this people with blindness." The word went out. The men of Syria stopped where they were. Their eyes were open. Their eyes saw nothing they could name.

Elisha came down to where they stood. He spoke to them as a man speaks to lost travelers. "This is not the way," he said. "Neither is this the city. Follow me, and I will bring you to the man whom you seek."

He turned. He began to walk. The whole army of Syria, the horses and chariots and the great host, fell in behind him. The walk was a long one. The road climbed. The road fell. The road passed villages whose names the Syrian captains had carried in their heads for years and could not now place. Children stood by the way and watched the strange procession pass — a single man on foot, leading an army that did not look at where it was going. Elisha walked at the pace of an old prophet on a road he knew. The men of Syria followed him, step by step, all the long miles to Samaria.

When they came into the city, Elisha prayed once more. "Lord, open these men's eyes, that they may see."

Their eyes were opened.

The Syrian captains looked up. The gates they had passed under were the gates of Samaria. The streets around them were the streets of the king of Israel's own city. Israelite soldiers stood at every corner of the square, in arms, watching them.

The king of Israel came down. When he saw the army standing in his own square, his hand went to his sword and his voice rose in eagerness. "My father, shall I strike them? Shall I strike them?"

Elisha looked at him. He answered slowly.

"You shall not strike them. Would you strike those whom you have taken captive with your sword and with your bow? Set bread and water before them, that they may eat and drink, then go to their master."

The king stood with his hand on the hilt. Then he let his hand fall.

A great feast was prepared in the city. Long tables were set up in the open. Loaves were carried out and set down, and jars of water, and dishes of meat. The men of Syria sat. They sat the way men sit who do not know if they will rise. They looked at the food in front of them. They looked at the Israelite soldiers around them, who stood with their arms lowered. They looked at Elisha, who walked among the tables as a host walks among guests.

They ate. They drank. The captains who had counted failures on their fingers in the king's tent ate the bread of Israel out of Israel's own hand. The strangeness of it was a quietness over the whole square.

When they had eaten and drunk, Elisha sent them away. They went back to their king.

And the bands of Syria came no more into the land of Israel.
