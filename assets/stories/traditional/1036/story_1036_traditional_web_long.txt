There was a wealthy man in Bethlehem named Boaz, a relative of Elimelech. He was a mighty man of valor. He held land outside the city walls, and his name was spoken with respect at the gate. The barley harvest had come, and the fields outside the city stood ripe and golden under the wide sky. Men with sickles walked the rows from first light. Women followed behind them, binding what was cut into sheaves. The gleaners came last — the poor, the widow, the foreigner — gathering what the reapers' hands had let fall.

Ruth the Moabite spoke to Naomi in the small house they shared. "Let me go to the field and glean among the ears of grain, behind someone in whose sight I may find favor." Naomi looked at her a long moment. The morning light was grey through the doorway. "Go, my daughter."

She went out early. The air was cool and dew still clung to the lower stalks. The path from the city wound down past the threshing floor and out into the fields. Other gleaners were already on the road, their cloths folded under their arms, their feet quiet in the dust. Ruth did not know any of them. She walked with her head covered and her eyes lowered. She came to a field and began to glean behind the reapers. She did not know whose field it was. It belonged to Boaz.

She bent low and gathered what fell from the reapers' hands. The stalks were sharp at the cut ends. Chaff stuck to her forearms and clung to her dress. The sun rose higher and pressed heat against her back. She moved along the rows with the other gleaners but worked alone, speaking to no one. When a stalk fell within reach of two gleaners, the other waited and let her take it. She did not look up to see why.

Boaz came from Bethlehem. He walked along the edge of the field with the steady gait of a man who knew his land. He greeted his reapers. "May Yahweh be with you." They lifted their heads from their work. "May Yahweh bless you."

He looked across the field and saw the young woman gleaning at the far end. Her head covering was unfamiliar. The way she bent and rose, the way she did not speak with the others, drew his eye. He turned to his servant who was set over the reapers. "Whose young woman is this?"

The servant answered. "She is the Moabite woman who came back with Naomi from the country of Moab. She said when she came, 'Please let me glean and gather behind the reapers among the sheaves.' She has continued from the morning until now. She has hardly rested in the shelter of the house."

Boaz crossed the field to where Ruth worked. The dust rose where his sandals struck the ground. She straightened when she heard his step and turned. He spoke first. "Listen, my daughter. Do not go to glean in another field. Do not leave this one. Stay close to my young women. Their eyes shall be on the field that they reap. Walk after them. I have charged the young men not to touch you. When you are thirsty, go to the vessels and drink what the young men have drawn."

Ruth fell on her face. She bowed to the ground. Her hands pressed into the loose soil between the cut stalks. "Why have I found favor in your eyes, that you should notice me, since I am a foreigner?"

Boaz answered. He did not raise his voice. "It has been fully reported to me, all that you have done for your mother-in-law since your husband died. How you left your father, your mother, and the land of your birth, and came to a people you did not know before. May Yahweh reward your work. May a full reward be given you by Yahweh, the God of Israel, under whose wings you have come to take refuge."

She lifted her face. "Let me find favor in your sight, my lord. You have comforted me and you have spoken kindly to your servant, though I am not as one of your servant women."

The sun reached its highest point. The reapers set down their sickles and gathered in the shade of a tree at the edge of the field. Cloths were spread on the ground. Wineskins were brought out. Boaz called to Ruth. "Come here at mealtime. Eat bread and dip your morsel in the vinegar."

She sat beside the reapers. They made room. Boaz passed her roasted grain with his own hand. She ate and was satisfied. She had some left over, and she folded it into a cloth at her side. The bread was coarse, the vinegar sharp, the grain warm from the fire. The reapers ate and spoke quietly among themselves, and now and again one of them glanced toward her and looked away.

When she rose to glean again, Boaz commanded his young men privately. "Let her glean even among the sheaves. Do not rebuke her. Also pull out some from the bundles on purpose for her, and leave it. Let her glean. Do not shame her."

She gleaned in the field until the evening. The reapers worked steadily through the long heat. The gleaners walked their rows behind them. When the shadows began to grow long across the stubble, Ruth gathered what she had collected onto a cloth at the edge of the field. She beat out what she had gleaned, striking the stalks against the ground until the grain fell free. It was about an ephah of barley — more than a day's wages for a working man. She lifted the cloth onto her shoulder. It was heavy. She walked back toward the city in the slanting light.

The road into Bethlehem rose between low walls. Other gleaners were on it now too, carrying their day's gathering. Some had little. Some had less. None had what Ruth carried. The people at the gate watched her pass.

Naomi was at the doorway. Ruth set the grain on the floor inside and brought out the food saved from the meal. Naomi looked at the heap of barley. Her eyes widened. She looked at her daughter-in-law and back at the grain. "Where did you glean today? Where did you work? Blessed be the man who took notice of you."

Ruth told her what had happened. She told her about the servant who had spoken for her, about Boaz crossing the field, about the meal in the shade, about the command to the young men. Then she said, "The man's name with whom I worked today is Boaz."

Naomi's hands went still upon the grain. She looked up. Her face changed. "Blessed be he of Yahweh, who has not left off his kindness to the living and to the dead." She paused, and her voice was lower. "This man is a close relative to us. He is one of our kinsman-redeemers."

Ruth said, "He also said to me, 'You shall stay close to my young men until they have finished all my harvest.'"

Naomi said to Ruth her daughter-in-law, "It is good, my daughter, that you go out with his young women, and that they not meet you in any other field."

So Ruth kept close to the young women of Boaz to glean to the end of the barley harvest and of the wheat harvest. The mornings were many and the work was steady. Each evening she returned with grain on her shoulder and food saved from the meal at her side. The neighbors in Bethlehem grew used to the sight of her on the road in the long light. She lived with her mother-in-law, and the house was not empty, and the grain did not run out, and the harvest came in.
