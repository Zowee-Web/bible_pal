The decree had gone out throughout all the king's provinces. The writing had been carried by post-riders on swift horses, sealed with the king's signet. It said that on the thirteenth day of the twelfth month, in the month Adar, all the Jews — old and young, women and children, in all the provinces from India to Ethiopia — would be destroyed in one day, and their goods spoiled. The decree had been sealed. There was no taking it back.

When Mordecai heard, he rent his clothes. He put on sackcloth. He covered his head with ashes. He went out into the midst of the city. He cried with a loud and bitter cry. He came as far as the king's gate. He could not enter in. No one might enter the king's gate clothed with sackcloth.

In every province where the king's commandment came, the news brought mourning among the Jews. There was weeping. There was fasting. Many of them lay in sackcloth and ashes. In Shushan itself, the mourning was heaviest. The synagogues were full. The houses were quiet. The streets where the Jews lived were filled with the sound of weeping that came up out of the open doors.

Esther's maids and her chamberlains came in to her and told her about Mordecai. She was exceedingly grieved. She sent raiment out to him to put on, that he might come into the gate. He would not take it. The clothing came back unworn.

She sent Hatach the chamberlain. Hatach went out to him in the open square that was before the king's gate. Mordecai told him everything. He told him the king had been bribed to destroy them. He told him the silver Haman had promised to weigh into the king's treasuries. He gave him a copy of the writing of the decree, that he might show it to Esther. He charged Hatach with the message that she should go in unto the king. She should make supplication for her people.

"Who knoweth," Mordecai said, "whether thou art come to the kingdom for such a time as this?"

Hatach carried the message back to Esther. He carried the copy of the decree with it. She read the copy. She did not speak for a while.

She knew what going in unto the king without being called could mean. She knew the law. There was one law for him or her that went in unto the king into the inner court and had not been called. They should be put to death, unless the king held out his golden scepter, that they might live. The scepter was in his hand. She had not been called for thirty days. She did not know whether his heart toward her had changed.

She sent her answer back. She did not send it quickly. She thought about it first.

"Go," she said when she had thought. "Gather all the Jews that are present in Shushan. Fast for me. Eat not, neither drink, for three days, night and day. I and my maidens will fast in the same manner. And so will I go in unto the king, which is not according to the law. And if I perish, I perish."

When the answer was carried out, Mordecai went his way and did all that Esther had commanded him. The Jews of Shushan stopped their eating. The houses went quiet across the city. The fast was kept in the streets, in the synagogues, in the houses, in the courts. The three days began.

In the inner chamber Esther's table was set aside. The lamps burned through the night. The maidens did not speak. The dishes remained where they had been put. The wine was not poured. Esther sat in her own room and did not see anyone. She prayed in the silence. The hours went over her one by one.

The first day went. The second came. The third came. The houses of the city stayed quiet. Esther did not eat. She did not drink. The maidens watched her from across the room. They did not speak.

On the second day, the priests in the synagogues read the scrolls aloud. The people listened. The fast deepened. The mourning was no longer only in the streets where the Jews lived. The whole city had felt it now.

The third night went on across the city. The lamps in Esther's chamber were burning low. The maidens were keeping watch. They walked the corridor between the rooms on quiet feet. They had not slept either. They had taken the fast on themselves with her. They had been with her in the inner court for three days.

The morning of the fourth day was coming on. She would put on her royal apparel. She would walk down the corridor to the inner court of the king's house. She would stand where the king could see her. The gold scepter would be in his hand. He would either hold it out to her, or not.

Beyond the corridor, in the king's own room, the scepter rested on the table beside him. The lamps in his chamber had been turned down for the night. The wine and the bread on his table were not touched. He had been awake some hours already. The morning was coming on. The walls of the palace held the city's silence inside them, and the country of Shushan went on with the last hours of the dark.
