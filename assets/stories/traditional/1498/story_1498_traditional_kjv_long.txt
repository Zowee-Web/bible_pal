The decree had gone forth throughout all the king's provinces. The writing had been carried by posts upon swift horses, sealed with the king's signet. It said that upon the thirteenth day of the twelfth month, in the month Adar, all the Jews — both young and old, little children and women, in all the provinces from India to Ethiopia — should be destroyed in one day, and their goods taken for a spoil. The decree had been sealed. There was no taking it back.

When Mordecai heard, he rent his clothes. He put on sackcloth. He covered his head with ashes. He went out into the midst of the city. He cried with a loud and a bitter cry. He came even before the king's gate. He could not enter in. None might enter the king's gate clothed with sackcloth.

In every province whithersoever the king's commandment came, there was great mourning among the Jews. There was weeping. There was fasting. Many of them lay in sackcloth and ashes. In Shushan itself, the mourning was heaviest. The synagogues were full. The houses were silent. The streets wherein the Jews dwelt were filled with the sound of weeping that came up out of the open doors.

Esther's maids and her chamberlains came in unto her and told her concerning Mordecai. She was exceedingly grieved. She sent raiment unto him to put on, that he might come into the gate. He would not receive it. The raiment came back unworn.

She sent Hatach the chamberlain. Hatach went forth unto him into the open square that was before the king's gate. Mordecai told him all. He told him the king had been bribed to destroy them. He told him the silver Haman had promised to pay into the king's treasuries. He gave unto him a copy of the writing of the decree, that he might shew it unto Esther. He charged Hatach with the word that she should go in unto the king. She should make supplication for her people.

"Who knoweth," Mordecai said, "whether thou art come to the kingdom for such a time as this?"

Hatach carried the word back unto Esther. He carried the copy of the decree withal. She read the copy. She spake not for a while.

She knew what going in unto the king without being called could mean. She knew the law. There was one law for him or her that went in unto the king into the inner court and was not called. They should be put to death, except such to whom the king should hold out the golden sceptre, that they might live. The sceptre was in his hand. She had not been called for thirty days. She knew not whether his heart toward her had changed.

She sent her answer back. She sent it not in haste. She thought upon it first.

"Go," she said when she had thought. "Gather together all the Jews that are present in Shushan. Fast ye for me. Neither eat nor drink three days, night nor day. I also and my maidens will fast likewise. And so will I go in unto the king, which is not according to the law. And if I perish, I perish."

When the answer was carried out, Mordecai went his way and did according unto all that Esther had commanded him. The Jews of Shushan ceased their eating. The houses were silent throughout the city. The fast was kept in the streets, in the synagogues, in the houses, in the courts. The three days began.

In the inner chamber the table of Esther was set aside. The lamps burned through the night. The maidens spake not. The dishes remained where they had been laid. The wine was not poured. Esther sat in her own room and saw not any. She prayed in the silence. The hours went over her one by one.

The first day went. The second came. The third came. The houses of the city stayed silent. Esther did eat not. She did drink not. The maidens watched her from across the room. They spake not.

On the second day, the priests in the synagogues read the scrolls aloud. The people hearkened. The fast deepened. The mourning was no longer only in the streets wherein the Jews dwelt. The whole city had felt it.

The third night went on across the city. The lamps in Esther's chamber were burning low. The maidens were keeping watch. They walked the corridor between the chambers upon quiet feet. They had not slept either. They had taken the fast upon themselves with her. They had been with her in the inner court for three days.

The morning of the fourth day was coming on. She should put on her royal apparel. She should walk down the corridor unto the inner court of the king's house. She should stand where the king could see her. The golden sceptre should be in his hand. He should either hold it out unto her, or not.

Beyond the corridor, within the king's own room, the sceptre rested upon the table beside him. The lamps in his chamber had been turned down for the night. The wine and the bread upon his table were not touched. He had been awake some hours already. The morning was coming on. The walls of the palace held the silence of the city within them, and the land of Shushan went on with the last hours of the dark.
