In the wilderness, when the dew of morning still clung to the tents of Israel, a hush drifted over the camp. The sun rose, burning slowly through thin clouds, its glare stretching across the gathering people. They stood outside their shelters, hundreds of men and women, their children at their feet, the dust gathering on their sandals. It was a worn path, for each new day brought new quarrels and questions. Some had been waiting since the deep hours before dawn, clutching hopes and grievances, longing for judgment from Moses himself.

Moses came from his tent at sunrise, his face shadowed with the labor of many days. He passed through the rows of tribes, his steps steady, but his eyes weary. The people called to him as he passed, some with gentle voices, others with tense insistence. All of them waited for his word.

Jethro, his father-in-law, was still a guest among them. He watched from the doorway of Moses’ tent. The aged Midianite’s eyes followed Moses’ path as he moved toward his place of judgment. Jethro listened to the hum of the camp, to the shifting sounds of sandals and murmured voices, to the cries of infants and the creak of leather under the weight of the day.

The gathering grew as the morning burned toward noon. The sun pressed down, heavy and bright. Moses sat in his seat. The people drew near, each waiting for their turn. Some shaded their eyes with their hands. Others huddled together, dust rising around their feet. A mother soothed her child as she waited. Elder men leaned on their staffs.

One by one, they came before Moses. They laid out their disputes—issues of property, offenses given or taken, matters of debt, misunderstandings about the statutes God had given. Some brought fears for their kin; others wounds from the night before. Moses listened, leaning forward, his brow furrowed. He judged between friend and neighbor, between brother and stranger.

His voice carried, patient but strained. “Between you and your brother, you shall repay what is owed, according to the word of the Lord.” Another came with anxious words. Moses listened. He searched the law, handed down by the Lord, and gave his answer. The line of people melted forward, new faces for each decision.

Jethro watched this scene unfold. From dawn until the afternoon shadow, the line never parted. The sun climbed; the camp shimmered in the heat. Moses did not rise from his seat. He spoke with kindness, but weariness crept into his voice. A tremor sometimes overtook his hand as he gestured for the next person. The air grew thick, the noise constant and pressing. Cattle lowed on the outskirts. Fires for the evening meal were already being kindled, their smoke curling into the yellow sky.

Now and then, a frustrated voice would rise—a complaint about the wait or an argument not yet settled. Children wailed, impatient for resolution. The old among them grew restless, leaning more heavily on their staffs.

Evening drew near. The shadows lengthened and the golden light spread. Still, Moses sat, never breaking from his labor. His face glistened with sweat; his back ached beneath the weight of so many burdens. For every answer he gave, another question lined up behind it.

Jethro waited until the day’s labor concluded. He saw Moses rise at last, his steps slow, his shoulders bent. When Moses returned to his tent, Jethro approached him quietly, offering water for his thirst and sitting across from him. The old man’s face was set with care, his gaze steady.

The next morning, when the hush of dawn returned and the cycle began anew, Jethro came while Moses was seated among the people. He watched the crowd, the constant stream of faces, the sound of dispute mixing with the hum of anxious expectation.

When the first quiet came, Jethro leaned near to Moses. His voice was low but firm. “What is this that you are doing for the people?” Jethro asked. “Why do you sit alone, and all the people stand around you from morning until evening?”

Moses looked at his father-in-law, the strain evident in his eyes. “Because the people come to me to inquire of God,” Moses replied. “When they have a matter, it comes to me; I judge between man and his neighbor, and I make them know the statutes of God and his laws.”

Jethro listened as Moses spoke. The old man’s face grew grave. He shook his head gently. “What you are doing is not good,” Jethro said. “You will surely wear yourself out, both you and these people who are with you. This task is too heavy for you; you cannot do it alone.”

The wind moved through the camp, shifting the dust at their feet. Jethro spoke again, his words clear and filled with wisdom born of years. “Now listen to me. I will give you advice, and God be with you. You represent the people before God, and you bring their causes to him. Teach them the statutes and the laws, and show them the way they must walk and the work that they must do.”

He looked once more to the long lines of waiting people, then back to Moses. “Moreover, you shall choose able men out of all the people—men who fear God, men of truth, those who hate dishonest gain. Place such men over them, to be rulers of thousands, of hundreds, of fifties, and of tens. Let them judge the people at all times. Every great matter they shall bring to you, but every small matter they shall judge themselves. So shall it be easier for you, and they shall share the load with you. If you do this, and God commands you so, then you will be able to endure, and all these people will go to their place in peace.”

Moses listened with care. The lines of weariness on his face softened, and he nodded. He heard wisdom in his father-in-law’s counsel and took it to heart.

Without delay, Moses set to work. As the sun rose on fresh days and the camp roused itself for morning, Moses gathered the elders of the tribes. He sought able men from among the people—men known for their integrity, men who feared God and would not be bribed. He chose them, each for his appointed place: some over thousands, some over hundreds, some over fifties, and some over tens.

They stood before him, men of strength, their faces marked by experience and truth. Moses gave them charge, instructing them in the statutes of God, the laws handed down in the wilderness. He taught them what to judge and what to bring before him. His voice was still tired, but there was relief in its tone—a burden lessened.

Soon, the change was clear in the camp. The people no longer formed a single, endless line. Wherever tents clustered, elders sat in judgment, listening to disputes, deciding matters small and urgent. The crowds thinned. The air grew lighter; the heat less oppressive. Those waiting were no longer as many, and their faces were less strained. Only the gravest matters were brought to Moses, and he spoke with wisdom as before.

The camp settled into its new order. Moses no longer sat from morning until night, worn by the constant stream. There were moments to rest, to pray, to walk among the people without the weight of every quarrel upon his shoulders. The burdens were divided, and peace settled among the tents.

Jethro saw what Moses had done, and he was content. He saw his advice heeded, order restored, and his son-in-law’s strength preserved. The air that once pulsed with exhaustion now stirred quietly with gratitude, the complaints and calls for judgment eased by many hands.

When his time as guest was ended, Jethro made ready to depart. He stood with Moses, the tents and fires of Israel around them, the wilderness stretching away in all directions. There was no need for further words. Jethro’s eyes lingered on his son-in-law. He gathered his things and turned to the road home.

Moses let his father-in-law depart, and he went his way into his own land.
