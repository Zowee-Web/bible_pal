At the edge of the wilderness, the camp of Israel stretched out beneath the open sky. Tents were spread upon the sandy earth as far as the eye could see. The sun climbed steadily, pouring heat upon the people and their goods. Men, women, and children moved in slow lines, their faces marked by dust and expectation.

In the midst of the camp, Moses took his seat. Each morning, as the first light crept beyond the horizon, he sat down to judge the people. Beside him, a stone served as his seat. The people stood nigh, waiting for Moses to settle their questions and disputes. Some held fast unto objects—broken tools or torn cloaks—to lay evidence before him. Others whispered fearfully, glancing at the faces round about them.

As the sun passed overhead, the lines grew longer. The voices of the people mingled with the restless shifting of feet. Children pressed close unto their fathers' robes, while the elders sought to shield their faces from the harsh light. Hours slipped by, the heat pressing down heavily upon all. Moses leaned forward, hearkening intently unto every plea. He answered patiently, striving to shew them the statutes of God and to teach them his laws.

Yet, with every name called, another took its place. The line crept forward but never failed. Moses' shoulders bowed. His voice grew quiet, weariness creeping in as the day wore on. Still, he did not arise. The people found no rest from their troubles, and Moses found little from his duty.

Jethro, Moses' father in law, beheld all this from a place nigh by. He watched as dispute after dispute was brought before Moses. The long lines. The weary faces. The unending questions. Jethro's eyes narrowed with concern. He saw Moses burdened, beset by need on every side.

When evening drew nigh, the sun dipping low, the people at length went unto their tents. Moses sat back, his shoulders heavy. Jethro went unto the place where Moses rested. The cool air of the night drifted betwixt them as Jethro spake.

"What is this thing that thou doest to the people?" he asked. "Why sittest thou thyself alone, and all the people stand by thee from morning unto even?"

Moses answered honestly. "Because the people come unto me to enquire of God. When they have a matter, they come unto me, and I judge betwixt one and another, and I make them know the statutes of God, and his laws."

Jethro hearkened. Then his tone grew firm, touched with the authority of an elder. "The thing that thou doest is not good. Thou wilt surely wear away, both thou, and this people that is with thee: for this thing is too heavy for thee; thou art not able to perform it thyself alone."

He paused, letting the warning sink in. There was nothing harsh in his voice, only the measured rhythm of wisdom given out of care. He drew nigher, lowering his voice as the evening deepened. "Hearken now unto my voice, I will give thee counsel, and God shall be with thee. Be thou for the people to God-ward, that thou mayest bring the causes unto God. Thou shalt teach them ordinances and laws, and shalt shew them the way wherein they must walk, and the work that they must do."

Jethro's words moved with clearness, his eyes unwavering. He laid out a path of relief and strength for Moses. "Moreover thou shalt provide out of all the people able men, such as fear God, men of truth, hating covetousness; and place such over them, to be rulers of thousands, and rulers of hundreds, rulers of fifties, and rulers of tens. And let them judge the people at all seasons. Every great matter they shall bring unto thee, but every small matter they shall judge themselves. So shall it be easier for thee, and they shall bear the burden with thee. If thou shalt do this thing, and God command thee so, then thou shalt be able to endure, and all this people shall also go to their place in peace."

Moses hearkened unto the voice of his father in law. The words pierced through the haze of weariness. Moses perceived the wisdom in that which Jethro had spoken. Relief was mingled with thankfulness as he saw there was another way.

He set about choosing able men out of Israel, even as Jethro had counselled. Out of every tribe, he appointed them as rulers over thousands, hundreds, fifties, and tens. These appointed men stood up in their places. The lines were divided, becoming many shorter lines instead of one unending one. Each man took up some part of the burden—judging daily matters, hearkening unto questions, settling disputes with fairness and reverence.

The sun rose and went down, but the strain began to lift. Moses was yet the leader of the people, but now he could commit the daily matters unto others. The people themselves beheld the difference—a lighter burden upon Moses, but also a swifter answer unto their disputes. The wise words of Jethro sounded in the camp every morning.

From that day, every difficult cause was brought unto Moses. The simpler matters, the daily questions, were judged by the men he had set over Israel. Order spread throughout the camp, and a sense of balance returned. Moses could teach the statutes, guide the people, and seek God, no longer weighed down by every single concern. The camp settled into a new course, and the weariness that had been written upon so many faces softened.

When the time was come, Moses sent his father in law upon his way. And Moses let his father in law depart, and he went his way into his own land.
