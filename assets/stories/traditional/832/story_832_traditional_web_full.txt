At the edge of the wilderness, the camp of Israel stretched out under the open sky. Tents dotted the sandy earth as far as the eye could see. The sun climbed steadily, pouring heat over the people and their belongings. Men, women, and children moved in slow lines, their faces marked by dust and expectation.

In the midst of the camp, Moses took his seat. Each morning, as the first light crept beyond the horizon, he sat down to judge the people. Beside him, a stone served as his seat. The people stood nearby, waiting for Moses to settle their questions and disputes. Some clutched objects—broken tools or torn cloaks—to lay evidence before him. Others whispered anxiously, glancing at the faces around them.

As the sun passed overhead, the lines grew longer. The voices of the people mingled with the restless shifting of feet. Children pressed close to their fathers’ robes, while elders tried to shelter their faces from the harsh light. Hours slipped by, the heat pressing down heavily upon all. Moses leaned forward, listening intently to each plea. He answered patiently, striving to show them God’s statutes and teach them His laws.

Yet, with every name called, another took its place. The line inched forward but never faded. Moses’ shoulders sagged. His voice quieted, weariness creeping in as the day wore on. Still, he did not rise. The people found no rest from their troubles, and Moses found little from his duty.

Jethro, Moses’ father-in-law, observed all this from a place nearby. He watched as dispute after dispute was brought before Moses. The long lines. The tired faces. The unending questions. Jethro’s eyes narrowed with concern. He saw Moses burdened, surrounded by need on every side.

When evening approached, the sun dipping low, the people finally went to their tents. Moses sat back, shoulders heavy. Jethro moved to where Moses rested. The night’s cool air drifted between them as Jethro spoke.

“What is this thing that you are doing for the people?” he asked. “Why do you sit alone, with all the people standing around you from morning to evening?”

Moses answered honestly. “Because the people come to me to inquire of God. When they have a matter, they come to me, and I judge between one and another, and I make them know the statutes of God, and his laws.”

Jethro listened. Then his tone grew firm, touched with the authority of an elder. “What you are doing is not good. You will surely wear away, both you and this people with you. The task is too heavy for you. You cannot do it alone.”

He paused, letting the warning sink in. There was nothing harsh in his voice, only the measured rhythm of wisdom given out of care. He drew closer, lowering his voice as evening deepened. “Listen now to my voice. I will give you counsel, and God be with you. You stand before God for the people, that you may bring the causes to God. Teach them the statutes and the laws, and show them the way in which they must walk, and the work that they must do.”

Jethro’s words moved with clarity, his eyes unwavering. He laid out a path of relief and strength for Moses. “You should choose able men out of all the people—men who fear God, men of truth, hating unjust gain—and set them over the people as rulers of thousands, hundreds, fifties, and tens. Let them judge the people at all times. Every great matter they shall bring to you, but every small matter they shall judge themselves. In this way, it will be easier for you, and they will share the load with you. If you do this thing, and God commands you so, then you will be able to endure, and all this people also will go to their place in peace.”

Moses listened to the counsel of his father-in-law. The words pierced through the haze of exhaustion. Moses understood the wisdom in what Jethro had spoken. Relief mingled with gratitude as he saw there was another way.

He set about choosing able men from among Israel, just as Jethro had advised. From every tribe, he appointed them as rulers over thousands, hundreds, fifties, and tens. These appointed men stood up in their places. The lines divided, becoming many shorter lines instead of one unending one. Each man took up some of the burden—judging daily matters, listening to questions, settling disputes with fairness and reverence.

The sun rose and set, but the strain began to lift. Moses was still the people’s leader, but now he could delegate the daily matters. The people themselves saw the difference—a lighter burden on Moses, but also a swifter answer for their disputes. The wise words of Jethro echoed in the camp each morning.

From this day, every difficult cause was brought to Moses. The simpler matters, the daily questions, were judged by the men he had set over Israel. The order spread across the camp, and a sense of balance returned. Moses could teach the statutes, guide the people, and seek God, no longer weighed down by every single concern. The camp settled into a new rhythm, and the tiredness that had been written on so many faces softened.

When the time came, Moses sent his father-in-law on his way. Moses let his father-in-law depart, and he went his way into his own land.
