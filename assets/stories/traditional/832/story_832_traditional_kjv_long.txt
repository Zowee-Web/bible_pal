In the wilderness, when the dew of morning yet clung unto the tents of Israel, a hush drifted over the camp. The sun arose, burning slowly through thin clouds, its glare stretching across the gathering people. They stood without their shelters, hundreds of men and women, their children at their feet, the dust gathering upon their sandals. It was a worn path, for each new day brought new quarrels and questions. Some had been waiting since the deep hours before dawn, holding fast unto hopes and grievances, longing for judgment from Moses himself.

Moses came forth from his tent at sunrise, his face shadowed with the labour of many days. He passed through the rows of the tribes, his steps steady, but his eyes weary. The people called unto him as he passed, some with gentle voices, others with tense insistence. All of them waited for his word.

Jethro, his father in law, was yet a guest among them. He watched from the door of Moses' tent. The aged Midianite's eyes followed Moses' path as he moved toward his place of judgment. Jethro hearkened unto the hum of the camp, unto the shifting sounds of sandals and murmured voices, unto the cries of infants and the creak of leather under the weight of the day.

The gathering grew as the morning burned toward noon. The sun pressed down, heavy and bright. Moses sat in his seat. The people drew near, each waiting for his turn. Some shaded their eyes with their hands. Others huddled together, dust rising round about their feet. A mother soothed her child as she waited. Elder men leaned upon their staves.

One by one, they came before Moses. They laid out their disputes—matters of property, offences given or taken, questions of debt, misunderstandings concerning the statutes God had given. Some brought fears for their kinsmen; others wounds from the night before. Moses hearkened, leaning forward, his brow furrowed. He judged betwixt friend and neighbour, betwixt brother and stranger.

His voice carried, patient but strained. "Betwixt thee and thy brother, thou shalt restore that which is owed, according to the word of the Lord." Another came with fearful words. Moses hearkened. He searched the law, handed down by the Lord, and gave his answer. The line of people melted forward, new faces for every decision.

Jethro beheld this scene unfold. From dawn until the afternoon shadow, the line never parted. The sun climbed; the camp shimmered in the heat. Moses did not arise from his seat. He spake with kindness, but weariness crept into his voice. A tremor sometimes overtook his hand as he beckoned for the next. The air grew thick, the noise constant and pressing. Cattle lowed on the outskirts. Fires for the evening meal were already being kindled, their smoke curling into the yellow sky.

Now and then, a frustrated voice would rise—a complaint about the waiting or an argument not yet settled. Children wailed, impatient for resolution. The aged among them grew restless, leaning more heavily upon their staves.

Evening drew nigh. The shadows lengthened and the golden light spread abroad. Still, Moses sat, never breaking from his labour. His face glistened with sweat; his back ached beneath the weight of so many burdens. For every answer he gave, another question lined up behind it.

Jethro waited until the day's labour was ended. He saw Moses arise at the last, his steps slow, his shoulders bent. When Moses returned unto his tent, Jethro came nigh quietly, offering water for his thirst and sitting over against him. The old man's face was set with care, his gaze steady.

On the morrow, when the hush of dawn returned and the cycle began anew, Jethro came while Moses sat among the people. He watched the multitude, the constant stream of faces, the sound of dispute mingling with the hum of fearful expectation.

When the first quiet came, Jethro leaned near unto Moses. His voice was low but firm. "What is this thing that thou doest to the people?" Jethro asked. "Why sittest thou thyself alone, and all the people stand by thee from morning unto even?"

Moses looked upon his father in law, the strain plain in his eyes. "Because the people come unto me to enquire of God," Moses answered. "When they have a matter, they come unto me; and I judge betwixt one and another, and I do make them know the statutes of God, and his laws."

Jethro hearkened as Moses spake. The old man's face grew grave. He shook his head gently. "The thing that thou doest is not good," Jethro said. "Thou wilt surely wear away, both thou, and this people that is with thee: for this thing is too heavy for thee; thou art not able to perform it thyself alone."

The wind moved through the camp, shifting the dust at their feet. Jethro spake again, his words clear and filled with wisdom born of years. "Hearken now unto my voice, I will give thee counsel, and God shall be with thee: Be thou for the people to God-ward, that thou mayest bring the causes unto God. And thou shalt teach them ordinances and laws, and shalt shew them the way wherein they must walk, and the work that they must do."

He looked once more unto the long lines of waiting people, then back unto Moses. "Moreover thou shalt provide out of all the people able men, such as fear God, men of truth, hating covetousness; and place such over them, to be rulers of thousands, and rulers of hundreds, rulers of fifties, and rulers of tens. And let them judge the people at all seasons. Every great matter they shall bring unto thee, but every small matter they shall judge themselves. So shall it be easier for thee, and they shall bear the burden with thee. If thou shalt do this thing, and God command thee so, then thou shalt be able to endure, and all this people shall also go to their place in peace."

Moses hearkened with care. The lines of weariness upon his face softened, and he assented. He heard wisdom in his father in law's counsel and took it to heart.

Without delay, Moses set unto the work. As the sun arose on fresh days and the camp roused itself for morning, Moses gathered the elders of the tribes. He sought able men out of the people—men known for their integrity, men who feared God and would not be bribed. He chose them, each for his appointed place: some over thousands, some over hundreds, some over fifties, and some over tens.

They stood before him, men of strength, their faces marked by experience and truth. Moses gave them charge, instructing them in the statutes of God, the laws handed down in the wilderness. He taught them what to judge and what to bring before him. His voice was yet weary, but there was relief in its tone—a burden lessened.

Soon, the change was clear in the camp. The people no longer formed one endless line. Wheresoever tents clustered, elders sat in judgment, hearkening unto disputes, deciding matters small and urgent. The multitudes thinned. The air grew lighter; the heat less oppressive. Those waiting were no longer as many, and their faces were less strained. Only the gravest matters were brought unto Moses, and he spake with wisdom as before.

The camp settled into its new order. Moses no longer sat from morning until night, worn by the constant stream. There were moments to rest, to pray, to walk among the people without the weight of every quarrel upon his shoulders. The burdens were divided, and peace settled among the tents.

Jethro saw that which Moses had done, and he was content. He saw his counsel heeded, order restored, and his son in law's strength preserved. The air that once pulsed with weariness now stirred quietly with thankfulness, the complaints and calls for judgment eased by many hands.

When the time of his sojourn was ended, Jethro made ready to depart. He stood with Moses, the tents and fires of Israel round about them, the wilderness stretching away on every side. There was no need for further words. Jethro's eyes lingered upon his son in law. He gathered his things and turned unto the road home.

And Moses let his father in law depart, and he went his way into his own land.
