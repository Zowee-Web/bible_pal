The king of Syria was making war against Israel. He was careful in his planning. He gathered his servants, his commanders, his most trusted men, and chose the places where his troops would wait in ambush. A road near a particular pass. A valley where Israelite soldiers would need to march. He gave the orders quietly and positioned his forces in secret.

But before the trap could close, a message reached the king of Israel. It came from Elisha, the prophet. "Beware that you do not pass by this place, for the Syrians have come down there."

The king of Israel sent scouts to confirm. It was true. He diverted his men. The ambush failed.

It happened again. The king of Syria chose a new position. A new place. And again the word came from Elisha to the king of Israel, and again the Israelites avoided the trap.

Not once. Not twice. Many times.

The king of Syria was greatly troubled by this. He called his servants before him. His jaw was tight. "Will you not tell me which of us is for the king of Israel?"

Silence in the room. The accusation hung in the air like smoke.

One of his servants spoke. "None, my lord, O king." He paused. "But Elisha, the prophet who is in Israel, tells the king of Israel the words that you speak in your bedroom."

The king's eyes narrowed. A prophet. Not a spy among his own men, but a prophet who heard what could not be heard.

"Go and find where he is," the king said, "that I may send and seize him."

His men searched. The report came back quickly. "He is in Dothan."

Dothan was a small city. The king of Syria did not send a small force. He sent horses and chariots and a great army. They moved at night, their wheels muffled in the dark, their torches unlit. By the time the last stars faded, they had surrounded the city completely. A ring of iron and muscle. Horses standing shoulder to shoulder. Chariots positioned at every road. No one could enter or leave Dothan.

Elisha's servant rose early that morning. The sky was just beginning to pale. He stepped outside to begin the day's work and stopped in the doorway.

Soldiers. Everywhere.

He could see the line of them stretching along the ridge to the east, silhouettes against the brightening sky. Horses stamped and snorted in the cool air. Chariot wheels gleamed. To the north, more. To the south, more. To the west, the road was blocked with armed men standing three ranks deep.

The servant turned and ran back inside. His voice cracked. "Alas, my master! What shall we do?"

Elisha looked at him. His voice did not waver. "Do not be afraid, for those who are with us are more than those who are with them."

The servant's eyes moved from Elisha to the open door. Two of them. Two men in a house. Outside, hundreds of Syrian soldiers, perhaps thousands, armed and waiting.

Then Elisha prayed. He spoke plainly, without raising his voice. "O Lord, please open his eyes that he may see."

The Lord opened the eyes of the young man.

He looked again. The hills around Dothan had not changed shape. The rocks were the same. The scrub brush clung to the slopes as before. But now the mountains were full. Horses and chariots of fire covered the ridgelines, rank upon rank, blazing with a light that was not the sun. They filled every slope around Elisha. The fire did not consume the ground. It held steady, rippling and bright, and the chariots stood ready, and the horses stood still, and the heat of them pressed against the morning air.

The servant stared. His mouth was open. He did not speak.

Below, the Syrian army began to move. Officers called commands. Soldiers formed ranks and advanced toward the city walls.

Elisha walked out to meet them. He did not hurry. When he was close enough that the front line of soldiers could hear him, he stopped and prayed. His words were quiet. "Please strike this people with blindness."

The Lord struck them.

The front rank stumbled. Men reached for their eyes. Horses tossed their heads. The tight formation broke apart as soldiers lost their footing, groping in sudden darkness. Voices rose — confused, sharp, calling for each other. Hands gripped arms. Men bumped into chariots, stumbled over spear shafts dropped in the dust.

Elisha walked toward them. His sandals crunched on the dry ground. He raised his voice so they could hear. "This is not the way, and this is not the city. Follow me, and I will bring you to the man whom you seek."

They had come for Elisha. They did not know they were listening to him now.

They followed. The blind army organized itself slowly, soldiers gripping each other's shoulders, horses led by their bridles. The chariots rolled forward, guided by the sound of the prophet's footsteps. Elisha walked ahead of them on the road, a single man leading an entire army.

The road from Dothan to Samaria was not short. Dust rose in a long pale column behind the procession. The sun climbed higher. The soldiers shuffled forward, unable to see where they were going, trusting the voice ahead of them. Their weapons hung useless at their sides.

They came to Samaria.

The gates of the capital city of Israel stood open. Guards on the walls watched the strange sight below — an army stumbling forward behind one man, their eyes blank, their hands outstretched. Whispers ran along the walls. Soldiers gripped their spears and looked to their officers.

Elisha led the Syrian army through the gates and into the city.

When they stood in the streets of Samaria, surrounded on every side by the walls and the soldiers of Israel, Elisha stopped. He prayed once more. "O Lord, open the eyes of these men, that they may see."

The Lord opened their eyes.

The Syrians blinked. Light flooded back. The first thing they saw was not Dothan. Not the hills. Not the open road. They were standing in a city they did not know. Stone walls rose on every side. Israelite soldiers lined the rooftops, armed and watching. The gates behind them were guarded. They were inside Samaria.

Their hands went to their weapons. But they were surrounded, outnumbered, and trapped inside a fortified city. There was nowhere to go.

The king of Israel came down from the upper city. He saw the Syrian soldiers packed into his streets, blinking and bewildered. His face flushed. He turned to Elisha. The words came fast, eager.

"My father, shall I strike them down? Shall I strike them down?"

He asked it twice. His hand was near his sword.

Elisha answered. "You shall not strike them down. Would you strike down those whom you have taken captive with your sword and with your bow?" He paused. "Set bread and water before them, that they may eat and drink and go to their master."

The king stared at Elisha. Then he turned to his servants and gave the order.

They prepared a great feast. Not just bread and water. Tables were brought out into the streets. Meat was roasted. Bread was stacked high. Jars of water and wine were carried from the storehouses. The smell of food drifted through the open streets of Samaria.

The Syrian soldiers sat down. Their weapons lay beside them on the ground. Israelite servants set plates before them. They ate. They ate until they were full. They drank. No one spoke threats. No one drew a blade. The clatter of plates and the murmur of voices filled the streets where, moments before, an army had stood ready to fight.

When the meal was finished, the Syrian soldiers stood. The king of Israel sent them away. They walked through the gates of Samaria, back down the road they had traveled blind, back to their master the king of Syria.

And the bands of Syria came no more on raids into the land of Israel.
