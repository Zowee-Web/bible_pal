The sound from the upper room had reached the streets of Jerusalem before the morning was full. A noise as of a rushing mighty wind, and then voices — voices speaking out the wonderful works of God in tongues the city did not expect to hear from Galilean mouths.

The multitude came. They came up from the markets and down from the porches of the temple. Parthians and Medes. Elamites. Men from Mesopotamia, from Judaea and Cappadocia, from Pontus and Asia. Men from Phrygia and Pamphylia, from Egypt and the parts of Libya about Cyrene. Strangers of Rome. Cretes. Arabians. They gathered shoulder to shoulder in the square below, faces turned upward, and every man heard, in his own tongue, the speech of God.

They were amazed. They were perplexed. They said one to another, "What meaneth this?"

Others, mocking, said, "These men are full of new wine," and laughter scattered through one corner of the gathering.

Then a man stepped to the front of the upper room. The eleven rose with him and stood at his back. Together they faced the crowd, and Peter lifted up his voice.

"Ye men of Judaea, and all ye that dwell at Jerusalem, be this known unto you, and hearken unto my words. For these are not drunken, as ye suppose, seeing it is but the third hour of the day."

The laughter at the back of the crowd faltered. A man lowered the wineskin he had been gesturing with. The mockers in the corner went quiet, not yet convinced, but listening.

"But this is that which was spoken by the prophet Joel. 'It shall come to pass in the last days, saith God, I will pour out of my Spirit upon all flesh. Your sons and your daughters shall prophesy. Your young men shall see visions. Your old men shall dream dreams. And on my servants and on my handmaidens I will pour out in those days of my Spirit, and they shall prophesy. And I will shew wonders in heaven above, and signs in the earth beneath; blood, and fire, and vapour of smoke. The sun shall be turned into darkness, and the moon into blood, before that great and notable day of the Lord come. And it shall come to pass, that whosoever shall call on the name of the Lord shall be saved.'"

A murmur traveled through the square. Old men turned one to another. Mothers drew their children closer. The mockers in the back had stopped mocking. They had heard the prophet's name spoken — and they had heard their own scriptures fitted, suddenly, to the morning around them.

Peter lifted his voice again, and the murmur fell away.

"Ye men of Israel, hear these words. Jesus of Nazareth, a man approved of God among you by miracles and wonders and signs, which God did by him in the midst of you, as ye yourselves also know — him, being delivered by the determinate counsel and foreknowledge of God, ye have taken, and by wicked hands have crucified and slain. Whom God hath raised up, having loosed the pains of death, because it was not possible that he should be holden of it."

A woman near the front pressed her hand against her mouth. A man beside her had set down what he was carrying without noticing. The square went quieter still.

"For David speaketh concerning him, 'I foresaw the Lord always before my face, for he is on my right hand, that I should not be moved. Therefore did my heart rejoice, and my tongue was glad. Moreover also my flesh shall rest in hope, because thou wilt not leave my soul in hell, neither wilt thou suffer thine Holy One to see corruption. Thou hast made known to me the ways of life. Thou shalt make me full of joy with thy countenance.'"

He paused. The eleven stood with him.

"Men and brethren, let me freely speak unto you of the patriarch David — that he is both dead and buried. His sepulchre is with us unto this day. Therefore being a prophet, and knowing that God had sworn with an oath to him, that of the fruit of his loins, according to the flesh, he would raise up Christ to sit on his throne, he foresaw this. He spake of the resurrection of Christ — that his soul was not left in hell, neither his flesh did see corruption."

The crowd had stopped shifting. Every face in the front rows was lifted. A scribe at the edge of the square had stopped writing.

"This Jesus hath God raised up, whereof we all are witnesses." Peter's voice carried, steady, over the heads of the gathered. The eleven stood unmoved behind him. "Therefore being by the right hand of God exalted, and having received of the Father the promise of the Holy Ghost, he hath shed forth this, which ye now see and hear. For David is not ascended into the heavens, but he saith himself, 'The Lord said unto my Lord, Sit thou on my right hand, until I make thy foes thy footstool.'"

Peter drew a breath. The silence had become its own pressure.

"Therefore let all the house of Israel know assuredly, that God hath made that same Jesus, whom ye have crucified, both Lord and Christ."

The words fell into the square like a stone dropped into still water. The silence that followed was not the silence of confusion. It was the silence of a wound opening.

They were cut to the heart. They were pricked in their hearts. They turned one to another and could not finish their sentences. A craftsman with sawdust still on his sleeves staggered half a step. An old man let his staff sink to the stones. A young mother's eyes filled.

A man near the front lifted his face to Peter and the eleven and called out, his voice catching, "Men and brethren, what shall we do?"

Others took up the cry. It moved outward through the square in rings. "Brethren, what shall we do? What shall we do?" The square trembled with it.

Peter answered them. His voice did not rise. It steadied.

"Repent, and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the remission of sins, and ye shall receive the gift of the Holy Ghost. For the promise is unto you, and to your children, and to all that are afar off, even as many as the Lord our God shall call."

He testified further. With many other words he exhorted them. "Save yourselves," he said, "from this untoward generation."

And they came. They that gladly received his word turned, and they began to move. Through the gates. Down the long stone steps. Unto the pools and the waters without the city. The Galileans came down from the upper room to baptize them, and the eleven moved among the crowd, lifting men and women up out of the water by the wrist, the water running off their tunics into the dust. A scribe whose pen had stopped at the speech now stepped down into the pool himself. A craftsman with sawdust on his sleeves followed. A grandmother held by both arms. A boy not yet bearded.

And there were added in that day about three thousand souls.

By evening the streets of Jerusalem were quieter than they had been in the morning. The shouting was gone. The mockery was gone. In its place, in house after house through the city, a new sound was beginning. The breaking of bread. The naming of Jesus in prayer. The lifting of voices, one in Aramaic, one in Greek, one in Latin, one in tongues older than the temple itself, gathered up into one slow rising hymn. The eleven stood on the steps of the upper room. They looked out across the rooftops, and the city of Jerusalem settled into the cool of its long evening under the slow rising of one new song.
