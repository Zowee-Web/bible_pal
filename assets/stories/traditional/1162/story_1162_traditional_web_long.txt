The sound from the upper room had reached the streets of Jerusalem before the morning was full. A noise like a rushing mighty wind, and then voices — voices speaking out the great works of God in tongues the city did not expect to hear from Galilean mouths.

The multitude came. They came up from the markets and down from the porches of the temple. Parthians and Medes. Elamites. Men from Mesopotamia, from Judea and Cappadocia, from Pontus and Asia. Men from Phrygia and Pamphylia, from Egypt and the parts of Libya about Cyrene. Sojourners from Rome. Cretans. Arabians. They gathered shoulder to shoulder in the square below, faces turned upward, and each one heard, in his own language, the speech of God.

They were amazed. They were perplexed. They said one to another, "What does this mean?"

Others, mocking, said, "They are filled with new wine," and laughter scattered through one corner of the gathering.

Then a man stepped to the front of the upper room. The eleven rose with him and stood at his back. Together they faced the crowd, and Peter lifted up his voice.

"You men of Judea, and all you who dwell at Jerusalem, let this be known to you. Listen to my words. For these are not drunken, as you suppose. It is only the third hour of the day."

The laughter at the back of the crowd faltered. A man lowered the wineskin he had been gesturing with. The mockers in the corner went quiet, not yet convinced, but listening.

"This is what was spoken through the prophet Joel. 'It will be in the last days,' says God, 'that I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh. Your sons and your daughters will prophesy. Your young men will see visions. Your old men will dream dreams. Yes, and on my servants and on my handmaidens in those days I will pour out my Spirit, and they will prophesy. I will show wonders in the sky above, and signs on the earth beneath. Blood, and fire, and billows of smoke. The sun will be turned into darkness, and the moon into blood, before the great and glorious day of the Lord comes. And it will be that whoever will call on the name of the Lord will be saved.'"

A murmur traveled through the square. Old men turned to one another. Mothers drew their children closer. The mockers in the back had stopped mocking. They had heard the prophet's name spoken — and they had heard their own scriptures fitted, suddenly, to the morning around them.

Peter lifted his voice again, and the murmur fell away.

"Men of Israel, hear these words. Jesus of Nazareth, a man approved by God to you by mighty works and wonders and signs which God did by him among you — even as you yourselves know — him, being delivered up by the determined counsel and foreknowledge of God, you have taken by the hand of lawless men, crucified, and killed. Whom God raised up, having freed him from the agony of death, because it was not possible that he should be held by it."

A woman near the front pressed her hand against her mouth. A man beside her had set down what he was carrying without noticing. The square went quieter still.

"For David says concerning him, 'I saw the Lord always before my face. For he is on my right hand, that I should not be moved. Therefore my heart was glad, and my tongue rejoiced. Moreover my flesh also will dwell in hope, because you will not leave my soul in Hades, neither will you allow your Holy One to see decay. You made known to me the ways of life. You will make me full of gladness with your presence.'"

He paused. The eleven stood with him.

"Brothers, I may tell you freely of the patriarch David — that he both died and was buried. His tomb is with us to this day. Being therefore a prophet, and knowing that God had sworn with an oath to him that of the fruit of his body, according to the flesh, he would raise up the Christ to sit on his throne, he foresaw this. He spoke about the resurrection of the Christ — that his soul was not left in Hades, and his flesh did not see decay."

The crowd had stopped shifting. Every face in the front rows was lifted. A scribe at the edge of the square had stopped writing.

"This Jesus God raised up, of which we all are witnesses." Peter's voice carried, steady, over the heads of the gathered. The eleven stood unmoved behind him. "Being therefore exalted by the right hand of God, and having received from the Father the promise of the Holy Spirit, he has poured out this which you now see and hear. For David did not ascend into the heavens, but he says himself, 'The Lord said to my Lord, "Sit by my right hand, until I make your enemies a footstool for your feet."'"

Peter drew a breath. The silence had become its own pressure.

"Let all the house of Israel therefore know assuredly that God has made him both Lord and Christ — this Jesus whom you crucified."

The words fell into the square like a stone dropped into still water. The silence that followed was not the silence of confusion. It was the silence of a wound opening.

They were cut to the heart. They were pricked in their hearts. They turned to one another and could not finish their sentences. A craftsman with sawdust still on his sleeves staggered half a step. An old man let his staff sink to the stones. A young mother's eyes filled.

A man near the front lifted his face to Peter and the eleven and called out, his voice catching, "Brothers — what shall we do?"

Others took up the cry. It moved outward through the square in rings. "Brothers, what shall we do? What shall we do?" The square trembled with it.

Peter answered them. His voice did not rise. It steadied.

"Repent, and be baptized, every one of you, in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of sins, and you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit. For the promise is to you, and to your children, and to all who are far off — even as many as the Lord our God will call to himself."

He testified further. With many other words he exhorted them. "Save yourselves," he said, "from this crooked generation."

And they came. Those who gladly received his word turned, and they began to move. Through the gates. Down the long stone steps. To the pools and the waters outside the city. The Galileans came down from the upper room to baptize them, and the eleven moved among the crowd, lifting men and women up out of the water by the wrist, the water running off their tunics into the dust. A scribe whose pen had stopped at the speech now stepped down into the pool himself. A craftsman with sawdust on his sleeves followed. A grandmother held by both arms. A boy not yet bearded.

There were added in that day about three thousand souls.

By evening the streets of Jerusalem were quieter than they had been in the morning. The shouting was gone. The mockery was gone. In its place, in house after house through the city, a new sound was beginning. The breaking of bread. The naming of Jesus in prayer. The lifting of voices, one in Aramaic, one in Greek, one in Latin, one in tongues older than the temple itself, gathered up into one slow rising hymn. The eleven stood on the steps of the upper room. They looked out across the rooftops, and the city of Jerusalem settled into the cool of its long evening under the slow rising of one new song.
