Peter began not with the trials of his hearers but with blessing. He blessed the God and Father of the Lord Jesus Christ. According to God's great mercy, believers had been born again into a living hope. The beginning of that hope was the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead.

The hope reached toward an inheritance. Peter described it by what cannot happen to it: it cannot decay, it cannot be defiled, and it does not fade away. That inheritance is reserved in heaven for them. Those who await it are themselves guarded by God's power through faith. The guarding looks toward a salvation already prepared to be revealed in the last time.

Within this promise Peter spoke of rejoicing. He also spoke plainly of grief. For a little while, if necessary, the believers had been grieved by various trials. Peter supplied no names, locations, or histories for those trials. He did not say that grief had ceased to be grief. Instead, he placed the trials beside the proving of their faith.

Gold can perish even though it is tested by fire. Faith, Peter said, is more precious than that gold. Its proof will be found to result in praise, glory, and honor at the revelation of Jesus Christ. The trial therefore did not receive the final word; Peter directed attention to what would be found when Christ is revealed.

Christ remained unseen by the people addressed. They had not known him, yet they loved him. They did not see him now, yet they believed in him. Peter joined that believing to great rejoicing: joy beyond speech and filled with glory. Even as they believed without sight, they were receiving the result of faith, the salvation of their souls.

Then Peter widened the testimony only as far as the prophets who had already spoken of this grace. The salvation announced to the believers had been the object of diligent inquiry. Prophets had prophesied about the grace that would come to them, and those same prophets searched carefully concerning it.

Their search had a particular question. The Spirit of Christ within them had pointed beforehand to the sufferings of Christ and to the glories that would follow those sufferings. The prophets sought to know whom the Spirit indicated, or what kind of time was being shown. Peter did not claim that they possessed a complete account of every circumstance. Their inquiry remained inquiry, directed by what the Spirit had testified in advance.

Something had been revealed to them: their service in these matters was not for themselves, but for those who would later hear the announcement. The earlier prophecy and the present proclamation belonged to one movement of testimony, but Peter kept their places distinct. The prophets searched and served; others now preached the Good News.

Those heralds announced the message by the Holy Spirit sent from heaven. What the prophets had foretold concerning Christ's sufferings and following glories had now been reported in the Good News. Peter closed this movement by looking beyond human hearers and human messengers. These are things into which angels desire to look.

The blessing therefore moved from God's mercy to Christ's resurrection, from resurrection to living hope, and from hope to an inheritance kept in heaven. It acknowledged temporary grief without inventing its shape. It described faith under trial and the honor awaiting Christ's revelation. It spoke of love and belief directed toward the unseen Christ, and of salvation being received. Finally, it remembered prophets who searched, servants who preached by the Holy Spirit, and angels who desire to look. The hope remained alive at every point because Peter had anchored it in the risen Jesus Christ.
