The word of the Lord came unto Abram in Haran. It came without prelude, without negotiation, without the comfort of context. The instruction was blunt and total. Get thee out of thy country. Leave thy kindred. Depart from thy father's house. Go unto a land that I will shew thee.

Haran was a city of merchants and herdsmen, a crossroads upon the trade routes of the ancient world. Abram had dwelt there since his father Terah had settled the family in that place, after departing from Ur of the Chaldees. The streets smelled of roasting grain and the hides of beasts. The wells were deep and sure. The neighbours spoke his tongue, knew his name, remembered his father. To leave Haran was to leave all that made a life known unto a man.

No map was given him. No name for the place appointed. Only a promise, spoken with an authority that left no room for bargaining: I will make of thee a great nation. I will bless thee, and make thy name great. Thou shalt be a blessing. I will bless them that bless thee, and curse him that curseth thee. In thee shall all families of the earth be blessed.

The promise was immense. It stretched beyond anything Abram could verify or prove. A great nation — from a man of five and seventy with a barren wife. A great name — for a herder with no throne. A blessing to all families of the earth — spoken unto one family, in one city, upon one ordinary day.

Abram was five and seventy years old when he departed out of Haran. He did not argue. He did not delay. The record keepeth no account of hesitation, no council of elders consulted, no season of deliberation. The Lord spake. Abram went forth. As the Lord had spoken unto him, so he departed.

He took Sarai his wife. She was his companion in this uprooting — a woman who had known no children, who now walked away from the place wherein she had built what life she had. He took Lot, his brother's son, the young man who had become his charge after the death of Lot's father. He gathered all their substance — the flocks of sheep and goats, the herds of cattle, the stores of grain and oil and dried fruit, the tents and tent poles, the vessels for cooking, the skins for water. He gathered the souls they had gotten in Haran — servants, workers, those who had joined themselves unto their household. It was no small company. The caravan stretched along the road, a moving column of dust and noise and purpose.

They went forth to go into the land of Canaan.

The road led south and west, through open country where the grass grew thin and the soil turned pale. The asses walked with their heads low, their burdens swaying with each step. Goats scattered unto the edges of the path and had to be driven back. The sun beat down. Water was rationed between the wells. Sarai walked beside the lead animals, her head covered against the glare, her sandals leaving prints in the fine dust. Lot rode near the back of the company, keeping watch over the cattle that trailed behind. Abram led.

Day upon day. Stage upon stage. The land changed slowly — from the green irrigated fields near Haran, through dry scrubland, over rocky passes where the wind funnelled between the hills and the cold came sharply at night. They kindled fires in the evenings and slept in tents pitched upon uneven ground. The stars were thick overhead, and the silence was vast.

They came into the land of Canaan.

It was a land of hills and valleys, of thick groves of oak and open plains. Streams ran through some of the lowlands, and wild flowers grew in patches upon the slopes. But it was not empty. The Canaanite was then in the land — settled and established, tilling the soil and grazing the pastures. Abram and his company were strangers there. They had no claim, no allies, no standing among the people.

Abram passed through the land unto the place of Sichem, unto the plain of Moreh. The oaks were ancient, their trunks broad and deeply furrowed, their branches spreading wide and casting shade upon the stony ground. It was a place of mark, known unto travellers. Abram halted there. He stood among the trees in a land that was not his.

The Lord appeared unto Abram. The record saith not how — whether in vision, or in voice, or in some manner the narrative doth not describe. But the Lord appeared, and he spake. "Unto thy seed will I give this land."

This land. The land of the Canaanite. The land wherein Abram stood as a sojourner, without title or deed, without offspring to inherit anything. This land — unto descendants who did not yet draw breath, from a wife who had borne no child.

Abram builded an altar unto the Lord, who appeared unto him. He gathered stones from the ground and set them one upon another. It was not a grand structure. It was a cairn, a rough heap of fieldstones raised in a foreign place. No priest attended. No congregation looked on. No incense was burned. Only a man, old and far from home, raising a memorial to mark the place where God had spoken.

He removed from thence unto a mountain on the east of Bethel. The ground rose and the air grew cooler. He pitched his tent upon the high ground, having Bethel on the west and Hai on the east. From that height he could look upon the land spreading in both directions — the rolling hills westward, the slopes descending toward the plain of Jordan to the east. The wind came across the ridge and drew the tent cloth taut against its poles.

There Abram builded another altar unto the Lord. Again the stones. Again the marking of a place. And there he called upon the name of the Lord. He spake into the open air, upon a hilltop between two cities that were not his, and the sound of his voice carried over the empty country.

Two altars now stood in the land of Canaan, builded by a man who owned nothing therein. Two markers set in stone, declaring that the God who had called him out of Haran had spoken again in this new place. They were not monuments unto the courage or faith of Abram. They were monuments unto the voice that had spoken and the promise that had been made.

Then Abram journeyed, going on still toward the south. He moved by stages toward the Negev, the dry land where the earth turned to dust and the horizon wavered in the heat. Each stage was a camp broken and raised again, a fire kindled and put out, a night beneath strange stars. The caravan moved steadily, the beasts plodding, the people walking, the goods swaying upon the backs of asses.

Behind him lay Haran, the city of his father, the place unto which he would not return. Behind him lay the oaks of Sichem and the ridge between Bethel and Hai, where the altars stood open to the weather. Before him lay the south country, and beyond it, whatsoever the Lord had prepared.

Abram walked forward into it. Five and seventy years old. No children. No deed to the land. No surety save the word that had been spoken. The dust rose behind him and settled slowly in the still air, and the road stretched on.
