The word of the Lord came to Abram in Haran. It arrived without warning, without prelude, without the comfort of explanation. Leave your country. Leave your relatives. Leave your father's house. Go to the land that I will show you.

No map was given. No name for the destination. The Lord did not say how far, or how long, or what the road would cost. He gave only a promise, layered and vast: I will make of you a great nation. I will bless you and make your name great. You will be a blessing. I will bless those who bless you and curse the one who treats you with contempt. In you all the families of the earth will be blessed.

Abram was seventy-five years old. He had lived in Haran with his family, among people he knew. The streets were familiar. The wells were mapped in his memory. He knew which merchants set their stalls near the gate at dawn, which flocks grazed the hills east of town, which neighbors would nod as he passed. His father's house was there. His kin were there. Everything he had built or earned or gathered lay within the reach of a morning's walk.

And the Lord said: go.

So Abram went. As the Lord had spoken to him, he went.

He told Sarai. He told Lot. The household began to move. Servants took down the tents and folded them into bundles. Sarai wrapped jars of oil and grain in heavy cloth. Lot counted the livestock — sheep, goats, the few cattle they owned. Donkeys were loaded with rolled leather and stacked provisions. Ropes were checked and tightened. The last of the stores were divided and packed.

Abram took Sarai his wife and Lot his brother's son. He gathered their possessions and the people they had acquired in Haran. They set out for the land of Canaan.

The road was dry and long. Dust rose behind the caravan in a low brown cloud that did not settle. The sun climbed and pressed down on the open ground. The air tasted of dirt and dry grass. Sarai walked beside the lead donkey, her head covered against the glare, her sandals raising small puffs of dust with every step. Lot rode at the rear, watching the livestock stretch into a slow uneven line behind them. A servant called out to a stray goat. A child sitting atop a bundle of tents shielded her eyes and looked ahead at nothing but more road.

Abram led them forward. He did not know the way. He had no landmark to steer by, no friend waiting at the other end. He had a word from God. He walked toward it.

They came into the land of Canaan. The terrain changed — rocky hillsides covered in low scrub, valleys where wild grasses bent in the wind, ridgelines of pale stone. Abram passed through the land to the place of Shechem, to the oak of Moreh. It was a broad tree, old and thick-trunked, standing alone in the open ground. The Canaanite was in the land then. Their settlements dotted the hills. Their language filled the marketplaces. Their altars stood beside the roads, built to gods Abram did not know.

This was not his country. These were not his people.

The Lord appeared to Abram there. He said, "I will give this land to your offspring."

The land around him — the hills, the oak, the valleys stretching south — all of it promised to a future Abram could not yet see. He had no offspring. Sarai had borne no children. The promise reached past the present into something that did not yet exist.

Abram built an altar to the Lord who had appeared to him. He gathered stones from the ground, rough and uncut, and stacked them one upon another. The work was simple. No priest attended. No congregation watched. Just a man kneeling in the dirt of a foreign place, setting stone on stone in answer to a voice only he had heard.

From there he moved on. He traveled to the mountain east of Bethel and pitched his tent, with Bethel on the west and Ai on the east. The ground was hard and stony. The wind came down off the hills and pressed the tent fabric flat against its poles. At night the air turned cold, and the stars spread thick across the sky.

There Abram built another altar to the Lord and called on the name of the Lord. A second altar. A second response. The same God, the same promise, marked again in stone on foreign ground.

Then Abram journeyed on, stage by stage, moving steadily toward the Negev. The desert stretched before him — brown, wide, shimmering under the heat. Behind him lay two altars in a land not yet his own. Ahead the road offered nothing certain. He walked between the promise already spoken and the fulfillment not yet given, an old man in a new land, carrying the word of God and the dust of a long road beneath his feet.
