Night had fallen over the Jabbok River. The water moved quickly over smooth stones, making a steady rushing sound that filled the darkness. Trees grew along the banks, their branches black against the stars. The air was cool and damp, carrying the smell of wet earth and river grass.

Jacob stood on the bank. He had just sent everyone across — his two wives, Leah and Rachel; his two servants, Zilpah and Bilhah; and his eleven children. One by one, they had waded through the ford in the dark water, bundles balanced on their heads, children held close. Jacob had watched them go. He had sent everything he owned across the river.

Then he was alone.

The night pressed in around him. No voices. No campfire. Just the river and the stars and the sound of his own breathing. Jacob was about to meet his brother Esau the next day. The last time they had seen each other, Esau had been angry enough to kill him. Jacob did not know what tomorrow would bring.

He stood there in the dark. Waiting. Thinking.

Then a man appeared. No warning. No footsteps. One moment Jacob was alone, and the next, someone grabbed him.

They wrestled. Jacob and the man locked together, arms gripping shoulders, feet pushing against the soft ground near the river. Jacob was strong. He had spent years working in the fields, lifting stones, carrying sheep. He pushed back hard. The man pushed harder.

They struggled through the darkness. Hour after hour. Their feet churned the mud on the riverbank. Their breathing came in rough, ragged gasps. Sweat ran down Jacob's face. His muscles burned. But he held on. He would not let go.

The stars moved across the sky. The night wore on. Neither one won. Neither one fell.

When the man saw that he could not overpower Jacob, he reached out and touched Jacob's hip socket. Just a touch. And the hip came out of joint with a jolt of sharp pain. Jacob gasped. His leg buckled under him. But even with his hip wrenched out of place, even with pain shooting through his whole side, Jacob held on. His fingers gripped the man's clothing. His arms wrapped tight.

The first pale light of dawn appeared along the edge of the sky. The man spoke. "Let me go, for the day is breaking."

Jacob's voice came out strained and fierce. "I will not let you go unless you bless me."

Even now — hurt, exhausted, barely able to stand — Jacob wanted a blessing. He wanted it more than sleep, more than rest, more than the end of pain. He held on with everything he had left.

The man asked, "What is your name?"

"Jacob," he answered.

Then the man said something Jacob would never forget. "Your name will no longer be called Jacob, but Israel. For you have struggled with God and with men, and you have prevailed."

Israel. The name meant "he struggles with God." Jacob — the man who had held on through a whole dark night of wrestling — now had a new name.

Jacob asked, still holding on, "Please tell me your name."

The man answered, "Why is it that you ask my name?" He did not give it. Instead, he blessed Jacob. Right there on the riverbank, in the gray light before sunrise, with the water rushing past and the sky slowly brightening, he blessed him.

Then the man was gone.

Jacob stood alone again. The dawn light spread across the river. His hip throbbed with every heartbeat. He took a step and winced. He was limping. He would limp from this day forward.

But Jacob understood something now. He looked at the river and the sky and the place where he had wrestled all night long. "I have seen God face to face," he said, "and yet my life has been delivered."

He named the place Peniel, which means "face of God."

The sun rose above the horizon, warm and golden, spreading light across the water. Jacob crossed the Jabbok, limping. Every step hurt. But he carried something with him that was worth more than a painless hip — a blessing, and a new name given to him by God in the darkest part of the night.
