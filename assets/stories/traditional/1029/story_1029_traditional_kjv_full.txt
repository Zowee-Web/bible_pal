Night had fallen over the river Jabbok. The water moved swiftly over smooth stones, making a steady rushing sound that filled the darkness. Trees grew along the banks, their branches black against the stars. The air was cool and damp, bearing the savour of wet earth and river grass.

Jacob stood upon the bank. He had but newly sent every one over — his two wives, Leah and Rachel; his two handmaids, Zilpah and Bilhah; and his eleven children. One by one, they had passed through the ford in the dark water, bundles balanced upon their heads, children held close. Jacob beheld them as they went. He had sent every thing he had over the river.

Then was he left alone.

The night pressed in around him. No voices. No campfire. Only the river and the stars and the sound of his own breathing. Jacob was to meet his brother Esau on the morrow. The last time they had seen one another, Esau had been wroth enough to slay him. Jacob knew not what the morrow would bring forth.

He stood there in the dark. Waiting. Thinking.

And behold, there came a man. No warning. No footsteps. One moment Jacob was alone, and the next, someone laid hold upon him.

They wrestled. Jacob and the man locked together, arms gripping shoulders, feet pushing against the soft ground near the river. Jacob was strong. He had spent years labouring in the fields, lifting stones, carrying sheep. He pushed back hard. The man pushed harder.

They strove together through the darkness. Hour after hour. Their feet churned the mud upon the riverbank. Their breathing came in rough, ragged gasps. The sweat ran down Jacob's face. His sinews burned. But he held on. He would not let go.

The stars moved across the sky. The night wore on. Neither one prevailed. Neither one fell.

When the man saw that he prevailed not against Jacob, he put forth his hand and touched the hollow of Jacob's thigh. Only a touch. And the thigh was put out of joint with a jolt of sharp pain. Jacob gasped. His leg buckled under him. But even with his hip wrenched out of place, even with pain shooting through his whole side, Jacob held on. His fingers gripped the man's garments. His arms wrapped tight.

The first pale light of dawn appeared along the edge of the sky. The man spake. "Let me go, for the day breaketh."

Jacob's voice came forth strained and fierce. "I will not let thee go, except thou bless me."

Even now — hurt, weary, scarce able to stand — Jacob would have a blessing. He desired it more than sleep, more than rest, more than the ending of pain. He held on with all that was left in him.

The man asked, "What is thy name?"

"Jacob," he answered.

Then the man said somewhat Jacob would never forget. "Thy name shall be called no more Jacob, but Israel. For thou hast had power with God and with men, and hast prevailed."

Israel. The name meant "he striveth with God." Jacob — the man who had held on through a whole dark night of wrestling — now had a new name.

Jacob asked, still holding on, "Tell me, I pray thee, thy name."

The man answered, "Wherefore is it that thou dost ask after my name?" He told it not. But he blessed Jacob. Right there upon the riverbank, in the grey light before sunrise, with the water rushing past and the sky slowly brightening, he blessed him.

Then the man was gone.

Jacob stood alone again. The dawn light spread across the river. His hip throbbed with every heartbeat. He took a step and winced. He was halting upon his thigh. He would halt thereon from that day forward.

But Jacob understood somewhat now. He looked upon the river and the sky and the place where he had wrestled all night long. "I have seen God face to face," he said, "and my life is preserved."

He called the name of the place Peniel, which meaneth "the face of God."

The sun arose above the horizon, warm and golden, spreading light across the water. Jacob passed over the Jabbok, halting upon his thigh. Every step hurt. But he carried somewhat with him that was worth more than a painless hip — a blessing, and a new name given him by God in the darkest part of the night.
