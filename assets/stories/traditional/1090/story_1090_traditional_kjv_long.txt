The Sea of Galilee lay calm in the late afternoon light. The water was flat and silver, reflecting the brown hills that rose upon either side. Near the shore, fishing vessels rocked gently at their moorings, their hulls dark with pitch, their nets spread over the gunwales to dry. The air smelled of fish and wet rope and warm stone. Children ran along the water's edge, casting pebbles and watching them skip. A few women sat upon the rocks hard by, mending nets with quick and practised hands.

Jesus and his disciples came down unto the shore. He had been teaching the whole day — multitudes pressing close, voices calling out, hands reaching toward him. He was weary. His face shewed it. His step was slower than it had been at the morning.

They entered into a ship — a broad, flat-bottomed fishing vessel, the sort common upon the lake. The wood was smooth and dark from years of use. The disciples took the oars and pushed off from the shore. The town fell behind them. The water opened.

Jesus went unto the hinder part of the ship. A pillow lay against the wooden frame — a rough thing, sacking stuffed with straw. He laid his head upon it, drew his cloak about his shoulders, and closed his eyes. In a moment he was asleep. His breathing slowed. His body settled into the rocking of the vessel. The water lapped softly against the hull.

The disciples rowed. The sun moved lower. The sky turned from pale blue to gold to a deep and darkening grey. The hills upon each side cast long shadows across the water. The air cooled. A few of the disciples spake softly among themselves. Others watched the far shore draw slowly nearer.

Then the wind changed.

It came off the eastern hills — sudden and fierce, dropping down the slopes and smiting the surface of the lake as a fist. The still water was shattered. Waves rose from nothing — short, steep, crashing against the side of the ship in swift succession. Spray flew across the bow. The vessel lurched sideways, then pitched forward, the prow digging into a wave and rising streaming with water.

The disciples laid hold upon the oars, the ropes, the edges of the ship. The wind howled. The waves grew higher. Water poured in over the sides — not spray but solid sheets of lake water, flooding the floor. The hull groaned. The mast, if there were one, bent and shuddered. The sky was near as black as night. Lightning flickered in the distance, and the hills were gone — hid behind walls of rain and churning water.

The disciples bailed with their hands, with vessels, with aught they could seize. Their garments were soaked through. Their sandals slipped upon the wet timber. They were fishermen — men who knew the lake, who had grown upon its shores, who had endured storms ere now. And they were afraid.

And Jesus was asleep in the stern.

He lay upon the pillow, his cloak wrapped about him, his countenance peaceful and still. The ship pitched and rolled beneath him. Water broke over his body. The wind screamed. And he slept on.

The disciples stumbled toward the stern, gripping the sides lest they be cast overboard. They reached him. They laid hands upon his shoulder. "Lord!" they cried above the tempest. "Save us: we perish!"

Jesus opened his eyes. He looked once about him. Water dripped from the timbers above. The wind tore at his hair. He rose slowly, then stood — bracing himself against the rail, his hand steady upon the wood.

He looked out upon the storm. He beheld the waves — black and capped with white, breaking against the hull. He beheld the sky — dark, churning, heavy with rain. He beheld the spray flying through the air, and the water upon the floor, and the faces of his disciples — pale, wet, wide of eye, trembling.

He rebuked the winds and the sea.

"Peace," said he. "Be still."

And all ceased.

The wind died. The waves were laid flat. The surface of the lake was made smooth, as though a great hand had been set upon it, pressing the water down until it became as glass. The ship steadied. The ropes went slack. The groaning of the hull was silenced. The rain ceased. The sky began to clear at its edges.

The silence was complete.

The disciples sat where they were — soaked through, breathing hard, their hands yet clutching the sides of the vessel. Water dripped from the oars. A fish flopped once upon the wet floor and was still. No man spake.

Jesus turned unto them. His voice was not raised. It was not wrathful. But it carried across the silent ship with the same authority that had stilled the tempest.

"Why are ye fearful?" he said. "O ye of little faith?"

No man answered. They looked upon him. They looked upon the water — flat, calm, impossibly still. They looked one upon another.

A great fear fell upon them. Not the fear they had felt in the storm — not the fear of drowning, not the fear of wind and wave. This was another thing. Something older. Something that went deeper than the body and the bone.

"What manner of man is this," they whispered one to another, "that even the winds and the sea obey him?"

The ship drifted upon the quiet water. The far shore was now visible, dark against the clearing sky. The disciples took up the oars and began to row — slowly, in silence — toward the land. Jesus settled again into the stern. The pillow was wet and his garments were soaked, but his countenance was calm. The lake was still. The wind was gone. And the only sound was the dipping of the oars and the soft dripping of water from the hull.
