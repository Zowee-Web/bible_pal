Long ago, a man named David sat down to write a song to God. It was not a song for trumpets or for crowds of people clapping. It was not for marching or for celebrating after a battle. It was a quiet kind of song. The kind a person sings when they are alone and the world is still around them.

David picked up the words carefully, one by one, the way a person picks up small stones from a riverbed.

He began.

"O Lord, you have searched me," David sang. "And you know me."

That was how the song started. God had searched David. God had looked through every part of him — not the way a person glances at something and moves on, but deeply, completely.

David sang that God knew when he sat down and when he stood up. Every single time. It did not matter where David was. Whether he sat on a flat rock in a dry field, or on a wooden bench in a quiet room, or on the dusty ground by a fire at night — God saw it. And when David stood, whether he rose at first light when the sky turned pale orange, or in the dark hours when the stars were still bright overhead, God noticed that too.

God understood David's thoughts from far away. David did not need to speak his thoughts out loud. He did not need to explain them. God already knew.

David sang that God was familiar with all his ways. His going out and his lying down. Every road David's sandals walked upon — the rocky paths, the dirt trails, the wide roads between towns — God knew each one. Every place David rested his body at night, every spot where he lay his head on the ground or on a folded cloth, God saw.

Then David sang something that made him shake his head in wonder. Before a word was even on his tongue — before David opened his mouth to speak, before the air moved past his teeth to make a sound — God already knew the whole thing. The complete sentence. Every word of it.

"You hem me in," David sang, "behind and before. And you lay your hand upon me."

Behind him and in front of him. Like a wall of knowing on every side. And God's hand resting on him — not heavy, not harsh, just there.

David sang that this knowledge was too wonderful for him. He could not understand it fully. It was too high. He could not reach up and take hold of it. It stayed above him like the highest clouds.

Then David asked a question in his song.

"Where can I go from your Spirit?"

He let the question sit in the air for a moment.

"Where can I flee from your presence?"

And then David answered his own question, one place at a time.

If he went up to the heavens — past the birds, past the clouds, past the highest point a person could imagine — God was there. Already there. Waiting.

If he made his bed in the depths, in the lowest and darkest place below the earth where no light reached and no voice carried, God was there too.

David kept going. He sang about the wings of the dawn. If he could somehow rise up on the very first rays of morning light as they stretched across the sky, and if those wings carried him all the way to the far side of the sea — the farthest place David could picture, the edge of the world — even there, God's hand would hold him. God's right hand would guide him forward.

There was nowhere to go. No direction. No distance. God was already in every place.

Then David sang about darkness. He imagined the thickest, blackest night he had ever known. A night when the moon hid and the stars blinked out and everything went so dark that a person could not see their own hand in front of their face.

David sang, "If I say, 'Surely the darkness will hide me and the light become night around me' — even the darkness will not be dark to you."

The night itself shone like the daytime to God. Darkness and light were the same to him. A person could wrap themselves in shadow like a thick blanket, and God would see right through it. There was no hiding.

Then David's song shifted. The words became slower, more careful. He was not singing about where God was anymore. He was singing about what God had done.

God had created his innermost parts. The pieces of David that no one could see from the outside — his organs, his veins, the hidden workings of his body — God had made those. God had knit David together inside his mother's body. Carefully, closely, piece by piece, the way a person works yarn on a loom, pulling each thread tight, weaving it into the one beside it until something whole takes shape.

"I praise you," David sang, "because I am fearfully and wonderfully made."

He sang that God's works were wonderful. David knew this. His bones had not been hidden from God when he was being made in that secret place. When he was being woven together in the dark, deep inside his mother, God saw every part of it. God watched every bone take its shape. God watched every muscle wrap around it. God watched skin stretch over all of it like cloth pulled smooth over a frame.

God's eyes had seen David when he was still unfinished. Before David had arms or legs that moved. Before his eyes could open. Before he looked like anything a person would recognize. God was there, watching him grow, day by day, piece by piece.

Then David sang something very large. He sang that all of his days — every single one, from the first breath to the last — were written in God's book before even one of them came to be. A book with all his days already on the pages. Mornings he had not yet woken to. Afternoons he had not yet walked through. Evenings he had not yet seen the sun set on. All written down. All known.

David's song turned one more time. His voice grew warm. He sang about God's thoughts.

"How precious to me are your thoughts, O God," he sang. "How vast is the sum of them."

If David tried to count God's thoughts toward him, there would be more than the grains of sand. Every beach he had ever seen, the sand stretching wide and golden under the sun, millions and millions of tiny grains. Every desert, where the sand blew in waves as far as the horizon. Every riverbed, where fine grit settled between the smooth stones. Every handful of dirt that crumbled into dust between his fingers. All the sand in all the world, in every place — and God's thoughts toward David outnumbered even that.

More than he could count. More than he could hold. More than a lifetime of counting could finish.

"When I awake," David sang, "I am still with you."

The song was done. The air around David was still. His lips were closed. His hands rested open in his lap, palms up, fingers loose. Somewhere, sand shifted on a distant shore, grain against grain, too many to number, catching the light.
