Long ago, a man named David sat down to write a song unto God. It was not a song for trumpets nor for multitudes clapping their hands. It was not for marching nor for rejoicing after a battle. It was a quiet kind of song. The kind a man singeth when he is alone and the world is still about him.

David took up the words carefully, one by one, the way a man taketh up small stones from a brook.

He began.

"O Lord, thou hast searched me," David sang. "And known me."

That was how the song began. The Lord had searched David. The Lord had looked through every part of him — not as a man glanceth at a thing and passeth on, but deeply, fully.

David sang that the Lord knew his downsitting and his uprising. Every single time. It mattered not where David was. Whether he sat upon a flat rock in a dry field, or upon a wooden bench in a quiet chamber, or upon the dusty ground beside a fire by night — the Lord beheld it. And when David arose, whether he rose at first light when the sky turned pale orange, or in the dark hours when the stars yet shone bright overhead, the Lord took notice of that also.

The Lord understood David's thoughts afar off. David needed not to speak his thoughts aloud. He needed not to explain them. The Lord already knew.

David sang that the Lord was acquainted with all his ways. His going out and his lying down. Every road his sandals trod upon — the rocky paths, the dirt trails, the wide roads betwixt towns — the Lord knew each one. Every place David laid his body by night, every spot where he laid his head upon the ground or upon a folded cloth, the Lord beheld.

Then David sang somewhat that made him shake his head in wonder. For there was not a word in his tongue — before David opened his mouth to speak, before the breath passed his teeth to frame a sound — but lo, the Lord knew it altogether. The whole saying. Every word of it.

"Thou hast beset me," David sang, "behind and before. And laid thine hand upon me."

Behind him and before him. Like a wall of knowing on every side. And the Lord's hand resting upon him — not heavy, not harsh, just there.

David sang that such knowledge was too wonderful for him. He could not understand it fully. It was high. He could not reach up and lay hold upon it. It stayed above him like the highest clouds.

Then David asked a question in his song.

"Whither shall I go from thy spirit?"

He let the question hang in the air a moment.

"Or whither shall I flee from thy presence?"

And then David answered his own question, one place at a time.

If he ascended up into heaven — past the fowls of the air, past the clouds, past the highest point a man could imagine — the Lord was there. Already there. Waiting.

If he made his bed in hell, in the lowest and darkest place beneath the earth where no light reached and no voice carried, the Lord was there also.

David went on. He sang of the wings of the morning. If he could somehow rise up upon the very first rays of morning light as they stretched across the sky, and if those wings should bear him all the way to the uttermost parts of the sea — the farthest place David could picture, the edge of the world — even there should the Lord's hand lead him. The Lord's right hand should hold him.

There was no whither to go. No direction. No distance. The Lord was already in every place.

Then David sang of darkness. He imagined the thickest, blackest night he had ever known. A night when the moon hid herself and the stars went out and all became so dark that a man could not see his own hand before his face.

David sang, "If I say, Surely the darkness shall cover me; even the night shall be light about me. Yea, the darkness hideth not from thee."

The night itself shineth as the day unto the Lord. The darkness and the light were both alike unto him. A man could wrap himself in shadow like a thick mantle, and the Lord would see through it as through linen. There was no hiding.

Then David's song shifted. The words came slower, more careful. He was not singing of where the Lord was anymore. He was singing of what the Lord had done.

The Lord had possessed his reins. The hidden parts of David that no man could behold from without — his inward parts, his veins, the secret workings of his body — the Lord had made those. The Lord had covered him in his mother's womb. Carefully, closely, piece by piece, as a man worketh yarn upon a loom, drawing each thread tight, weaving it into the one beside it until something whole taketh shape.

"I will praise thee," David sang, "for I am fearfully and wonderfully made."

He sang that the Lord's works were marvellous. David knew this in his soul. His substance was not hid from the Lord when he was made in secret. When he was curiously wrought in the lowest parts of the earth, the Lord beheld every part of it. The Lord watched every bone take its shape. The Lord watched every sinew wrap about it. The Lord watched skin stretched over all of it like cloth drawn smooth upon a frame.

The Lord's eyes had seen David's substance, yet being unperfect. Before David had arms or legs that moved. Before his eyes could open. Before he looked like aught a man would recognize. The Lord was there, beholding him grow, day by day, piece by piece.

Then David sang somewhat very great. He sang that all of his days — every single one, from the first breath to the last — were written in the Lord's book when as yet there was none of them. A book with all his days already upon the pages. Mornings whereunto he had not yet wakened. Afternoons through which he had not yet walked. Evenings upon which he had not yet seen the sun go down. All set down. All known.

David's song turned one more time. His voice grew warm. He sang of God's thoughts.

"How precious also are thy thoughts unto me, O God," he sang. "How great is the sum of them!"

If David sought to number the Lord's thoughts toward him, they should be more than the grains of sand. Every shore he had ever beheld, the sand stretching wide and golden beneath the sun, thousands upon thousands of tiny grains. Every desert, where the sand blew in waves as far as the horizon. Every river bed, where fine grit settled betwixt the smooth stones. Every handful of dirt that crumbled into dust between his fingers. All the sand in all the world, in every place — and the Lord's thoughts toward David outnumbered even that.

More than he could count. More than he could hold. More than a lifetime of counting could finish.

"When I awake," David sang, "I am still with thee."

The song was done. The air about David was still. His lips were closed. His hands rested open in his lap, palms up, fingers loose. Somewhere, sand shifted upon a distant shore, grain against grain, more than could be numbered, catching the light.
