In the third year of Cyrus king of Persia, Daniel was an old man. He had been carried out of Jerusalem as a boy. He had served kings whose names had become dust. He had outlived Babylon. He had outlived Belshazzar. He had outlived the writing on the wall. Now Persia was the world, and the temple in his city was being rebuilt, stone by stone, by men he had never met.

And in that third year, a thing was revealed unto Daniel, whose name was called Belteshazzar. The thing was true. It was a great warfare. He understood it, and he had understanding of the vision, and he could not eat.

In those days he was mourning three full weeks.

He ate no pleasant bread, neither came flesh nor wine in his mouth. He did not anoint his head. He did not soften his beard with oil. He did not change into clean garments at the appointed hour. The servants of his household set food before him and took it away again untouched. One and twenty days, the lamp by his window burned, and the old prophet sat by it, and his clothing hung looser on him as the weeks went by.

In the four and twentieth day of the first month, he rose. He went out beyond the city. He went out unto the bank of the great river, which is Hiddekel.

A handful of men walked with him. The river was wide and brown in the early light. Reeds stirred along its edge. Somewhere downstream a heron stood ankle-deep in the shallows. The sun had not yet broken the horizon, but the sky above the water was already pale.

Daniel lifted up his eyes, and looked.

And behold, a certain man stood before him.

He was clothed in linen, white and falling straight, and his loins were girded with fine gold of Uphaz. His body also was like the beryl — a sea-green and pale-fire color that did not belong unto flesh. His face was as the appearance of lightning. His eyes were as lamps of fire, burning, holding their own light. His arms and his feet were like in colour to polished brass, polished until they threw the morning back at the air. And when he spoke, the voice of his words was like the voice of a multitude — not one voice, but the sound of many, layered and pressing into the air above the river.

The men that were with Daniel saw not the vision.

But a great quaking fell upon them. They felt it in the ground. They felt it in their chests. They turned and ran. They scrambled into the reeds and the broken ground beyond the bank, and they hid themselves there. Their faces were pressed against the earth, and they did not look up.

Daniel was left alone on the bank of the river, and saw this great vision.

And there remained no strength in him.

The blood drained from his face. His comeliness was turned in him into corruption. His knees gave beneath him. His hands could not grip. His tongue lay heavy in his mouth. The sound of the voice went on above him, and he heard the voice of the words, and his body could not hold him any longer. He was in a deep sleep upon his face, his face toward the ground, his palms open at his sides.

The river kept running. The reeds kept stirring. The old prophet lay motionless on the bank.

Then, behold, an hand touched him.

It was a hand. It was warm. It was firm. It set him upon his knees and upon the palms of his hands. He felt the stones cold beneath his palms. He felt the grit pressing into his skin. He could not yet lift his head. His shoulders trembled.

A voice spoke above him. "O Daniel, a man greatly beloved, understand the words that I speak unto thee, and stand upright: for unto thee am I now sent."

Daniel stood.

He stood trembling. His knees would not steady. His hands shook at his sides. He stood at the edge of the river before the shining figure, and he listened.

"Fear not, Daniel: for from the first day that thou didst set thine heart to understand, and to chasten thyself before thy God, thy words were heard, and I am come for thy words."

Daniel heard.

"But the prince of the kingdom of Persia withstood me one and twenty days: but, lo, Michael, one of the chief princes, came to help me; for I remained there with the kings of Persia. Now I am come to make thee understand what shall befall thy people in the latter days: for yet the vision is for many days."

One and twenty days.

Daniel knew the number. It was the count of his mourning. While he had been sitting by his window with no bread and no oil on his face, the answer had been moving toward him. It had been delayed, but it had not been forgotten. From the first day, he had been heard. From the first day.

The voice went on. There were things to come. Days far ahead of him. The shape of nations rising and falling. Daniel listened, and his strength left him again. He set his face toward the ground. His mouth would not open. He stood mute on the river bank, and no breath was in him.

Then one in the likeness of the sons of men touched his lips.

His mouth opened. Words came up out of him — broken, halting, but his own.

"O my lord, by the vision my sorrows are turned upon me, and I have retained no strength. For how can the servant of this my lord talk with this my lord? for as for me, straightway there remained no strength in me, neither is there breath left in me."

He said it because it was true. He was an old man. He was hungry. He had been on his face. He had no breath.

The figure touched him again.

A third time.

And this time the touch did not set him upon his hands and knees. It did not open his mouth. It strengthened him. He felt it move into his arms. He felt it move into his chest. He felt it move into the place beneath his ribs where the breath had gone out of him, and the breath came back. He felt it in the muscles of his legs. He felt it in his jaw.

The voice spoke once more, and it was quieter now. It was close.

"O man greatly beloved, fear not: peace be unto thee, be strong, yea, be strong."

Daniel stood there.

He stood by the great river. The reeds moved. The brown water ran. The sun had cleared the horizon now, and the light was spreading across the bank. The men who had fled were still somewhere out among the broken ground, hiding. They had seen nothing. They would come out later. They would find him, and they would not understand the look upon his face.

But Daniel knew.

He drew a long breath. His shoulders steadied. The trembling in his hands slowed and went still. He stood upright on the bank of the Hiddekel, an old man whose words had been heard from the first day, who had been told three times to fear not, who had been touched three times — on his hands, on his lips, in his body — and who could feel his own strength returning.

He answered the figure who stood before him in linen and bronze and lightning.

"Let my lord speak; for thou hast strengthened me."

The river kept running. The reeds kept stirring. The day wore on.
