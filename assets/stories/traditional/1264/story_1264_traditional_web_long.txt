In the third year of Cyrus king of Persia, Daniel was an old man. He had been carried out of Jerusalem as a boy. He had served kings whose names had become dust. He had outlived Babylon. He had outlived Belshazzar. He had outlived the writing on the wall. Now Persia was the world, and the temple in his city was being rebuilt, stone by stone, by men he had never met.

And in that third year, a thing was revealed to him. The thing was true. It was a great warfare. He understood it, and he had understanding of the vision, and he could not eat.

For three whole weeks he mourned.

He took no pleasant bread. No meat nor wine came into his mouth. He did not anoint his head. He did not soften his beard with oil. He did not change into clean garments at the appointed hour. The servants of his household set food before him and took it away again untouched. Twenty-one days, the lamp by his window burned, and the old prophet sat by it, and his clothing hung looser on him as the weeks went by.

On the twenty-fourth day of the first month, he rose. He went out beyond the city. He went out to the bank of the great river, which is Hiddekel.

A handful of men walked with him. The river was wide and brown in the early light. Reeds stirred along its edge. Somewhere downstream a heron stood ankle-deep in the shallows. The sun had not yet broken the horizon, but the sky above the water was already pale.

Daniel lifted up his eyes, and he looked.

A man stood before him.

He was clothed in linen, white and falling straight, and his waist was adorned with pure gold of Uphaz. His body was like beryl — a sea-green and pale-fire color that did not belong to flesh. His face had the appearance of lightning. His eyes were as flaming torches, burning, holding their own light. His arms and his feet were the color of burnished bronze, polished until they threw the morning back at the air. And when he spoke, the voice of his words was like the voice of a multitude — not one voice, but the sound of many, layered and pressing into the air above the river.

The men who walked with Daniel did not see what he saw.

But a great quaking fell upon them. They felt it in the ground. They felt it in their chests. They turned and ran. They scrambled into the reeds and the broken ground beyond the bank, and they hid themselves there. Their faces were pressed against the earth, and they did not look up.

Daniel was left alone on the bank of the river, and he saw the great vision.

And no strength remained in him.

The blood drained from his face. His comeliness was turned to corruption. His knees gave beneath him. His hands could not grip. His tongue lay heavy in his mouth. The sound of the voice went on above him, and he heard the voice of the words, and his body could not hold him any longer. He fell into a deep sleep upon his face, his face turned toward the ground, his palms open at his sides.

The river kept running. The reeds kept stirring. The old prophet lay motionless on the bank.

Then a hand touched him.

It was a hand. It was warm. It was firm. It set him upon his knees and upon the palms of his hands. He felt the stones cold beneath his palms. He felt the grit pressing into his skin. He could not yet lift his head. His shoulders trembled.

A voice spoke above him. "Daniel, you greatly beloved man, understand the words that I speak to you, and stand upright. For I have been sent to you, now."

Daniel stood.

He stood trembling. His knees would not steady. His hands shook at his sides. He stood at the edge of the river before the shining figure, and he listened.

"Don't be afraid, Daniel. For from the first day that you set your heart to understand, and to humble yourself before your God, your words were heard. I have come for your words' sake."

Daniel heard.

"But the prince of the kingdom of Persia withstood me twenty-one days. Behold, Michael, one of the chief princes, came to help me, because I remained there with the kings of Persia. Now I have come to make you understand what will happen to your people in the latter days. For the vision is yet for many days."

Twenty-one days.

Daniel knew the number. It was the count of his mourning. While he had been sitting by his window with no bread and no oil on his face, the answer had been moving toward him. It had been delayed, but it had not been forgotten. From the first day, he had been heard. From the first day.

The voice went on. There were things to come. Days far ahead of him. The shape of nations rising and falling. Daniel listened, and his strength left him again. He set his face toward the ground. His mouth would not open. He stood mute on the river bank, and no breath was in him.

Then one in the likeness of the sons of men touched his lips.

His mouth opened. Words came up out of him — broken, halting, but his own.

"My lord, by reason of the vision my sorrows have overtaken me, and I retain no strength. For how can the servant of this my lord talk with this my lord? For as for me, immediately there remained no strength in me. There was no breath left in me."

He said it because it was true. He was an old man. He was hungry. He had been on his face. He had no breath.

The figure touched him again.

A third time.

And this time the touch did not set him on his hands and knees. It did not open his mouth. It strengthened him. He felt it move into his arms. He felt it move into his chest. He felt it move into the place beneath his ribs where the breath had gone out of him, and the breath came back. He felt it in the muscles of his legs. He felt it in his jaw.

The voice spoke once more, and it was quieter now. It was close.

"Greatly beloved man, don't be afraid. Peace be to you. Be strong. Yes, be strong."

Daniel stood there.

He stood by the great river. The reeds moved. The brown water ran. The sun had cleared the horizon now, and the light was spreading across the bank. The men who had fled were still somewhere out among the broken ground, hiding. They had seen nothing. They would come out later. They would find him, and they would not understand the look on his face.

But Daniel knew.

He drew a long breath. His shoulders steadied. The trembling in his hands slowed and went still. He stood upright on the bank of the Hiddekel, an old man whose words had been heard from the first day, who had been told three times not to be afraid, who had been touched three times — on his hands, on his lips, in his body — and who could feel his own strength returning.

He answered the figure who stood before him in linen and bronze and lightning.

"Let my lord speak. For you have strengthened me."

The river kept running. The reeds kept stirring. The day went on.
