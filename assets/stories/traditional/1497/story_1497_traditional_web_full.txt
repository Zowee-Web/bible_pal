David and his men had been three days on the road back from where Achish had sent them. They had been turned out of the Philistine army at Aphek. The lords of the Philistines had not trusted David among them when the battle line was forming. He had argued with Achish. Achish had let him go. They had marched south, six hundred men with their weapons and their dust.

They came up to Ziklag on the third day. The city was their city. David had been given it by Achish a year and four months before. Their wives and their children lived there. Their possessions were there.

Ziklag had been burned.

The Amalekites had come up against the south country while the men had been gone. They had come into Ziklag and they had taken it. They had not killed anyone. They had carried the women and the children away. The houses had been put to the fire. The smoke was still rising from some of the timbers as David and his men walked into what had been their city.

The wives were gone. The sons and the daughters had been taken. Not a body was left in the ash — no slain, no one wounded — only the silence of a place where the people had been carried away.

David lifted up his voice and wept. The men around him wept also. They wept until they had no more strength to weep. They sat down on the broken ground in the ash. They could not move. The sun went over them as they sat.

After a while one of them spoke against David. Another took up the word. A bitterness was rising in the men. They began to talk of stoning him, every man for his sons and his daughters that had been carried away. They had been with him in the wilderness for years. They had eaten what he had eaten. They had hidden in caves with him. Now they could not bear the weight of what had happened, and the bitter word turned in them and turned again.

David heard them speaking. He sat alone among them in the ashes. He did not answer them. He did not get up.

He encouraged himself in the LORD his God.

That is what the chronicle says — and the chronicle is sparing on what it means. He did not get up at once. He did not call to the men. He sat where he had sat. Something steadied in him in the silence between the words spoken against him and the words he would speak back.

When he had encouraged himself, he called for the ephod. He called for Abiathar the priest. Abiathar brought the ephod to him. David asked: "Shall I pursue after this troop? Shall I overtake them?"

The answer came: "Pursue. For you shall surely overtake them. Without fail you shall recover all."

David got up. He went out. The men with him got up and followed. The country before them was the country into which the troop had gone. The ash of the city was behind them. The day was wearing on. They went after the Amalekites with the strength that had come back into them, and the bitterness that had been about to break the company was put away.
