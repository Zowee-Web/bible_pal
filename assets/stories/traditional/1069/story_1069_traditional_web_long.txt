David sang to the Lord.

He was alone when the words began. The room was close and warm, the stones of the floor worn smooth beneath his feet. Light came through a narrow window set high in the wall. Dust hung suspended in the shaft of it, turning slowly, going nowhere.

David opened his mouth and sang.

He sang that God had searched him. The word he used was thorough — the kind of searching a man does when he empties a bag onto the ground and turns every object over in his hands, examining each one. God had done this to David. Searched him completely. And known him.

The knowing was not partial. God knew when David sat down. When he lowered himself onto a bench at the end of the day, when his legs folded beneath him on a patch of dry grass in the field, when he settled onto a stone ledge and let his weight come to rest — God saw it. God knew when he rose. The first movement of morning, the shift of limbs under a wool covering, the press of feet against cold ground.

David sang that God discerned his thoughts from afar. Not standing beside him. Not leaning close to listen. From a great distance, a height beyond measuring, God perceived what moved inside David's mind. The half-formed ideas, the worries that surfaced at night, the sudden flashes of memory that arrived without invitation — God saw them all, clear and distinct, before David himself understood them.

God was acquainted with all David's ways. The paths he traveled, the roads he chose, the places where he stopped to rest. When David walked through the hill country with dust rising around his ankles, God was familiar with every step. When David lay down at night and pulled his cloak over his chest and stared up at the darkness, God knew the exact position of his body against the earth.

Before a word was on David's tongue, the Lord knew it completely. The sentence that had not yet been spoken. The prayer that had not yet been formed. The cry that was still gathering in the throat — God knew it before the air moved past David's lips.

The song continued. David sang that God hemmed him in. Behind and before. Like a wall on every side, like the stones of a narrow pass that rise on the left and on the right with no way through except forward. God laid his hand upon David. The weight of it. The presence of it.

David sang that this knowledge was too wonderful for him. Too high. He could not attain it. He could not climb to the place where such understanding rested. It was above him, out of reach, like the top of a cedar that disappears into cloud.

Then the question came.

Where could he go from God's Spirit? Where could he flee from God's presence?

David did not wait for an answer from outside himself. He answered with his own voice, steady and unhurried.

If he ascended to heaven — climbed past the clouds, past the highest point a bird could fly, past the place where the air thinned and the blue deepened to black — God was there.

If he made his bed in Sheol — descended into the place of the dead, the silent country beneath the earth where no light reached and no voice called — God was there. Even there. In the lowest place, the furthest distance a man could fall from the land of the living, God waited.

If he took the wings of the morning — the first spreading of light that raced across the sky ahead of the sun, faster than any runner, faster than any chariot — and if those wings carried him to the uttermost parts of the sea, to the place where the water met the rim of the world and the horizon bent down into nothing — even there, God's hand would lead him. God's right hand would hold him.

There was no distance far enough.

David sang on. If he said, Surely the darkness shall cover me. If he called on the night to wrap itself around him like a thick garment. If he wanted the light to become night, to be swallowed, to vanish so that he stood in a blackness so complete he could not see his own hand before his face — even that darkness was not dark to God.

The night shone like the day. To God, there was no difference between them. Darkness and light were alike. No shadow could hide what God had already seen. No night could conceal what God had already known.

The tone of the song changed. David's voice dropped lower, quieter, closer to the ground.

He sang about his own making. God had formed his inward parts. The organs that moved inside him, the heart that beat against his ribs, the lungs that filled and emptied — God had fashioned each one. God had knit him together in his mother's womb. The word was precise. Knit. The careful interlocking of thread upon thread, the patient work of making something whole from separate strands.

David praised God. He was fearfully and wonderfully made. The words came out of him plainly, without ornament. Wonderful were God's works. David's soul knew it well. Knew it the way a man knows the weight of his own hands, the rhythm of his own breathing. Not argued into. Not persuaded of. Known.

His frame had not been hidden from God when he was being made in secret. When his bones were being shaped in darkness, when his body was being formed where no human eye could see — God saw him. In the depths of the earth, in the hidden places, in the silence before birth, God watched.

David was woven together there. The sinew and the skin. The fine network of veins that carried blood to his fingers and toes. The tendons that attached muscle to bone. All of it constructed in a place no one else could enter or observe.

God's eyes had seen his unformed substance. Before David had arms or legs. Before his face had features. Before his fingers could grip or his mouth could cry out. God looked upon the raw matter of him and saw what he would become.

In God's book, all of David's days were written. Every one of them. Formed and set down before a single day existed. Before the first morning broke over the hills where David would eventually tend sheep. Before the first evening settled over the fields where he would one day run from a king who wanted him dead. Before the first night he would spend on a palace roof with the sounds of the city below him. All of it written. All of it known.

David's voice thinned but did not stop. He sang the final words with the same steadiness he had carried from the beginning.

How vast was the sum of them. David sang that if he tried to count those thoughts, they would outnumber the sand. More than the grains on the shore of the Great Sea. More than the fine particles that shifted along the banks of the Jordan when the water was low. More than the dust that blew across the Negev and settled in the cracks of dry ground. More than a man could hold in both hands. More than a man could hold in a lifetime of gathering.

David's mouth closed. The last sound faded into the walls of the room. The shaft of light from the window had moved, falling now across the floor in a long bright stripe. Dust still turned in it, slow and weightless. David stood with his hands at his sides, his chest rising and falling, the silence settling around him like sand.
