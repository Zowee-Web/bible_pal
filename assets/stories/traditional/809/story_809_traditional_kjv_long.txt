As the evening fell upon the shores of the sea of Galilee, the air grew cool, and a hush settled upon the gathered multitude. The sky was touched with the deepening blue of twilight, and the many people whom Jesus had taught now began to drift back unto their houses. The same day, when the even was come, Jesus turned unto his disciples and said, "Let us pass over unto the other side." Without delay, the disciples, who knew the ways of the water, made ready a small ship. They left the multitude behind, taking Jesus along even as he was. Other little ships gathered nigh, their hulls bobbing quietly upon the gentle swells. The sea seemed peaceful, holding the savour of wet wood and cool water, as the disciples set forth toward the further shore.

As the ship slipped further from the land, the darkness wrapped itself round about the little company. The sky grew thick with clouds, making the waters beneath them seem even more uncertain. The fishermen among them kept a weathered eye upon the horizon, and unease settled in their hearts. Jesus, having spent the day teaching the great multitudes, found a place in the hinder part of the ship. There, resting upon a pillow, he soon fell asleep amid the gentle rocking of the vessel. Round about him, the disciples ordered the sails and the oars, and the only sounds were the creaking of the wood and the low murmur of voices against the sweep of the wind.

And it came to pass that the calm was suddenly broken. Without warning, there arose a great storm of wind upon the lake, swift and exceeding violent. Cold blasts swept over the water, churning it into wild waves that beat against the sides of the ship. The darkness deepened as the clouds pressed lower, and the rain began to fall in fierce slants. The thunder rolled across the water, echoing among the hills of Galilee. The ship, small against the power of the storm, was hammered by the waves, swaying and shuddering as the water poured in. The disciples, skilled though they were, found themselves straining every sinew to keep the vessel afloat. Their voices rose, tight with fear, as the ship filled rapidly, threatening to be swallowed up by the angry sea.

The spray smote their faces; the salt stung their eyes and their mouths. The wind howled through the rigging, and the lightning flashed, lighting their anxious faces. Every wave seemed to crash higher than the last, drenching them and lurching the ship close unto being overturned. The men shouted above the roar, fighting the unceasing weight of water with their hands and whatsoever they could find. Desperation filled their hearts, for they saw no end to the fury of the storm. And all the while, in the hinder part of the ship, Jesus slept on, his head upon the little pillow, as though untouched by the terror that had seized them. The sounds of fear and the battering of the rain did not wake him, and every moment made his restful silence the more striking against the wildness round about them.

At length, in their fear, the disciples came unto Jesus. They shook him from his sleep, their voices choked with dread and amazement that he could yet slumber while their lives were in such danger. "Master," they cried out above the tumult, "carest thou not that we perish?" Their words spake not of fear alone, but of a deep dismay — an urgent cry from them that had reached the end of their own strength. The storm threatened to drag them under at any moment; the darkness pressed in upon every side with nothing but the wailing of the wind and the endless water.

And Jesus awoke and stood in the midst of the chaos. The rain ran down from his hair and his garments as he looked first upon the storm, and then upon his frightened disciples. Without haste, but with authority, he arose and turned his face unto the fury of the wind and the thrashing sea. Lifting up his voice over the panic and the storm, he commanded, "Peace, be still." And straightway, as though the creation itself obeyed its Maker, the wind dropped away. The roaring air became calm. The waves that had battered the little ship now were stilled and smoothed, the tumult turned into a deep and sudden silence. The ship lay steady upon a glassy face, the water shewing forth the now quiet sky. Round about, the other ships also were caught up in that astonishing moment — wherein, in a single instant, the terror was exchanged for stillness.

A hush settled upon the disciples. The echo of their cries lingered, yet was now replaced by a great astonishment and an unsettled quiet. Jesus turned unto them, his gaze speaking both compassion and a gentle reproof. "Why are ye so fearful?" he asked them. "How is it that ye have no faith?" His words, calm and true, reached into the very depths of their hearts. The disciples, though relieved, were filled with a new kind of awe. Fear was mingled with wonder as they beheld one another and beheld him. With hushed voices they said one to another, "What manner of man is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" The night lay calm round about them now, where but a few moments before the waves had threatened destruction. The ship made her way safely upon the still waters, and the disciples sat with trembling hearts, left in silent awe of the authority of him that had commanded both the wind and the sea, even as it is written in the Gospel according to Mark, the fourth chapter, from the thirty-fifth verse unto the forty-first.

As the ship glided onward over the stilled water, a deep silence wrapped itself round about the disciples, broken only by the soft drip of the rainwater from their garments and the gentle lapping of water beneath the hull. Drenched and with their hearts pounding, they scarce dared to move or to speak, their minds wrestling with the memory of that which had just passed — a tempest hushed by a single word. The air itself seemed to tremble with a holy quiet, as though the world waited, breathless, for that which should come next.

Within the hush, Peter found that he could not turn away his eyes from Jesus. He beheld once more, as if for the first time, the lines of weariness and peace upon his countenance, the depths behind his eyes that spake of mysteries beyond man's understanding. Beside Peter, James wiped his brow with a shaking hand, still gazing down into the dark water, reliving the terror and the sudden calm. John, ever sensitive of soul, felt his own spirit quiver with a mingling of fear and longing. None of them desired to speak, for even their whispering would have seemed too loud, out of place in the aftermath of such a wonder.

By and by, the moon broke through the thinning clouds, casting a pale path across the water. The shore unto which they journeyed grew clearer in the distance, a dark line set against the horizon. Thomas at length broke the quiet, his voice small but steady: "Saw ye his eyes? The manner in which he beheld the wind and the sea, as one commanding old friends." Matthew, shivering yet, gave a small laugh, more of relief than of mirth. "Old friends, peradventure, or servants." The others considered these words, suffering them to settle upon their weary hearts. Jesus, meanwhile, sat quietly among them, his own gaze fixed upon the peaceful waters before them.

The other ships, which had set out with them, now drew nigh. From every vessel arose calls of amazement and anxious greetings, as men and women joined the disciples in marvelling at that which had taken place. Some wept openly, overcome with relief and awe. Fathers spake unto their sons with hushed voices, and mothers clave unto their children, the memory of terror giving way unto thanksgiving and solemn wonder.

As the ships glided toward the shore, the hearts of the disciples remained heavy with questions. Who, in truth, was this Master, that held within him both compassion and an authority unto which even the elements made answer? The storm had laid bare their own frailty, but in its stillness it had also revealed the presence of something — of someone — wholly other at the heart of their journey.

When at last their vessel ground gently against the pebbled shore upon the further side, the night was wholly calm. The travellers stepped down upon the solid ground, their legs unsteady beneath them. In the newfound quiet, the savour of wet earth and reeds mingled with the lingering brine of the lake.

Jesus led them a little way from the ships. Pausing, he beheld their faces — faces marked with weariness, awe, and burning questions. Though his words had rebuked their fear, his presence now shone forth with a warmth that bade them trust him. Without another word, he began to walk along the shore, and the disciples, drawn by love, by wonder, and by a deepening faith, followed after him into the night, uncertain yet willing, their hearts stretched wider by all that they had seen.

Thus the journey unto the other side became also a journey within, where every disciple pondered in silence what manner of man could hush a tempest with a single word — and what it might mean to follow him, whithersoever the path might lead. The night, once filled with fury, now promised peace, even as new mysteries gathered at the edges of the breaking dawn.
