The wilderness east of the Jordan was a place of stone and silence. The land rose in a series of dry, barren ridges — pale limestone baked white by the sun, cracked and weathered into strange shapes by centuries of wind. Between the ridges lay shallow wadis where the winter rains sometimes left a thin trickle of water, but in this season they were dry. The ground was scattered with loose stones and patches of brittle thorn. Nothing green grew. No trees gave shade. The only movement was the shimmer of heat above the rocks and the occasional flicker of a lizard disappearing into a crack.

This was where the Spirit led Jesus after his baptism.

He walked away from the river, away from the green banks and the crowds and the sound of John's voice over the water. He climbed into the hills. The path — if it could be called a path — was little more than a track worn by wild goats and shepherds. The stones shifted under his sandals. The air grew drier with every step. Behind him, the river valley fell away. Ahead, there was nothing but stone and sky.

He was alone. Completely alone. For forty days and forty nights he stayed in that place, eating nothing. The first days were the hardest — the hunger sharp and immediate, the body demanding what it needed. But as the days passed, the hunger changed. It became something deeper, less like pain and more like weight. His body thinned. His face grew gaunt. His skin darkened under the sun. His lips cracked. He sat on the bare ground or walked slowly along the ridges, his feet scraping against the stone, his shadow long in the early morning and the late afternoon, short and sharp at midday when the sun was directly overhead.

The nights were cold. The desert, so brutal in the day, turned sharp and still after dark. The stars were enormous — closer than they seemed anywhere else, filling the sky from horizon to horizon. The wind moved through the stones with a low, steady sound, like breath.

When the forty days were finished, and Jesus was at his weakest, the tempter came.

"If you are the Son of God," the devil said, "command these stones to become bread."

Jesus looked at the ground around him. The stones lay scattered across the dry earth — flat, round, baked smooth by the sun. They were the color of bread crusts. The shape of loaves. His stomach clenched. Every muscle in his body ached with want.

But he answered. His voice was rough from disuse, but steady. "It is written," he said: "'Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that comes from the mouth of God.'"

Then the devil took him to the holy city and set him on the pinnacle of the temple. Jerusalem spread out below them — the rooftops and the courtyards, the narrow streets, the smoke rising from cooking fires, the crowds moving through the gates. The temple itself gleamed white and gold in the sunlight. The drop from the highest point to the courtyard below was immense — a sheer fall through empty air to the hard stone pavement.

"If you are the Son of God," the devil said, "throw yourself down. For it is written: 'He will command his angels concerning you, and they will bear you up in their hands, so that you will not strike your foot against a stone.'"

The wind pulled at Jesus' clothing. The city moved below him, unaware. The people in the courtyard were small as insects.

Jesus said to him, "Again it is written: 'You shall not put the Lord your God to the test.'"

Then the devil took him to a very high mountain — higher than any mountain Jesus had climbed, higher than the hills of Galilee or the ridges of Judea. From its summit, the kingdoms of the world were visible in every direction. Cities with towers and walls. Rivers winding through green valleys. Harbors where ships sat at anchor. Fields golden with harvest. Mountains touched with snow at their peaks. Deserts and forests and coastlines stretching to the edge of sight. All of it — every nation, every throne, every beautiful and powerful thing the world contained — laid out below them, shining in the light.

"All of this I will give you," the devil said. His voice was smooth, almost kind. "Every kingdom. Every crown. All of it. If you will fall down and worship me."

Jesus turned to him. His body was weak. His hands were thin. His face was drawn from forty days without food. But his eyes were clear, and his voice did not waver.

"Away from me, Satan," he said. "For it is written: 'You shall worship the Lord your God, and him only shall you serve.'"

The devil left him.

The wilderness was quiet. The wind moved through the stones. The sun beat down on the pale ground. The horizon shimmered. Jesus stood alone on the mountain — thin, exhausted, his lips cracked, his clothing dusty and worn.

Then angels came. They came to him in that barren place, in the silence and the emptiness, and they ministered to him. How they came, and what they brought, and how long they stayed, the scripture does not say. Only that they came, and that he was not alone.
