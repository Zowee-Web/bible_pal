The wilderness east of the Jordan was a land of stone and silence. The ground rose in dry, barren ridges — pale limestone baked white by the sun, cracked and wrought into strange forms by ages of wind. Between the ridges lay shallow wadis where the winter rains sometimes left a thin line of water, but in this season they were dry. The earth was strewn with loose stones and brittle thorn. Nothing green grew. No trees gave shade. The only movement was the shimmer of heat above the rocks and the flicker of a lizard vanishing into a crack.

This was the place whither the Spirit led Jesus after his baptism.

He went from the river, from the green banks and the crowds and the sound of John's voice over the water. He climbed into the hills. The path — if path it could be called — was little more than a track worn by wild goats and shepherds. The stones shifted beneath his sandals. The air grew drier with every step. Behind him, the valley of the Jordan fell away. Before him was nothing but stone and sky.

He was alone. Utterly alone. For forty days and forty nights he abode in that place, eating nothing. The first days were the hardest — the hunger sharp and present, the body crying out for what it needed. But as the days passed, the hunger changed. It became something deeper, less like pain and more like weight. His body wasted. His face grew gaunt. His skin darkened beneath the sun. His lips cracked and bled. He sat upon the bare ground or walked slowly along the ridges, his feet scraping upon the stone, his shadow long at the morning and the evening, short at midday when the sun was straight above.

The nights were cold. The desert, so fierce by day, turned sharp and still when darkness fell. The stars were vast — nearer than they seemed in any other place, filling the sky from one edge to the other. The wind moved among the stones with a low, steady sound, like unto breathing.

When the forty days were accomplished, and Jesus was at his weakest, the tempter came unto him.

"If thou be the Son of God," said the devil, "command that these stones be made bread."

Jesus looked upon the ground about him. The stones lay scattered across the dry earth — flat, round, baked smooth by the sun. They were the very colour of bread crusts. The very shape of loaves. His stomach clenched. Every sinew of his body ached for food.

But he answered. His voice was rough from long disuse, but steady. "It is written," he said, "Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of God."

Then the devil taketh him up into the holy city, and setteth him on a pinnacle of the temple. Jerusalem lay spread below them — the rooftops and the courts, the narrow streets, the smoke of fires, the multitudes passing through the gates. The temple itself shone white and gold in the sun. The fall from the highest point to the pavement below was immense — a sheer drop through empty air to hard stone.

"If thou be the Son of God," the devil said, "cast thyself down: for it is written, He shall give his angels charge concerning thee: and in their hands they shall bear thee up, lest at any time thou dash thy foot against a stone."

The wind caught at the garments of Jesus. The city moved beneath him, unknowing. The people in the court below were small as motes.

Jesus saith unto him, "It is written again, Thou shalt not tempt the Lord thy God."

Then the devil taketh him up into an exceeding high mountain — higher than any hill he had climbed, higher than the ridges of Judaea or the mountains of Galilee. From the summit thereof, the kingdoms of the world lay visible in every quarter. Cities with towers and walls. Rivers winding through green valleys. Harbours where ships rocked at anchor. Fields golden with harvest. Mountains with snow upon their crowns. Deserts and forests and coastlands reaching unto the very edge of sight. All of it — every nation, every throne, every fair and mighty thing the world contained — laid out beneath them, glittering in the light.

"All these things will I give thee," the devil said. His voice was smooth, almost gentle. "Every kingdom. Every crown. If thou wilt fall down and worship me."

Jesus turned unto him. His body was weakened. His hands were thin. His face was drawn from forty days of fasting. But his eyes were clear, and his voice wavered not.

"Get thee hence, Satan," he said. "For it is written, Thou shalt worship the Lord thy God, and him only shalt thou serve."

Then the devil leaveth him.

The wilderness was quiet. The wind moved among the stones. The sun beat down upon the pale ground. The horizon shimmered. Jesus stood alone upon the mountain — thin, spent, his lips parched, his garments dusty and worn.

Then angels came. They came unto him in that barren place, in the silence and the emptiness, and they ministered unto him. How they came, and what they brought, and how long they abode with him, the scripture saith not. Only that they came, and that he was alone no more.
