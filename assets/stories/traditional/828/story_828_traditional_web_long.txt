In the days when the judges ruled over Israel, a famine fell upon the land. It was a time of struggle—fields lay parched, and hope seemed as distant as the rain. There was a certain man from Bethlehem in Judah, named Elimelech. With his wife Naomi and their two sons, Mahlon and Chilion, he journeyed across the dusty wilderness, leaving behind the hills of Judah for the country of Moab.

The family found a new home there, among unfamiliar people and foreign gods. But adversity followed them. In Moab, Elimelech died, and Naomi was left widowed, with only her two sons beside her. In time, the sons took Moabite wives—one named Orpah, the other Ruth. For ten years, the family endured, living as strangers in a strange land.

But sorrow was not finished with Naomi. First one son died, then the other. The house grew quiet, bereft of laughter and the voices of men. Naomi was left with only her two daughters-in-law, Orpah and Ruth. Together, the three women carried heavy hearts and the weight of uncertainty.

As the seasons shifted, word came from Judah. The Lord had visited His people once more—grain grew in Bethlehem, and bread was again on their tables. At this, Naomi’s longing for home became impossible to ignore. She prepared to return to Judah, to the land where she had once built a life with Elimelech. Orpah and Ruth, bound by custom and by affection, rose up to journey with her.

They set out from the land of Moab, three figures on a sunbitten road, traveling toward Judah’s rolling hills. The air was thick with dust. With each step, the world of Moab fell further behind—their memories, the graves, all that they had known. Naomi led the way, heart aching for both what had been lost and what might yet be regained.

Pausing on the open road, Naomi turned. The light caught the lines of grief on her face. She spoke with the deep care of one who had loved and lost, “Go, return each of you to your mother’s house.” Her voice was steady, though sorrow sat heavy upon it. “May Yahweh deal kindly with you, as you have dealt with the dead and with me.”

The wind stirred, and Naomi reached for their hands. “Yahweh grant you that you may find rest, each of you in the house of her husband.” She pressed her lips to their cheeks, blessing them with a mother’s love and sending them away, or so she hoped.

Orpah and Ruth wept. The road felt lonely, the future uncertain. Clinging to Naomi, they protested, “No, but we will return with you to your people.” Their voices trembled between duty and devotion.

But Naomi insisted. “Turn again, my daughters. Why will you go with me? Do I still have sons in my womb, that they may be your husbands?” She gazed upon the two younger women, her sorrow mingling with realism. “Turn again, my daughters, go your way; for I am too old to have a husband. If I should say, I have hope—even if I should have a husband tonight and should also bear sons—would you wait for them till they were grown?” Naomi’s gaze was steady, her care undimmed by grief. “Would you stay for them from having husbands? No, my daughters. For it grieves me exceedingly for your sakes, for Yahweh’s hand has gone out against me.”

Tears fell, tracing lines through the dust on their cheeks. The air was thick with grief. Orpah clung to Naomi, her embrace lingering a heartbeat longer, and then she let go. The pain was sharp and real. With one last kiss upon Naomi’s cheek, Orpah turned back, her figure soon distant against the sky, returning toward her own people and her gods.

Naomi turned her gaze to Ruth, who stood quietly beside her. The great road stretched ahead, empty fields lining the horizon. The ache of parting weighed heavily. “Behold,” Naomi said, a note of urgency in her voice, “your sister-in-law has gone back to her people and to her god. Follow your sister-in-law.”

But Ruth did not budge. The sound of Orpah’s retreating footsteps faded, but Ruth’s resolve grew only deeper. She spoke, her words clear and unwavering, “Don’t urge me to leave you, or to return from following you. For where you go, I will go; and where you stay, I will stay. Your people shall be my people, and your God my God. Where you die, I will die, and there I will be buried. Yahweh do so to me, and more also, if anything but death parts you and me.”

Naomi listened. Ruth’s devotion was steadfast. The morning sun shone down upon them, casting their shadows side by side on the stony road. There was nothing more to say. Naomi realized Ruth was determined, and so she relented, no longer pressing her to turn back.

They walked on together, two women cloaked in loss but united by love. The hills of Judah beckoned with silent promise—a fresh beginning, though the path was uncertain. Dust swirled around their feet as they pressed onward, the fields in the distance golden with the promise of harvests yet to come.

At last, they came to Bethlehem. As Naomi entered the city with Ruth at her side, the whole town was stirred because of them. Faces peered from doorways, voices rose in surprise. Some among the women of Bethlehem called out, “Is this Naomi?” Their wonder rippled through the gathering crowd—so long had Naomi been gone, so deep the changes wrought by years and by sorrow.

Naomi replied, her voice weighed by all that had befallen her, “Don’t call me Naomi. Call me Mara, for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me. I went out full, and Yahweh has brought me home again empty. Why do you call me Naomi, since Yahweh has testified against me, and the Almighty has afflicted me?”

Shadows stretched across the city streets, yet the noise and curiosity did not fade. Naomi’s face showed the long journey, the lines of hardship and endurance. Ruth, the Moabitess, stood quietly by her side, a stranger in a strange city, but steadfast in her resolve. The city’s eyes lingered on the two widows—the returning daughter of Judah and the Moabite woman who had clung to her.

There was no feasting or fanfare, only the simple return to Bethlehem among the women, the houses, and the empty fields. The barley was ripe for harvest, and the air carried the scent of growing grain.

So Naomi returned, and Ruth the Moabitess, her daughter-in-law, with her, who returned out of the country of Moab. They came to Bethlehem in the beginning of barley harvest.
