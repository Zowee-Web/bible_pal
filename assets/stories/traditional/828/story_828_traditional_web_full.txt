In the days when judges ruled over Israel, there came a time of famine in the land. A man from Bethlehem in Judah took his wife and two sons and went to live for a while in the country of Moab. The man’s name was Elimelech, his wife was Naomi, and their sons were Mahlon and Chilion. They left their home and crossed distant hills, seeking survival in a foreign field.

In Moab, tragedy struck. Elimelech died, leaving Naomi a widow with her sons. The sons took wives from the people of Moab—one named Orpah, and the other Ruth. Together, the family continued for about ten years. But sorrow deepened still, for Mahlon and Chilion also died, leaving Naomi without husband or sons. Only she and her two daughters-in-law, Ruth and Orpah, remained. Three widows now faced an uncertain future, with the harshness of famine shadowing their thoughts.

The news reached Naomi in Moab that the Lord had paid attention to His people by giving them bread in Bethlehem. With hope stirring, Naomi gathered her belongings and prepared to return to her homeland. She set out on the journey from the land of Moab, her two daughters-in-law following along the dusty road, hills rising in the distance. The path was difficult, the weight of loss heavy on every footstep.

As they traveled, Naomi stopped. She turned to her daughters-in-law and said, “Go, return each of you to your mother’s house. May the Lord deal kindly with you, as you have dealt with the dead and with me. May the Lord grant that you each find rest, each in the house of her husband.” Tears shimmered as she kissed them, and they raised their voices and wept together, the ache of parting swelling in the silence of the road.

Orpah and Ruth replied, “Surely we will return with you to your people.” But Naomi pressed them, her voice weary but kind, “Turn back, my daughters. Why will you go with me? Are there yet sons in my womb, that they may be your husbands? Turn back, my daughters, go your way, for I am too old to have a husband. If I should say, ‘I have hope,’ even if I had a husband tonight and also bore sons, would you wait for them to grow? Would you restrain yourselves for so long? No, my daughters; for it grieves me greatly for your sakes, for the Lord’s hand has gone out against me.”

Again, the women lifted their voices and wept together. Orpah hesitated, sorrow and love mingling in her tears. Finally, she kissed her mother-in-law, embracing her in farewell. With a heavy heart, Orpah turned back toward Moab, to the land and family she had known. Ruth, however, clung to Naomi. She would not let go.

Naomi spoke once more, trying to persuade Ruth. “Behold, your sister-in-law has gone back to her people and to her gods. Return with her after your sister-in-law.”

But Ruth’s response was resolute. She looked at Naomi and said, “Don’t urge me to leave you, or to return from following you, for where you go, I will go. Where you lodge, I will lodge. Your people shall be my people, and your God my God. Where you die, I will die, and there I will be buried. May the Lord do so to me, and more also, if anything but death parts you and me.”

Naomi saw the steadfastness in Ruth’s face and realized she was determined to go with her. She no longer insisted, and the two women continued together, leaving the place of lament behind them, step by step toward Bethlehem. The roads were empty except for them, their feet stirring dust into the morning air, the fields ahead lying ready for harvest.

At last, they arrived in Bethlehem. Their coming caused a stir throughout the town. The women of the city looked in astonishment, whispering among themselves, “Is this Naomi?” She replied, “Don’t call me Naomi. Call me Mara, for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me. I went out full, and Yahweh has brought me home again empty. Why do you call me Naomi, since Yahweh has testified against me, and the Almighty has afflicted me?”

Through dusty streets, Naomi and Ruth made their way, the past’s sorrows mingling with new beginnings. So Naomi returned, and Ruth the Moabitess, her daughter-in-law, with her, who returned out of the country of Moab. They came to Bethlehem in the beginning of barley harvest.
