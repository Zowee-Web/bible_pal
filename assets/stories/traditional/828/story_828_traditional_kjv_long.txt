In the days when the judges ruled over Israel, there fell a famine upon the land. It was a time of trouble — the fields lay parched, and hope seemed as far off as the rain. There was a certain man of Bethlehemjudah, named Elimelech. With his wife Naomi and his two sons, Mahlon and Chilion, he journeyed across the dusty wilderness, leaving behind the hills of Judah for the country of Moab.

The household found a new dwelling place there, among a strange people and foreign gods. But adversity followed them. In Moab, Elimelech died, and Naomi was left a widow, with only her two sons beside her. In process of time, the sons took them wives of the women of Moab — the name of the one was Orpah, and the name of the other Ruth. About ten years the household continued, sojourning as strangers in a strange land.

But sorrow had not yet finished with Naomi. First the one son died, and then the other. The house grew still, bereft of laughter and the voices of men. Naomi was left of her two daughters in law, Orpah and Ruth. Together the three women bare heavy hearts, and the burden of uncertainty.

As the seasons turned, tidings came out of Judah. The Lord had visited his people once again — grain grew in Bethlehem, and bread was once more upon their tables. At this, Naomi's longing for home grew too great to be denied. She made ready to return unto Judah, unto the land wherein she had once built a life with Elimelech. Orpah and Ruth, bound by custom and by love, arose to go with her upon the way.

They went forth out of the land of Moab, three figures upon a sunbeaten road, journeying toward the rolling hills of Judah. The air was thick with dust. With every step, the world of Moab fell yet further behind — their memories, the graves, all that they had known. Naomi led the way, her heart aching both for that which was lost and for that which might yet be restored.

Standing still upon the open way, Naomi turned. The light caught the lines of grief upon her countenance. She spake with the deep tenderness of one who had loved and lost. "Go, return each unto her mother's house." Her voice was steady, though sorrow lay heavy upon it. "The Lord deal kindly with you, as ye have dealt with the dead, and with me."

The wind stirred, and Naomi reached forth her hand unto theirs. "The Lord grant you that ye may find rest, each of you in the house of her husband." She pressed her lips unto their cheeks, blessing them with a mother's love and sending them away, or so she hoped.

Orpah and Ruth wept. The way felt lonely, and that which lay before them was uncertain. Cleaving unto Naomi, they made answer, "Surely we will return with thee unto thy people." Their voices trembled betwixt duty and devotion.

But Naomi pressed them. "Turn again, my daughters: why will ye go with me? Are there yet any more sons in my womb, that they may be your husbands?" She looked upon the two younger women, her sorrow mingling with truth. "Turn again, my daughters, go your way; for I am too old to have an husband. If I should say, I have hope, if I should have an husband also to night, and should also bear sons —would ye tarry for them till they were grown?" Naomi's gaze was steady, her tenderness undimmed by grief. "Would ye stay for them from having husbands? Nay, my daughters; for it grieveth me much for your sakes, that the hand of the Lord is gone out against me."

Tears fell, tracing lines through the dust upon their cheeks. The air was thick with grief. Orpah clave unto Naomi, her embrace lingering a heartbeat longer, and then she let go. The pain was sharp and real. With one last kiss upon Naomi's cheek, Orpah turned back, her form soon distant against the sky, returning unto her own people and her gods.

Naomi turned her gaze unto Ruth, who stood quietly beside her. The great way stretched on before them, empty fields lining the horizon. The ache of parting weighed heavy. "Behold," Naomi said, a note of urgency in her voice, "thy sister in law is gone back unto her people, and unto her gods: return thou after thy sister in law."

But Ruth would not be moved. The sound of Orpah's retreating steps faded, but Ruth's resolve grew only the deeper. She spake, her words clear and unfaltering: "Intreat me not to leave thee, or to return from following after thee: for whither thou goest, I will go; and where thou lodgest, I will lodge: thy people shall be my people, and thy God my God: where thou diest, will I die, and there will I be buried: the Lord do so to me, and more also, if ought but death part thee and me."

Naomi hearkened. Ruth's devotion was stedfast. The morning sun shone down upon them, casting their shadows side by side upon the stony way. There was nothing more to say. When Naomi beheld that Ruth was stedfastly minded to go with her, then she left speaking unto her.

They went on together, two women cloaked in loss but joined by love. The hills of Judah beckoned with silent promise — a new beginning, though the way before them was uncertain. Dust eddied about their feet as they pressed onward, the fields afar off golden with the promise of harvests yet to come.

At length, they came to Bethlehem. As Naomi entered the city with Ruth at her side, all the city was moved about them. Faces peered from doorways, voices rose in wonder. Some of the women of Bethlehem called out, "Is this Naomi?" Their wonder rippled through the gathering throng — so long had Naomi been gone, and so deep were the changes wrought by years and by sorrow.

Naomi made answer, her voice weighed by all that had befallen her, "Call me not Naomi, call me Mara: for the Almighty hath dealt very bitterly with me. I went out full, and the Lord hath brought me home again empty: why then call ye me Naomi, seeing the Lord hath testified against me, and the Almighty hath afflicted me?"

Shadows stretched across the streets of the city, yet the noise and wonder did not fade. Naomi's countenance shewed the long way, the lines of hardship and endurance. Ruth, the Moabitess, stood quietly beside her, a stranger in a strange city, but stedfast in her resolve. The eyes of the city lingered upon the two widows — the returning daughter of Judah and the Moabite woman who had cleaved unto her.

There was no feasting nor fanfare, only the simple returning unto Bethlehem among the women, the houses, and the empty fields. The barley was ripe for harvest, and the air bore the savour of growing grain.

So Naomi returned, and Ruth the Moabitess, her daughter in law, with her, which returned out of the country of Moab: and they came to Bethlehem in the beginning of barley harvest.
