Naaman was the commander of the entire army of Syria. He was the man the king sent into battle first. He was the one other soldiers followed. He rode the finest horses and wore the best armor and lived in a big house with servants. Everyone respected him.

But under his armor, under his fine robes, Naaman had a secret that everyone knew and nobody could fix. He had leprosy — a terrible disease that made patches of his skin turn white and numb. It spread slowly. There was no medicine for it. No doctor could help. No amount of money could buy a cure.

Naaman was the most powerful soldier in Syria, and he was helpless.

In his house, there was a little girl. She had been captured during a raid on Israel and brought back to Syria as a servant for Naaman's wife. She was young — maybe ten or eleven. She was far from home, far from her parents, far from everything she loved. She had every reason to be angry or bitter.

But she wasn't.

One day she said something to Naaman's wife. "I wish my master could go see the prophet who lives in Samaria. He could cure his leprosy."

It was a small sentence from a small girl in a big house full of important people. But Naaman's wife told Naaman, and Naaman told the king, and the king said, "Go. I'll write you a letter."

So Naaman set out. He didn't travel lightly. He brought his horses and his chariots, his soldiers and his servants. He brought ten talents of silver, six thousand pieces of gold, and ten sets of brand-new clothes. This was how important people did things — they showed up with money and power and expected to be treated accordingly.

He arrived at the prophet Elisha's house. His chariot stopped in the dust outside. His horses stamped their hooves. His servants stood at attention. Naaman climbed down and walked to the door.

Elisha didn't come out.

He sent a servant with a message: "Go wash yourself in the Jordan River seven times. Your skin will be healed and you will be clean."

Naaman stared at the servant. That was it? No dramatic ceremony? No impressive prayer? No waving of hands? Elisha didn't even bother to come to the door?

Naaman was furious. "I thought he would at least come out and stand here and call on the name of the Lord and wave his hand over my skin! And the Jordan? That muddy little river? We have better rivers back in Damascus. I could wash in those!"

He turned his chariot around and started to leave. He was angry and embarrassed and hurt.

But his servants — the people who knew him best, who had seen him at his strongest and his weakest — came close and spoke carefully.

"Sir," they said, "if the prophet had told you to do something hard and impressive, you would have done it without thinking. He only asked you to do something simple. Why not just try?"

Naaman sat still on his chariot for a long moment. The Jordan River was right there, muddy and brown, flowing through the low valley. Nothing special about it. Nothing impressive at all.

He stepped down. He walked to the water's edge. He took off his sandals and waded in.

The water was cool. The mud squished between his toes. He looked at the white patches on his arms. Then he dipped himself under.

Once. He came up. Nothing changed.

Twice. Still the same.

Three times. Four. Five. Six. The patches were still there. The skin was still numb and white.

Seven.

He came up out of the water and looked at his arms. The white was gone. The skin was pink and smooth and soft — like a baby's skin. He touched his face. His legs. Everywhere the disease had been, the skin was new.

Naaman stood in the middle of the Jordan River with water dripping from his beard. He turned his arms over in the sunlight. He pressed his fingers against his own skin and felt the pressure — actually felt it — for the first time in years.

His servants on the bank stared. Nobody said a word. The river flowed around Naaman's legs, brown and muddy and ordinary. And the commander of Syria's army stood in it, whole.
