A man sat upon a stone in open country, his staff laid across his knees, and he lifted his voice so that those who walked the road nearby could hear him. The words he spoke were measured, carried upon the dry wind that moved through the grass of the hillside.

He declared that the Lord was his shepherd. He said it plainly, the way a man states what he hath seen with his own eyes, and he opened his hands as he spoke, palms upward upon his knees.

He told of green pastures — not barren ground, not the cracked earth of the waste places, but grass thick and living, the kind that foldeth soft beneath the weight of a body laid down to rest. He said the Lord maketh him to lie down there. His voice slowed as he spoke it, and the wind bent the dry stalks around him, though the pastures he described were of another place, another provision.

Then he spoke of still waters. Not the rushing torrent that carrieth away the sheep, not the flood that breaketh the banks, but waters that lay quiet, waters led unto gently, where a man might kneel and drink without fear of the current. He said the Lord leadeth him beside such waters, and he made a motion with his hand, low and level, as one who traceth the surface of a calm pool.

He restored his soul, the man said. He spoke it as a thing accomplished, a thing already done, and he struck his staff lightly against the stone beneath him.

The man continued. He told of paths — right paths, paths of righteousness — and he said the Lord led him in them for his name's sake. He gestured outward along the road that wound between the hills, a road of packed dust and small stones, and those who listened turned their heads to follow the line of his hand.

Then his voice descended lower, and the air about him seemed to gather close. He spoke of the valley of the shadow of death. He named it without flinching, his jaw set, his eyes fixed forward upon the far ridge where the hills cast long shadows as the sun moved behind them. The valley, he said, was a place through which he walked. Not a place where he abode. He walked through it.

And he declared he would fear no evil. His hand closed around the head of his staff and he drew it upright beside him, planting its end against the ground. For thou art with me, he said, and his voice rose so that even those farthest along the road paused in their walking. Thy rod and thy staff, he said, they comfort me. He held his own staff before him as he spoke, turning it slowly in his grip, the wood worn smooth from years of use.

A stillness settled upon the hillside. No bird called. The wind held its breath between the stones.

Then the man spoke of a table. He said the Lord prepared a table before him in the presence of his enemies. He spread his hands apart as one who measureth the length of a board, and he looked to his left and to his right as though adversaries stood on every side, yet the table remained set, the bread laid out, the cup filled. He did not name the enemies. He did not count them. He spoke only of the table and its preparation, and of the Lord who set it.

He said his head was anointed with oil. He touched his own brow as he spoke, drawing his fingers across the skin above his eyes, and the gesture was slow and deliberate. The oil, he said, was given — not taken, not earned, but poured out upon him by another hand.

His cup, he declared, runneth over. He cupped both hands before him as though holding a vessel too full to carry without spilling, and he lifted them slightly, and his voice broke open with the weight of the word — runneth over — so that it carried down the hillside and across the road.

At the last, the man rose from the stone. He stood to his full height, staff in hand, and he spoke of what would follow him. Surely, he said — and the word was an anchor, a stake driven deep — surely goodness and mercy shall follow me all the days of my life. He did not say he would chase after them. He said they would follow him, as a shepherd's dogs follow at the heel, as the shadow followeth the body that casteth it.

And he said he would dwell in the house of the Lord for ever. He pointed with his staff toward no visible structure, no temple on the horizon, but he pointed nonetheless, arm extended, the end of the staff trembling slightly against the open air.

Then he lowered the staff. He gathered his cloak about his shoulders. The wind returned, pressing the grass flat against the hillside, and the dust rose in small curls along the road. Those who had stopped to listen shifted their weight, adjusted their bundles, and began again to walk. The man sat once more upon his stone, laid the staff across his knees, and was silent.
