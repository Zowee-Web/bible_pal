The vessel sat upon the waters, her timbers groaning beneath the weight of cargo and men, her sail drawn taut against the wind that had carried her forth from Joppa. The mariners moved about her deck with the practiced ease of men long acquainted with the sea, coiling ropes and checking the lashings that held the wares in place. The coast had fallen behind them, and there was nothing now but the grey expanse stretching in every direction, the smell of brine thick upon the air.

Then the Lord hurled a great wind upon the sea.

It did not come as winds commonly come, building slow from the horizon. It fell upon them. The surface of the water, which had been rolling in long and even swells, broke apart into jagged peaks. The ship lurched hard to one side, and men staggered where they stood, reaching for mast and rail and rope. The sail cracked like a whip above them, the fabric straining against its yard until the whole mast shuddered from base to top. Spray leapt over the bow in sheets, cold and stinging, drenching every man upon the deck.

A mighty tempest raged upon the sea, and the ship threatened to break apart.

The planks beneath their feet shivered and bucked. Water poured through the seams where the hull flexed against the force of the waves. Each swell lifted the vessel high, and each trough dropped her with a sound like the splitting of wood. The mariners cried out, every man unto his god. Their voices rose against the roar of the storm, calling upon names in tongues of Tyre and Sidon and the coasts beyond, arms raised toward a sky that had gone black with cloud. No answer came. The wind only grew.

They cast the wares that were in the ship into the sea, to lighten it of them. Barrels of oil rolled across the tilting deck and plunged over the side, swallowed instantly by the churning water. Bolts of dyed cloth, heavy with spray, were heaved by straining arms over the rail. Amphorae of wine shattered against the hull as they went. Crates of goods purchased at great cost tumbled into the deep, vanishing beneath the foam. The men labored with bleeding hands and salt-raw faces, throwing overboard everything they could lift, and still the storm did not abate.

But Jonah was gone down into the sides of the ship. He lay upon the inner hull in the lowest part of the vessel, where the ribs of the ship curved close overhead and the air was thick with the smell of pitch and damp wood. He was fast asleep. The roaring of the wind above, the crash of water against the planks beside his head, the shouts of desperate men — none of these had roused him. He lay still in that dark hold while the ship heaved and groaned around him.

The shipmaster came to him. His feet found the ladder in the dark, and he descended into the hold, bracing himself against the timbers as the vessel rolled. He found Jonah there, lying motionless. The shipmaster's voice was hoarse from shouting above the gale.

He called upon the sleeper to arise and cry unto his God, saying that perhaps this God would give thought unto them, that they perish not.

Jonah stirred. The shipmaster stood over him, water dripping from his garments onto the boards below. Above them, the hull shuddered again, and the sound of something heavy sliding across the deck echoed through the hold.

Upon the deck, the mariners gathered together, their garments soaked through and clinging to their limbs, their beards matted with salt. The storm had not relented. Every god they had called upon had given no relief. They spoke among themselves and determined to cast lots, that they might discover for whose cause this evil had come upon them.

They cast lots, and the lot fell upon Jonah.

Every face turned toward him. He stood among them now, the wind tearing at his robes, the deck pitching beneath his feet. The mariners pressed close, their voices sharp above the howling of the gale. They demanded of him an account — what was his occupation, whence he had come, what was his country, and of what people he was. Their questions came one upon another, urgent as the waves that broke against the bow.

Jonah answered them. He declared himself a Hebrew. He told them that he feared the Lord, the God of heaven, which hath made the sea and the dry land.

The men were exceedingly afraid when they heard these words. The ship pitched again, and several of them seized the rigging to keep their footing. They asked him what he had done, for they perceived now that he fled from the presence of the Lord, because he had told them so.

The waves mounted higher still. Green water swept the deck from stern to bow, dragging loose rope and shattered wood with it. The mast groaned, and somewhere below, a timber cracked with a sound that carried even above the storm. The mariners turned again to Jonah, shouting to be heard, and asked what they should do unto him, that the sea might be calm unto them. For the sea wrought and was tempestuous, growing worse with every passing moment.

Jonah answered them plainly. He told them to take him up and cast him forth into the sea, and then the sea would be calm unto them. He declared that he knew it was for his sake that this great tempest had come upon them.

Nevertheless, the men would not do it. Instead, they rowed hard to bring the ship to land. Their arms strained at the oars, muscles taut beneath wet skin, pulling against a sea that would not yield. The oars bent and shuddered with each stroke, and the water fought them, pushing the vessel back for every length they gained. Foam churned white around the blades. The shore, if it lay anywhere near, remained hidden behind walls of rain and spray. They could not prevail, for the sea wrought and was tempestuous against them, and every effort brought them no closer to dry ground.

At last they ceased their rowing. The oars hung limp in the water. The men stood gasping upon the deck, their chests heaving, their hands raw from the wood. The storm raged on without mercy, and the ship groaned as though her very frame would give way beneath the next wave.

They cried unto the Lord. Not unto the gods of Tyre, not unto the gods of Sidon, but unto the Lord God of Jonah. They besought Him, saying that they prayed He would not let them perish for this man's life, and that He would not lay upon them innocent blood, for He, the Lord, had done as it pleased Him.

Then they took up Jonah. Many hands gripped his arms and his garments. They carried him to the side of the ship, where the rail was slick with spray and the black water heaved below. The wind screamed across the deck. The men braced their feet against the planking.

They cast him forth into the sea.

The moment his body struck the water, the wind ceased. The waves, which had been rising in great walls of grey and green, began to settle. The surface of the deep, torn and churning only a breath before, grew still. The rain slackened and then stopped. The ship steadied beneath their feet, her timbers no longer groaning, her mast no longer swaying. Water drained from the scuppers in long streams, and the deck grew quiet.

The mariners stood where they were, drenched and trembling, staring at the sea that had gone calm around them. They feared the Lord exceedingly. There upon the wet and battered deck, they offered a sacrifice unto the Lord and made vows. The smoke of their offering rose into the air above the still water, and their voices carried across the silence where the storm had been.
