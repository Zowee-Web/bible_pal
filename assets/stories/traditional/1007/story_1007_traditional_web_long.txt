The ship had cleared the harbor at Joppa and moved out into open water, her timbers groaning as the hull settled into the rhythm of the sea. Sailors moved along the deck, hauling lines and checking the cargo lashed below. The sail caught wind and bellied outward, pulling the vessel toward Tarshish. Salt spray kicked up over the bow and misted across the weathered planks. Jonah had gone down into the inner part of the ship, into the hold where the air was close and smelled of pitch and damp wood. He lay down there in the darkness, and he fell into a deep sleep.

Then Yahweh hurled a great wind onto the sea.

It did not arrive gradually. One moment the water stretched out in long, rolling swells beneath a working sky. The next, the horizon darkened as if a curtain had been drawn across it, and the wind struck the ship broadside with a sound like tearing cloth. The mast shuddered. Rigging snapped taut and sang a high, thin note. The sea rose up in walls of gray-green water, foam streaking across their crests, and the ship pitched violently to starboard before swinging back. Waves broke over the gunwale and swept across the deck in sheets, pooling around the feet of the sailors and draining through the scuppers in white torrents.

The vessel shook from keel to masthead. Planks flexed and popped. Water forced its way through seams in the hull, trickling at first, then streaming in thin lines down the ribs of the hold. The cargo shifted with a heavy, grinding sound, crates and jars sliding against their lashings. The ship was in danger of breaking apart.

Every sailor cried out to his own god. Their voices rose above the roar of the storm — some shouting in languages the others could not understand, some dropping to their knees on the heaving deck, some lifting their hands toward the black sky with rain streaming down their arms. One man pressed his forehead against the mast, his lips moving rapidly. Another gripped the rail with both hands and called out a name that was lost in the wind.

Then they began to throw the cargo into the sea. Hands that had spent hours loading the ship now worked to empty it. Heavy jars of oil were wrestled up from below and heaved over the side, disappearing into the churning water without a sound that could be heard above the gale. Bales of goods, crates of trade items, sacks of grain — all of it went overboard. The sailors strained and shouted to one another, passing loads hand over hand, their tunics soaked through and clinging to their bodies. The deck ran with seawater and rain. Still the ship pitched and rolled, her hull groaning under the assault of each wave.

Below, in the lowest part of the vessel, Jonah remained asleep. The hold was dark. Water sloshed around the ribs of the hull and crept across the planking where he lay. The sound of the storm was muffled down there, reduced to a deep, rhythmic pounding and the creak of strained wood.

The shipmaster came down. He had to brace himself against the beams as the ship lurched, and he found Jonah lying in the hold, motionless. The shipmaster stood over him and spoke sharply.

"What do you mean, sleeper? Get up! Call on your God! Maybe your God will notice us, so that we won't perish."

Jonah rose.

Up on deck, the sailors gathered together, their faces slick with spray, their hair plastered flat. The storm had not relented. If anything, the waves had grown taller, and the intervals between them shorter, so the ship barely had time to right herself before the next one struck. The men stood in a rough circle, gripping ropes and rails to keep their footing.

They said to one another, "Come! Let's cast lots, so that we may know who is responsible for this evil that is on us."

They cast lots. The lot fell on Jonah.

Every face turned toward him. Rain drove sideways across the deck. The sail had been torn loose from one corner and cracked in the wind like a whip. The sailors pressed closer, their voices raised to be heard.

"Tell us, please, for whose cause this evil is on us. What is your occupation? Where do you come from? What is your country? Of what people are you?"

Jonah stood among them on the tilting deck, seawater running past his ankles. He answered them.

"I am a Hebrew, and I fear Yahweh, the God of heaven, who has made the sea and the dry land."

The men were exceedingly afraid. The ship dropped into a trough between two waves and the deck fell away beneath their feet before surging upward again. Spray exploded over the bow and drenched them all. They spoke to Jonah again, their voices strained.

"What have you done?"

For the men knew that he was fleeing from the presence of Yahweh, because he had told them.

The storm continued to build. The waves crashed against the hull with a sound like stone striking stone. Timber cracked somewhere below. The rigging hummed and vibrated. Foam poured across the deck in thick sheets, and the sailors had to hook their arms through the ropes to keep from being swept away.

They said to him, "What shall we do to you, that the sea may be calm for us?"

The water surged higher. The ship rolled so far to port that the rail dipped below the surface before she came back. Men stumbled and caught themselves against one another.

Jonah said to them, "Take me up and throw me into the sea. Then the sea will be calm for you, for I know that because of me this great storm is on you."

But the men did not do it. Instead, they rowed hard to bring the ship back to land. They dug the oars into the heaving water, their backs bending with the effort, their hands white on the oar shafts. Muscles stood out on their arms and shoulders. They pulled and pulled, grunting with each stroke, but the sea worked against them. Every wave pushed the vessel farther out. The oars caught in the crests and were nearly torn from their grips. Water poured over the stern. The sea grew more and more tempestuous against them, and they could not make headway.

They stopped rowing. The oars hung loose in the oarlocks, dripping. The men stood on the flooded deck, chests heaving, arms trembling from the effort. The storm raged around them — wind screaming through what remained of the rigging, waves towering above the gunwales, the hull shuddering with every impact.

They cried out to Yahweh. Their voices carried across the roaring water.

"We beg you, Yahweh, we beg you, don't let us die for this man's life, and don't lay on us innocent blood; for you, Yahweh, have done as it pleased you."

Then they picked Jonah up. Hands gripped his arms, his clothing, his body. They carried him to the rail. The sea churned below — dark water streaked with foam, rising and falling in violent swells. The wind tore at their garments and flung spray into their faces.

They threw Jonah into the sea.

The moment his body struck the water, the sea stopped its raging. The waves that had been climbing higher and higher flattened and spread. The wind dropped. The surface of the water smoothed out, still heaving in long, diminishing swells but no longer breaking. The terrible sound — that roar that had filled every space on the ship and in the air around it — faded. The torn sail hung limp. Water drained from the deck through the scuppers in steady streams, and the wood beneath the sailors' feet became still.

The men stood in the sudden quiet. Their clothing dripped. Their hands were raw from the ropes and oars. The ship rocked gently now, settling, her timbers ticking and popping as the strain released.

Then the men feared Yahweh exceedingly. They offered a sacrifice to Yahweh and made vows. The smell of the offering rose from the deck of the battered ship into the calm air, and the sea lay flat and quiet all around them.
