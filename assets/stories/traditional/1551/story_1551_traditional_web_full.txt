The tribes of Israel gathered at Shechem. They came from every territory — Judah and Ephraim, Manasseh and Dan, Simeon and Zebulun, Asher and Issachar, Benjamin and Naphtali, Reuben and Gad. They came with their families, their animals, their tents and provisions. The roads into Shechem filled with dust and the sound of feet. The valley between Mount Ebal and Mount Gerizim held them all, and the air smelled of dry grass and livestock and cook fires.

The elders came. The judges came. The officers came. Every leader of every tribe and clan presented himself, and together the whole nation stood before God in that place.

Joshua stood before them.

He was old. His years were many, and every man and woman there knew it. He had crossed the Jordan at the head of the people when the waters stood in a heap. He had marched around the walls of Jericho. He had fought at Gibeon, where the sun held still in the sky. He had taken the hill country and the lowlands and the Negev. He had divided the land among the tribes, measuring it out by lot, territory by territory. Now he stood at Shechem with the sun on his face and the whole nation spread out before him on the open ground.

He spoke.

"Fear the Lord," Joshua said. His voice carried in the still air. "Serve him with sincerity and truth."

The crowd was silent. Thousands of faces turned toward the old commander.

"Put away the gods your fathers served beyond the River," he said. "Put away the gods they served in Egypt. Serve the Lord."

He stopped. The wind came through the valley and bent the dry grass on the hillsides. A child cried somewhere in the crowd and was hushed. Joshua waited. He let the silence build until it pressed against the assembly like a hand on the chest.

Then he spoke again, and his voice did not waver.

"If it seems evil to you to serve the Lord," Joshua said, "then choose today whom you will serve."

He swept his hand wide, a slow gesture that took in the whole land around them — the hills, the valleys, the distant ridgelines.

"The gods your fathers served beyond the River. Or the gods of the Amorites, in whose land you now live."

He lowered his hand. His shoulders were square. His jaw was set. His eyes moved across the faces of the people — the old men who remembered Egypt, the young men who had been born in the wilderness, the women, the children standing at their mothers' sides.

"But as for me and my house," Joshua said, "we will serve the Lord."

The words fell across the assembly. No one moved. No one spoke. The only sound was the wind and the breathing of animals tethered at the edges of the crowd. Joshua stood with both feet planted on the ground, his arms at his sides, and he did not look away from the people.

Then the people answered.

It began as a murmur, voices near the front, then it spread and grew until the whole assembly spoke.

"God forbid," they said, "that we should leave the Lord to serve other gods."

The words came from every part of the crowd, from the elders on the left and the families on the right and the officers standing in their ranks.

"The Lord our God," the people said, "he is the one who brought us and our fathers out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery."

Men lifted their hands as they spoke. Women held their children closer. The words rolled across the valley floor.

"He did those great signs before our eyes," they said. "He kept us safe on every road we walked. He kept us safe among every people we passed through."

The assembly pressed forward, the way a field of grain leans when the wind comes from one direction. Their faces were turned toward Joshua, and their voices were strong.

"The Lord drove out before us all the peoples," the people said. "Even the Amorites who lived in this land."

The hills caught the sound and held it. The sun stood high and white above Shechem. Dust hung in the air where thousands of feet had shifted on dry ground.

"We also will serve the Lord," the people said, "for he is our God."

Joshua stood before them. The whole nation of Israel stood before him. The valley was full of their voices, and the wind carried the sound up into the hills and away.
