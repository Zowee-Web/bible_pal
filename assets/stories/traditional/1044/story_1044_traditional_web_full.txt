Ruth and Naomi had walked a long way to get to Bethlehem. Naomi was old and tired. Her husband was dead. Her two sons were dead. All she had left was Ruth — a young woman from Moab who had refused to leave her side.

They arrived at the beginning of the barley harvest. The fields around the little town were golden and full. Workers moved through the rows with sharp curved blades, cutting the ripe stalks. The air smelled like warm grain and dust.

Ruth and Naomi had no money. No food. No land. But Ruth was not the kind of person who sat still and waited.

"Please let me go to the field," Ruth said, "and glean among the ears of grain after the harvesters."

"Go, my daughter," Naomi said.

Ruth went out early. She walked along the dusty road until she came to a field of barley. She did not know whose field it was. She bent down, picked up a few stalks that the harvesters had missed. Stood up. Moved to the next spot. Bent down again. Over and over, all morning long. The sun beat down on her head. Her back ached. But she kept going.

The field belonged to a man named Boaz. He was wealthy and respected — a relative of Naomi's late husband. He was also kind.

Boaz arrived from Bethlehem to check on the harvest. "The Lord be with you!" he said to his workers.

"The Lord bless you!" they shouted back.

Then Boaz noticed Ruth working at the edge of the field. She moved steadily along the rows, never stopping. "Whose young woman is this?" he asked his foreman.

"She is the Moabite woman who came back with Naomi," the servant answered. "She came early this morning and asked if she could pick up grain behind the harvesters. She has been on her feet ever since. She has barely rested."

Boaz walked over to Ruth. She straightened up, surprised. "My daughter," he said gently, "listen to me. Do not go to any other field. Stay here close to my young women. I have told the young men not to bother you. When you are thirsty, go drink from the water jars."

Ruth dropped to her knees and bowed her face to the ground. "Why are you being so kind to me?" she asked. "I am a foreigner."

Boaz smiled. "I have heard about everything you have done for your mother-in-law since your husband died," he said. "How you left your own father and mother and your homeland and came to a people you did not know before. May the Lord reward your work. May you receive a full reward from the Lord, the God of Israel, under whose wings you have come to take refuge."

At mealtime, Boaz called Ruth over. "Come here," he said. "Eat some bread and dip it in the vinegar." Ruth sat down beside the workers. Boaz handed her roasted grain — warm and crunchy. She ate until she was full, and she still had food left over. She tucked the extra away for Naomi.

When Ruth got up to work again, Boaz pulled his young men aside. "Let her pick up grain even among the sheaves," he said quietly. "And pull out some stalks from the bundles on purpose and leave them for her. Do not say anything to her about it."

Ruth worked until evening. The sun turned orange and sank toward the hills. Long shadows stretched across the stubble. She gathered everything she had gleaned and beat the barley loose from the stalks. She had about an ephah — a huge amount for one day. She lifted the heavy bundle onto her shoulder and carried it home.

Naomi was waiting at the door. Her eyes went wide when she saw the grain. "Where did you work today? Blessed be the man who noticed you!"

Ruth set the bundle down. She brought out the leftover roasted grain too. "The man's name is Boaz," she said.

Naomi's hand went to her mouth. Her eyes filled with tears. "May he be blessed by the Lord, who has not stopped showing his kindness to the living and to the dead. This man is a close relative of ours — one of our family redeemers."

Ruth said, "He told me to stay close to his workers until the harvest is finished."

"It is good, my daughter," Naomi said. "Stay with his young women."

So Ruth stayed close to the young women of Boaz. She gleaned in his field through the barley harvest and the wheat harvest after that. Every evening she came home with arms full of grain. Every evening Naomi smiled a little wider. They had food. They had a friend. And they had hope.
