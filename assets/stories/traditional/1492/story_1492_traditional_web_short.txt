The decree was already signed, and Daniel had read it before he climbed the stairs. He went into his house and crossed to the upper room. The windows there stood open toward Jerusalem as they had stood for as long as he had lived in the king's city. He knelt on the stone, three times in the day as he had always knelt, and he gave thanks before his God.

The men who had been watching for him gathered at the courtyard wall. They saw the bent shape at the window. They went to the king with the law in their mouths.

The king's face changed when he heard. He labored at the matter until the sun had gone down behind the palace gardens, looking for a way to lift the seal he had set with his own hand. The conspirators came back at the going down of the day, and they reminded him that a decree of the Medes and Persians did not turn. The king commanded, and Daniel was brought, and they cast him into the den of lions.

A stone was rolled across the mouth of the den. The king pressed his own signet into the soft clay, and the signets of his lords beside it, and the seal hardened in the evening air. He spoke to Daniel through the stone before he turned away. "Your God whom you serve continually," he said, "he will deliver you."

The king went up to his palace and would not eat. He sent the musicians out of the chamber. Sleep fled from him. The window of his own room faced east, and the night went on a long time before the sky began to thin above the gardens.

He went down to the den very early, before the city was awake. He cried out with a troubled voice at the mouth of the stone. "Daniel, servant of the living God, is your God whom you serve continually able to deliver you from the lions?"

The voice that answered was steady through the stone. "O king, live forever. My God sent his angel and shut the lions' mouths."

They opened the den. Daniel walked out into the king's morning, and no kind of harm was found upon him.
