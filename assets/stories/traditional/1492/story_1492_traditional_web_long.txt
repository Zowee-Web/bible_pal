The decree had been signed in the afternoon. Daniel had stood in the king's hall when it was read. He had said nothing. The satraps had watched his face when the conditions of the writing were spoken aloud. Every petition for thirty days was to be made to the king alone. Every man who broke the decree was to be cast into the den of lions. They had been watching for a way to bring him down for a long time. Now they had found one.

He walked out of the hall when it was finished. He walked through the streets of the city the way he had walked them every other afternoon. He went home.

The upper room of his house was the room in which he prayed. The windows there stood open toward Jerusalem. They had stood open every day since the year the captivity had brought him into the king's service. He went into the room. He knelt on the stone where he had knelt every day for forty years. He gave thanks before his God three times in the day, as he had always done. He did not change anything.

The men who had been watching for him gathered at the courtyard wall. They saw the bent shape at the window. They went to the king with the law in their mouths. The king's face changed when he heard. He had set his heart on Daniel for a long time. He labored at the matter until the sun had gone down behind the palace gardens. He was looking for a way to lift the seal he had set with his own hand. The conspirators came back at the going down of the day. They reminded him that a decree of the Medes and Persians did not turn. The king commanded. They brought Daniel. They cast him into the den of lions.

A stone was rolled across the mouth of the den. The king pressed his own signet into the soft clay. The signets of his lords were pressed beside it. The seal hardened in the evening air. The torches lit the stone with a yellow light. The king spoke to Daniel through the stone before he turned away. "Your God whom you serve continually," he said. "He will deliver you." Then he turned and went up the steps to his palace.

The king would not eat that night. He sent the musicians out of the chamber. He sat in his own room with the door closed. The lamps were not lit. He did not call for wine. He sat in the dark and listened for the lions in the country below the palace. Sometime in the long hours of the night he stood at his window. He looked out across the gardens. The night was very still. The lions were silent below the palace, and that was not what they did. He listened a long time. He heard nothing. He sat back down on the edge of his bed and waited.

In the den below, Daniel had been kneeling on the floor of the place since the men had set him down on the dirt at the bottom of the pit. The lions were near him. He could feel their breath in the dark. He could hear them moving when one of them shifted on the floor of the den. Nothing else happened to him. The hours of the night passed in the country below the palace. He did not sleep. He prayed where he was. The same prayer he had prayed in the upper room at the going down of the sun was the prayer he prayed in the den. The lions did not come near him. They lay in the dark around him and did not approach.

The king went down to the den very early, before the city was awake. The torch-bearers walked ahead of him on the stone steps. The stone of the king's seal was still in place at the mouth. He stood before it. He cried out with a troubled voice. He cried the way a man cries who has not yet allowed himself to hope.

"Daniel," he said. "Servant of the living God. Is your God whom you serve continually able to deliver you from the lions?"

The voice that answered was steady through the stone. It came up out of the den the way the answer comes out of any place where a man is still alive.

"O king, live forever. My God sent his angel. He shut the lions' mouths. They have not hurt me. Innocence was found in me before him. And before you, O king, I have done no wrong."

The king was exceedingly glad. He gave the command at once. They broke the seal. They rolled the stone aside. Daniel walked up out of the den. The morning was very fresh on the upper stones. No kind of harm was found upon him. The king embraced him in the doorway. They stood there together a long moment in the rising light.

Daniel went home through the city. The streets were beginning to wake. The watchmen at the city gate had not yet heard the news. They looked up at him passing as they had always looked up at him. He went into his house. He climbed to the upper room. The windows still stood open toward Jerusalem. He knelt on the stone where he had knelt the day before. He gave thanks before his God as he had always done. The lions far below the palace were quiet for the rest of that morning.
