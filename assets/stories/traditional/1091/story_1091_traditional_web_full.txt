King Nebuchadnezzar built an image of gold on the plain of Dura, in the province of Babylon. It stood ninety feet tall and nine feet wide, gleaming in the sun like a column of fire. He set it up in the open ground where it could be seen from every direction, its surface catching the light and throwing it back across the dusty plain.

Then the king sent word throughout the empire. He summoned every official — the governors, the captains, the judges, the treasurers, the counselors, the magistrates, and all the rulers of the provinces. They came from every corner of the kingdom and gathered on the plain before the golden image, standing in long, ordered rows.

A herald cried out in a loud voice: "To you it is commanded, O peoples, nations, and languages, that when you hear the sound of the horn, the flute, the harp, the lyre, and every kind of music, you shall fall down and worship the golden image that King Nebuchadnezzar has set up. Whoever does not fall down and worship shall immediately be thrown into a burning fiery furnace."

The music played. The instruments sounded across the plain. And all the people fell down — every head bowed, every knee bent, every forehead pressed against the dust.

But three men did not bow.

Their names were Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego. They were Jews — exiles from the land of Israel, brought to Babylon as captives and appointed to serve in the king's government. They stood upright on the plain while every other person lay flat on the ground around them. They did not bend. They did not kneel. They stood still.

Certain officials noticed and brought the report to Nebuchadnezzar. The king was enraged. He summoned the three men before him. His face was dark with fury.

"Is it true, Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego?" he said. "You do not serve my gods or worship the golden image I have set up?" He leaned forward. "I will give you one more chance. When the music plays, if you fall down and worship, very well. But if you do not, you shall be thrown immediately into a burning fiery furnace. And what god will be able to deliver you from my hand?"

The three men stood before the king. They did not flinch. They did not look at one another for reassurance.

"O Nebuchadnezzar," they said, "we have no need to answer you in this matter. If we are thrown into the burning fiery furnace, the God whom we serve is able to deliver us from it, and he will deliver us out of your hand, O king. But even if he does not — we want you to know that we will not serve your gods or worship the golden image you have set up."

The king's face twisted with rage. He ordered the furnace heated seven times hotter than it had ever been heated. He commanded the strongest soldiers in his army to bind Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego. They were tied up in their robes and turbans and thrown into the flames. The furnace was so hot that the fire killed the soldiers who threw them in.

But Nebuchadnezzar leaped from his throne. He stared into the furnace with wide eyes. "Did we not cast three men, bound, into the fire?" he said to his counselors.

"True, O king," they said.

"But I see four men," the king said, "walking in the midst of the fire, unbound, unharmed. And the appearance of the fourth is like a son of the gods."

Nebuchadnezzar approached the mouth of the furnace. The heat pressed against him. "Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego, servants of the Most High God — come out!" he called.

They walked out of the fire. The governors and captains and officials crowded around them, examining them closely. Their hair was not singed. Their robes were not scorched. Their skin was untouched. There was no smell of fire on them — not even the faintest trace of smoke.

Nebuchadnezzar spoke before the assembly. "Blessed be the God of Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego," he said, "who sent his angel and delivered his servants who trusted in him. They defied the king's command and yielded up their bodies rather than serve or worship any god except their own God."
