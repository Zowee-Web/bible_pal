Nebuchadnezzar the king made an image of gold and set it upon the plain of Dura, in the province of Babylon. It stood ninety feet in height and nine feet in breadth, and it gleamed in the sun as a column of fire. He set it up in the open ground where it might be seen from every quarter, its surface catching the light and throwing it back across the dusty plain.

Then the king sent word throughout the empire. He summoned every official — the princes, the governors, the captains, the judges, the treasurers, the counsellors, the sheriffs, and all the rulers of the provinces. They came from every corner of the kingdom and gathered upon the plain before the golden image, standing in long, ordered rows.

A herald cried aloud: "To you it is commanded, O people, nations, and languages, that at what time ye hear the sound of the cornet, flute, harp, sackbut, psaltery, dulcimer, and all kinds of musick, ye shall fall down and worship the golden image that Nebuchadnezzar the king hath set up. And whoso falleth not down and worshippeth shall the same hour be cast into the midst of a burning fiery furnace."

The musick played. The instruments sounded across the plain. And all the people fell down — every head bowed, every knee bent, every forehead pressed against the dust.

But three men bowed not.

Their names were Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego. They were Jews — captives from the land of Israel, brought to Babylon when Nebuchadnezzar took Jerusalem. They had been set over the affairs of the province. But they had not forgotten the God of their fathers.

Certain Chaldeans came before the king and accused the Jews. The king was full of rage. He commanded Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego to be brought before him.

"Is it true?" he demanded. "Ye do not serve my gods, nor worship the golden image which I have set up?" His face was dark with fury. "I will give you one chance more. When ye hear the musick, if ye fall down and worship — well. But if ye worship not, ye shall be cast the same hour into the midst of a burning fiery furnace; and who is that God that shall deliver you out of my hands?"

The three men stood before the king. They did not flinch.

"O Nebuchadnezzar," they said, "we are not careful to answer thee in this matter. If it be so, our God whom we serve is able to deliver us from the burning fiery furnace, and he will deliver us out of thine hand, O king. But if not — be it known unto thee, O king, that we will not serve thy gods, nor worship the golden image which thou hast set up."

The form of the king's visage was changed. He commanded the furnace heated seven times more than it was wont to be heated. He commanded the most mighty men in his army to bind them. They were bound in their coats, their hosen, their hats, and their other garments, and cast into the midst of the burning fiery furnace. The furnace was exceeding hot, and the flame slew the men that took them up.

Then Nebuchadnezzar the king was astonied, and rose up in haste. "Did not we cast three men bound into the midst of the fire?" he said unto his counsellors.

"True, O king."

"Lo," said he, "I see four men loose, walking in the midst of the fire, and they have no hurt; and the form of the fourth is like the Son of God."

Nebuchadnezzar came near to the mouth of the furnace. "Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego, ye servants of the most high God, come forth, and come hither," he said.

They came forth from the midst of the fire. The princes, governors, and captains gathered about them. Their hair was not singed. Their coats were not changed. Neither was the smell of fire upon them.

Nebuchadnezzar spake and said, "Blessed be the God of Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego, who hath sent his angel, and delivered his servants that trusted in him, and have changed the king's word, and yielded their bodies, that they might not serve nor worship any god, except their own God."
