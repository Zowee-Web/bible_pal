The plain of Dura lay flat and wide without the walls of Babylon. The ground was hard-packed earth, dry and pale, strewn with loose stones and tufts of dead grass. To the west, the walls of the city rose in massive tiers of brick, the hanging gardens visible above the ramparts, their green terraces bright against the brown. To the east, the plain stretched on toward the distant hills, shimmering in the heat.

In the midst of the plain stood an image of gold. King Nebuchadnezzar had commanded it made — ninety feet in height, nine feet in breadth — and it caught the sun and flung the light back across the flat ground in a blinding glare. It was not subtle. It was not modest. It was a declaration — a monument unto power, set where every eye in the province might behold it.

The king sent heralds throughout the empire. Every man of rank was summoned — princes, governors, captains, judges, treasurers, counsellors, sheriffs, and all the rulers of the provinces. They came from across the kingdom and gathered upon the plain in long rows, their robes bright with the colours of their offices, their sandals caked with the dust of the road.

A herald stepped forth and cried with a loud voice: "To you it is commanded, O people, nations, and languages, that at what time ye hear the sound of the cornet, flute, harp, sackbut, psaltery, dulcimer, and all kinds of musick, ye shall fall down and worship the golden image that Nebuchadnezzar the king hath set up: and whoso falleth not down and worshippeth shall the same hour be cast into the midst of a burning fiery furnace."

The instruments sounded. The musick rose across the plain in a great wave of sound. And the people fell. Every head was bowed. Every knee was bent. Every forehead was pressed into the dust. The plain became a sea of prostrate forms, row upon row, silent and still.

But three men bowed not.

Their names were Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego. They were Jews — young men carried captive to Babylon when Nebuchadnezzar conquered Jerusalem. They had been taught the tongue and learning of Babylon. They had been given places in the king's government. But they had not forgotten the God of their fathers, and they had not forgotten the first commandment: Thou shalt have no other gods before me.

They stood upright upon the plain. All about them lay flat upon the ground. The sun beat down. The golden image blazed. The musick ceased. And the three men stood.

Certain of the Chaldeans — men that bore ill will toward the Jews and their influence in the government — took note at once. They came before the king. "O king, live for ever," they said. "Thou hast made a decree that every man must fall down and worship the golden image. But there are certain Jews — Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego — whom thou hast set over the affairs of the province. They serve not thy gods. They worship not the golden image."

Nebuchadnezzar was full of fury. His visage was changed. He commanded the three men brought before him.

"Is it of purpose?" he demanded. "Ye serve not my gods? Ye worship not the image which I have set up?" He leaned forward upon his throne, his voice tight with wrath. "I will give you one chance more. When ye hear the musick, if ye fall down and worship — well. But if ye worship not, ye shall be cast the same hour into the midst of a burning fiery furnace." He paused. "And who is that God that shall deliver you out of my hands?"

The three men stood before the most powerful king in all the earth. The furnace burned behind them — they could feel the heat upon their backs, hear the roar of the flames. The court watched in strained silence.

Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego answered the king. Their voices were steady.

"O Nebuchadnezzar, we are not careful to answer thee in this matter. If it be so, our God whom we serve is able to deliver us from the burning fiery furnace, and he will deliver us out of thine hand, O king."

They paused. Then they spake again.

"But if not — be it known unto thee, O king — we will not serve thy gods, nor worship the golden image which thou hast set up."

The words hung in the air. But if not.

The form of the king's visage was changed against them. He commanded the furnace heated seven times more than it was wont to be heated. He called for the mightiest men in his army. They bound Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego — in their coats, their hosen, their hats, and their other garments — and bore them toward the mouth of the furnace. The heat that poured forth was so fierce that the flame of the fire slew those men that took up the three.

The bound men fell into the fire.

Nebuchadnezzar sat upon his throne, watching. Then he rose up in haste. The fury was gone from his face, and in its place was something near unto astonishment.

"Did not we cast three men bound into the midst of the fire?" he said unto his counsellors.

"True, O king."

"Lo," said the king, and his voice was unsteady. "I see four men loose, walking in the midst of the fire, and they have no hurt; and the form of the fourth is like the Son of God."

He drew near unto the mouth of the furnace. The heat pressed against his face. He cried aloud:

"Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego, ye servants of the most high God, come forth, and come hither!"

They walked forth from the midst of the fire. They stepped through the opening and stood before the assembly upon the plain of Dura. The princes, governors, captains, and counsellors gathered about them, beholding them closely. The hair of their heads was not singed. Their coats were not changed. Neither had the smell of fire passed upon them — not so much as a trace of smoke.

Nebuchadnezzar stood before the assembly. His voice carried across the plain.

"Blessed be the God of Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego," he said, "who hath sent his angel, and delivered his servants that trusted in him, and have changed the king's word, and yielded their bodies, that they might not serve nor worship any god, except their own God."

The golden image stood yet upon the plain, gleaming in the sun. But the three men stood beside it, unburned, untouched, and the crowd looked from the image to the men and again to the image, and no man spake.
