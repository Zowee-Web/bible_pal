The plain of Dura stretched flat and wide outside the city of Babylon. The ground was hard-packed earth, dry and pale, scattered with loose stones and tufts of dead grass. To the west, the walls of Babylon rose in massive tiers of mud brick, the hanging gardens visible above the ramparts, their green terraces bright against the brown of the city. To the east, the plain ran on toward the distant hills, shimmering in the heat.

In the center of the plain stood a golden image. King Nebuchadnezzar had ordered it built — ninety feet tall, nine feet wide — and it caught the sun and threw the light back across the flat ground in a blinding glare. It was not subtle. It was not modest. It was a declaration, a monument to power, planted where every eye in the province could see it.

The king sent heralds throughout the empire. Every official of rank was summoned — governors, captains, judges, treasurers, counselors, magistrates, and all the rulers of the provinces. They came from across the kingdom and gathered on the plain in long, ordered rows, their robes bright with the colors of their offices, their sandals caked with the dust of the road.

A herald stepped forward and cried out in a loud voice: "To you it is commanded, O peoples, nations, and languages: when you hear the sound of the horn, the flute, the harp, the lyre, and every kind of music, you shall fall down and worship the golden image that King Nebuchadnezzar has set up. Whoever does not fall down and worship shall immediately be cast into a burning fiery furnace."

The instruments sounded. The music rolled across the plain — horns and strings and flutes, rising together in a wave of sound. And the people fell. Every head bowed. Every knee bent. Every forehead pressed into the dust. The plain of Dura became a sea of prostrate bodies, row after row, silent and still.

But three men did not bow.

Their names were Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego. They were Jews — exiles from the land of Israel, young men carried to Babylon as captives when Nebuchadnezzar conquered Jerusalem. They had been trained in the language and learning of Babylon. They had been given positions in the king's government. But they had not forgotten the God of their fathers, and they had not forgotten the first commandment: You shall have no other gods before me.

They stood upright on the plain. Everyone around them lay flat. The sun beat down. The golden image blazed. The music faded. And the three men stood.

Certain officials — men who resented the Jews and their influence in the government — noticed at once. They went to the king. "O king, live forever," they said. "You gave a decree that every man must fall down and worship the golden image. But there are certain Jews — Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego — whom you appointed over the affairs of the province. They do not serve your gods. They do not worship the golden image."

Nebuchadnezzar was furious. His face darkened. He summoned the three men before him.

"Is it true?" he demanded. "You do not serve my gods? You do not worship the image I have set up?" He leaned forward on his throne, his voice tight with controlled rage. "I will give you one more chance. When the music plays, if you fall down and worship — very well. But if you do not, you will be thrown immediately into a burning fiery furnace." He paused. "And what god will be able to deliver you from my hand?"

The three men stood before the most powerful man in the known world. The furnace burned behind them — they could feel the heat on their backs, hear the roar of the flames. The court watched in tense silence.

Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego answered the king. Their voices were steady.

"O Nebuchadnezzar, we have no need to answer you in this matter. If it be so, the God whom we serve is able to deliver us from the burning fiery furnace, and he will deliver us out of your hand, O king."

They paused. Then Meshach spoke again — or perhaps all three together.

"But even if he does not — we want you to know, O king — we will not serve your gods or worship the golden image you have set up."

The words hung in the air. Even if he does not.

Nebuchadnezzar's face twisted. He commanded the furnace heated seven times beyond its usual temperature. He called for the strongest soldiers in his army. They bound Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego — still in their robes and turbans and trousers — and carried them toward the mouth of the furnace. The heat pouring from the opening was so intense that the soldiers who lifted the three men were killed by the flames before they could step back.

The bound men fell into the fire.

Nebuchadnezzar sat on his throne, watching. And then he leaped to his feet. His face had changed. The fury was gone, replaced by something close to shock.

"Did we not cast three men, bound, into the fire?" he said to his counselors.

"True, O king."

"I see four men," Nebuchadnezzar said. His voice was unsteady. "They are walking in the midst of the fire. They are unbound. They are unharmed. And the fourth — the fourth looks like a son of the gods."

He approached the mouth of the furnace. The heat pressed against his face. He shouted into the flames.

"Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego — servants of the Most High God — come out! Come here!"

They walked out of the fire. They stepped through the opening of the furnace and stood before the crowd on the plain of Dura. The governors and captains and officials pressed close, examining them. Their hair was not singed. Their robes were not scorched. Their skin was unmarked. There was no smell of fire on them — not even a trace of smoke.

Nebuchadnezzar stood before the assembly. His voice carried across the plain.

"Blessed be the God of Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego," he said, "who sent his angel and delivered his servants who trusted in him. They defied the king's command and yielded up their bodies rather than serve or worship any god except their own God."

The golden image still stood on the plain, gleaming in the sun. But the three men stood beside it, unburned, untouched, and the crowd looked from the image to the men and back again, and no one spoke.
