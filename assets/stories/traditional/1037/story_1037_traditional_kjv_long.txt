Jacob dwelt in the land of Canaan, in the land wherein his father was a stranger. Joseph was seventeen years old. He fed the flock with the sons of Bilhah and the sons of Zilpah, and he brought their report unto his father. His brethren knew the lad was watching them. They knew what he carried home.

Now Israel loved Joseph more than all his sons, because he was the son of his old age. He made him a coat of many colours. The fabric was fine. The stitching was careful. When Joseph wore it among the tents, the colours caught the light. It was a garment no man could miss.

His brethren beheld the coat. They saw their father's hand in the cutting of it. They hated him. They could not speak peaceably unto him.

Joseph dreamed a dream. He came unto his brethren in the open air, his face yet bright with sleep. "Hear, I pray you, this dream which I have dreamed. We were binding sheaves in the field. My sheaf arose and stood upright. Your sheaves came round about and made obeisance to my sheaf."

His brethren looked upon him. The work of their hands went still. "Shalt thou indeed reign over us? Shalt thou indeed have dominion over us?" They hated him yet the more for his dreams and for his words.

He dreamed yet again. This time he told his father also. Jacob heard him out, then set down that which he held. "What is this dream that thou hast dreamed? Shall I and thy mother and thy brethren indeed come to bow down ourselves before thee?" Jacob's voice was sharp. His brethren stood at the edge of the tent and hearkened. They envied him. But his father observed the saying.

His brethren went to feed the flock in Shechem. The pastures there were wide and the grass yet green, and they were many days from the vale of Hebron.

Jacob said unto Joseph, "Do not thy brethren feed the flock in Shechem? Come, I will send thee unto them."

Joseph said, "Here am I."

"Go, I pray thee," said Jacob. "See whether it be well with thy brethren and well with the flock, and bring me word again." He sent him forth out of the vale of Hebron, and the lad went northward in the coat of many colours.

Joseph came to Shechem and wandered in the open field. The flock was not there. He turned in a slow circle, looking. A certain man found him among the empty pastures. "What seekest thou?"

"I seek my brethren. Tell me, I pray thee, where they feed the flock."

"They are departed hence," the man said. "I heard them say, 'Let us go to Dothan.'"

Joseph went after his brethren and found them in Dothan. He came up over a low rise toward them, the coat moving with his steps.

They saw him afar off. The shape upon the brown hillside was unmistakable. One of them lifted a hand and pointed. The others turned. The work of their hands fell idle. "Behold, this dreamer cometh." Voices rose among them, low and quick. "Let us slay him and cast him into one of the pits. We will say some evil beast hath devoured him. And we shall see what will become of his dreams."

Reuben heard it. The colour drained from his face. "Let us not take his life," he said. He spake quickly, his eyes moving over them. "Shed no blood. Cast him into this pit that is in the wilderness, but lay no hand upon him." He purposed to come again later and lift him out. He purposed to bring him home.

Joseph came nigh. He raised a hand in greeting. No hand was raised in return. Their faces were set. He slackened his pace. He kept walking. And when he was come unto them, they laid hold of him.

They stripped the coat from his shoulders. They drew it over his head, and his arms came free of the sleeves last. The colours went limp in their hands. They lifted him and bore him unto the mouth of a pit and let him down into it. The pit was empty. There was no water in it. The walls were dry stone. The opening above him was a circle of sky.

They sat down to eat bread.

Joseph, below them, could hear the breaking of the loaf. He could hear the chewing. He could hear his brethren speak one to another of ordinary things, the flock and the road and the weather, as though nought had happened above him. He called out. The voices above answered not. He called again. The bread brake again. The circle of sky held still.

The brethren lifted up their eyes and looked, and they beheld a company of Ishmeelites coming from Gilead. The camels came on slowly through the heat. Their packs were heavy with spicery and balm and myrrh, going down to Egypt. The bells upon their harnesses rang faintly across the field.

Judah watched them. He spake at last. "What profit is it if we slay our brother and conceal his blood? Come, let us sell him to the Ishmeelites, and let not our hand be upon him, for he is our brother, our flesh." His brethren hearkened unto him.

Midianite merchantmen passed by. The brethren rose up from their meal and went unto the pit. They reached down. Hands gripped Joseph's arms and drew him up out of the dry stones into the daylight. He blinked against the sun. The coins changed hands at the side of the road. Twenty pieces of silver. The Ishmeelites set him upon his feet and turned him toward Egypt. He went with them. The coloured coat remained behind in the dust.

Reuben was not with them. He came again unto the pit later, alone, and stood at its edge and looked down. The pit was empty. He turned in place. He looked upon the field. He took hold of his garment and rent it from his throat down unto his girdle. He came running unto his brethren. "The child is not. And I, whither shall I go?"

They took the coat from where it lay. They killed a kid of the goats and dipped the coat in the warm blood. The blood soaked the fine fabric and darkened the colours. They carried it home.

They brought it unto their father. They held it forth at arm's length. "This have we found. Know now whether it be thy son's coat or no."

Jacob took the coat in his hands. The blood was already dry upon it. He looked upon the colours he himself had chosen. He looked upon the stitching he had given to be done. His knees gave a little. He knew it straightway. "It is my son's coat. An evil beast hath devoured him. Joseph is without doubt rent in pieces."

Jacob rent his garments. He put sackcloth upon his loins. He sat down upon the ground and would not rise.

All his sons and all his daughters rose up to comfort him. They came one by one and stood near him. They spake gently. He turned his face away. He refused to be comforted. "I will go down into the grave unto my son mourning." Thus his father wept for him many days.

And far from the tent of his father, far from the brethren who had eaten bread above his pit, Joseph walked beside the camels of the Ishmeelites in the long line bound for Egypt. The Midianites brought him into Egypt, and there they sold him unto Potiphar, an officer of Pharaoh, the captain of the guard. The lad who had dreamed was carried out of the land of his father's sojourning, and the saying his father had observed went on living.
