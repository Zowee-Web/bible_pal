Jacob lived in the land of Canaan, in the land where his father had sojourned. Joseph was seventeen years old. He fed the flock with the sons of Bilhah and the sons of Zilpah, and he brought a report of them to his father. The brothers knew the boy was watching them. They knew what he carried home.

Jacob loved Joseph more than all his sons, because he was the son of his old age. He made him a coat of many colors. The fabric was fine. The stitching was careful. When Joseph wore it among the tents, the colors caught the light. It was a garment no one could miss.

His brothers saw the coat. They saw their father's hand in the cutting of it. They hated him. They could not speak a kind word to him.

Joseph dreamed a dream. He came to his brothers in the open air, his face still bright with sleep. "Please hear this dream which I have dreamed. We were binding sheaves in the field. My sheaf arose and stood upright. Your sheaves came around and bowed down to my sheaf."

His brothers stared at him. The work of their hands went still. "Will you indeed reign over us? Will you indeed have dominion over us?" They hated him all the more for his dreams and for his words.

He dreamed again. This time he told his father also. Jacob heard him out, then set down what he was holding. "What is this dream that you have dreamed? Shall I and your mother and your brothers indeed come to bow ourselves down before you?" Jacob's voice was sharp. His brothers stood at the edge of the tent and listened. They envied him. But his father kept the saying in his heart.

His brothers went to feed the flock in Shechem. The pastures there were wide and the grass still green, and they were many days from the valley of Hebron.

Jacob said to Joseph, "Are not your brothers feeding the flock in Shechem? Come, I will send you to them."

Joseph said, "Here I am."

"Go now," Jacob said. "See whether it is well with your brothers and well with the flock, and bring me word again." He sent him out from the valley of Hebron, and the boy went north in the coat of many colors.

Joseph came to Shechem and wandered in the open field. The flock was not there. He turned in a slow circle, looking. A man found him among the empty pastures. "What are you seeking?"

"I am seeking my brothers. Please tell me where they are feeding the flock."

"They have departed from here," the man said. "I heard them say, 'Let us go to Dothan.'"

Joseph followed after his brothers and found them in Dothan. He came up over a low rise toward them, the coat moving with his steps.

They saw him afar off. The shape on the brown hillside was unmistakable. One of them lifted a hand and pointed. The others turned. The work of their hands fell idle. "Behold, this dreamer comes." Voices rose among them, low and quick. "Let us kill him and throw him into one of the pits. We will say that an evil animal has devoured him. Then we will see what will become of his dreams."

Reuben heard it. The color drained from his face. "Let us not take his life," he said. He spoke quickly, his eyes moving over them. "Shed no blood. Throw him into this pit that is in the wilderness, but lay no hand on him." He meant to come back later and lift him out. He meant to bring him home.

Joseph came closer. He raised a hand in greeting. No hand was raised in return. Their faces were set. He slowed. He kept walking. And when he came to them, they laid hold of him.

They stripped the coat from his shoulders. They pulled it over his head, and his arms came free of the sleeves last. The colors went limp in their hands. They lifted him and carried him to the mouth of a pit and let him down into it. The pit was empty. There was no water in it. The walls were dry stone. The opening above him was a circle of sky.

They sat down to eat bread.

Joseph, below them, could hear the breaking of the loaf. He could hear the chewing. He could hear his brothers speak to one another about ordinary things, the flock and the road and the weather, as if nothing had happened above him. He called out. The voices above did not answer. He called again. The bread broke again. The circle of sky held still.

The brothers lifted up their eyes and looked, and they saw a caravan of Ishmaelites coming from Gilead. The camels came on slowly through the heat. Their packs were heavy with spices and balm and myrrh, going down to Egypt. The bells on their harnesses rang faintly across the field.

Judah watched them. He spoke at last. "What profit is it if we kill our brother and conceal his blood? Come, let us sell him to the Ishmaelites, and not let our hand be on him, for he is our brother, our flesh." His brothers listened to him.

Midianite traders passed by. The brothers stood up from their meal and went to the pit. They reached down. Hands gripped Joseph's arms and drew him up out of the dry stones into the daylight. He blinked against the sun. The coins changed hands at the side of the road. Twenty pieces of silver. The Ishmaelites set him on his feet and turned him toward Egypt. He went with them. The colored coat stayed behind in the dust.

Reuben was not with them. He came back to the pit later, alone, and stood at its edge and looked down. The pit was empty. He turned in place. He looked at the field. He took hold of his garment and tore it from his throat down to his belt. He came running to his brothers. "The child is no more. And I, where will I go?"

They took the coat from where it lay. They killed a young goat and dipped the coat in the warm blood. The blood soaked the fine fabric and darkened the colors. They carried it home.

They brought it to their father. They held it out at arm's length. "We have found this. Please examine it. Is it your son's coat or not?"

Jacob took the coat in his hands. The blood was already dry on it. He looked at the colors he himself had chosen. He looked at the stitching he had given to be done. His knees gave a little. He knew it at once. "It is my son's coat. An evil animal has devoured him. Joseph is without doubt torn in pieces."

Jacob tore his garments. He put sackcloth on his waist. He sat down on the ground and would not rise.

All his sons and all his daughters rose up to comfort him. They came one by one and stood near him. They spoke gently. He turned his face away. He refused to be comforted. "I will go down to Sheol to my son, mourning." Thus his father wept for him many days.

And far from the tent of his father, far from the brothers who had eaten bread above his pit, Joseph walked beside the camels of the Ishmaelites in the long line bound for Egypt. The Midianites brought him into Egypt, and there they sold him to Potiphar, an officer of Pharaoh, the captain of the guard. The boy who had dreamed was carried out of the land of his father's sojourning, and the saying his father had kept in his heart went on living.
