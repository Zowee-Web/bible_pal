Jacob lived in the land of Canaan, in the land where his father had sojourned. Joseph was seventeen years old. He fed the flock with his brothers and brought a report of them to their father.

Now Jacob loved Joseph more than all his sons, because he was the son of his old age. He made him a coat of many colors. The fabric was fine, the stitching careful. When Joseph wore it among the tents, the colors caught the light. It was a garment no one could miss.

His brothers saw it. They hated him. They could not speak a kind word to him.

Joseph dreamed a dream. He told his brothers. "Listen to this dream I have dreamed. We were binding sheaves in the field. My sheaf arose and stood upright. Your sheaves gathered around it and bowed down to my sheaf."

His brothers stared at him. "Will you indeed reign over us? Will you indeed have dominion over us?" They hated him all the more for his dreams and for his words.

He dreamed again. He told his brothers. "The sun and the moon and eleven stars were bowing down to me." He told his father also. Jacob rebuked him. "What is this dream? Shall I and your mother and your brothers indeed come to bow ourselves down before you?"

His brothers envied him. His father kept the saying in mind.

His brothers went to feed the flock in Shechem. The pastures there were wide and the grass still green. Jacob said to Joseph, "Your brothers are feeding the flock in Shechem. Come, I will send you to them."

Joseph said, "Here I am."

"Go now," Jacob said. "See whether it is well with your brothers and well with the flock, and bring me word." He sent him out from the valley of Hebron.

Joseph walked north. He came to Shechem and wandered in the open field. A man found him. "What are you seeking?"

"I am seeking my brothers. Please tell me where they are feeding their flocks."

"They have departed from here," the man said. "I heard them say, 'Let us go to Dothan.'"

Joseph followed after his brothers and found them in Dothan. They saw him from far off, while he was still a long way from them. The figure in the colored coat was unmistakable against the brown hillside.

Before he came near, they conspired against him to kill him. "Here comes the dreamer," they said. "Let us kill him and throw him into one of the pits. We will say that a fierce animal has devoured him. Then we will see what becomes of his dreams."

Reuben heard it. He said, "Let us not take his life." He spoke to them. "Shed no blood. Throw him into this pit in the wilderness, but lay no hand on him." He intended to rescue Joseph and restore him to their father.

When Joseph came to his brothers, they seized him. They stripped his coat — the coat of many colors that was on him. They took him and threw him into the pit. The pit was empty. There was no water in it. The walls were dry stone and the opening above him was a circle of sky.

They sat down to eat bread. They lifted their eyes and saw a company of Ishmaelite traders coming from Gilead. The camels bore spices and balm and myrrh, bound for Egypt. The caravan moved slowly through the heat.

Judah said to his brothers, "What profit is it if we kill our brother and conceal his blood? Come, let us sell him to the Ishmaelites and not lay our hand on him, for he is our brother, our own flesh." His brothers listened.

Midianite traders passed by. They drew Joseph up out of the pit. They sold him to the Ishmaelites for twenty pieces of silver. The coins changed hands. The traders took Joseph down toward Egypt. He went with them, and the colored coat stayed behind in the dust.

Reuben returned to the pit. He looked down. Joseph was not there. He tore his clothes. He went back to his brothers. "The boy is gone. And I, where shall I go?"

They took Joseph's coat. They killed a young goat and dipped the coat in the blood. The blood soaked into the fine fabric and darkened the colors. They sent it to their father. "We found this. Please examine it. Is it your son's coat or not?"

Jacob examined it. He knew it at once. "It is my son's coat. A fierce animal has devoured him. Joseph is without doubt torn in pieces."

Jacob tore his garments. He put sackcloth on his waist. He mourned for his son many days. All his sons and all his daughters rose up to comfort him, but he refused to be comforted. He said, "I will go down to Sheol to my son, mourning."

Thus his father wept for him. And the Midianites sold Joseph in Egypt to Potiphar, an officer of Pharaoh, the captain of the guard.
