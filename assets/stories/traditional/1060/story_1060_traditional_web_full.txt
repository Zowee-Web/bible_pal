Something happened the day David came back from fighting the giant, and it happened faster than anyone expected.

David was still standing in front of King Saul, still dusty from the valley, still catching his breath from the biggest thing he had ever done. He was talking to the king — answering questions, standing straight, trying to look like he belonged in a palace even though he was a shepherd boy from Bethlehem who smelled like sheep and wind and grass.

Jonathan watched. Jonathan was King Saul's son — the prince of Israel, the heir to the throne. He wore fine robes and carried a real sword and a bow made just for him. He had fought in battles himself. He knew what courage looked like.

And when David finished speaking, something happened inside Jonathan that the Bible describes in the most beautiful way: his soul was knit to David's soul. That means they were connected — deep inside, in a way that was real and sudden and strong, like two threads being woven together into one piece of cloth. Jonathan loved David as much as he loved his own life.

It didn't make sense by the world's rules. Jonathan was a prince. David was a nobody. Jonathan had everything. David had a sling and a shepherd's bag. But friendship doesn't follow rules. It follows something deeper.

And then Jonathan did something no one in that palace had ever seen a prince do.

He reached up and unfastened the robe he was wearing. This wasn't just any robe — it was the prince's robe, the garment that told every person in every room, "This is the king's son. This is the one who will rule next." The cloth was dyed deep blue and purple, woven tight and fine, with edges that brushed the stone floor. Jonathan pulled it off his shoulders and held it out.

He gave it to David.

David took it. The fabric was heavier than anything he had ever worn. He probably didn't know what to say.

Then Jonathan took off his armor. He unfastened the straps one by one — the breastpiece of leather that had been fitted to his own chest, the bronze guard at his side that still carried dents from real battles. He lifted it over his head and handed it to David. The leather was warm from Jonathan's body.

Then his sword. His own sword — the blade that hung at his hip, sharp and balanced, the weapon he had trained with since he was old enough to hold one. The handle was smooth from years of use. He placed it in David's hands, and David felt the weight of it settle against his palms.

Then his bow. The weapon he had learned to shoot with as a boy, the one that felt like part of his own arm. The wood was dark and polished, curved just right. He gave that to David too.

And finally his belt. The wide leather strap that held everything together — sword, quiver, all the tools of a prince and a warrior. He unbuckled the bronze clasp, pulled it free, and laid it across David's arms.

Jonathan stood in the court of his father's palace. No robe. No armor. No sword. No bow. No belt.

David stood there holding it all. The robe was too long for him and pooled on the floor near his dusty sandals. The sword belt would need tightening. The armor sat differently on his shoulders than it had on Jonathan's — looser in some places, tighter in others.

Jonathan stood across from him with empty hands and bare shoulders, the cool palace air against his skin. David's arms were full. Neither of them moved. Light from the high windows fell across the stone floor between them, and the court was quiet.
