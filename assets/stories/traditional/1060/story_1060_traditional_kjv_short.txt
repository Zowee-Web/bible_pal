Somewhat came to pass on the day David returned from slaying the giant. Prince Jonathan — the son of King Saul — met David, and straightway somewhat uncommon bound them together. The scripture saith the soul of Jonathan was knit with the soul of David. That is to say, they became the dearest of friends, swift as a heartbeat.

Jonathan was a prince. He dwelt in the palace. He wore goodly raiment and bare a true sword and a true bow. He was the king's son — the chief young man in all Israel.

David was a shepherd. He came from a small town called Bethlehem. His hands were rough from labouring among the sheep. His garments bore the savour of wool and of grass and of the open sky.

They could not have been more unlike. Yet Jonathan loved him as his own soul.

And then Jonathan did the most wondrous thing.

He stripped himself of the robe that was upon him — the robe that told every man, "I am the prince" — and he gave it unto David. He did but lay it in his hands. The cloth was dyed and woven, and it was heavier than aught David had ever worn.

Then he took off his garments and gave them unto David also. The leather and the brass that kept him in battle.

Then his sword. The true, sharp, heavy sword that hung at his side. He laid it in David's hands.

Then his bow. The weapon wherewith he had practised since he was a little child.

Then his girdle. The wide leathern strap that held all things together — sword, quiver, all the tools of a man of war.

Jonathan stood there. No robe. No armour. No sword. No bow. No girdle.

David held the robe and the sword and the bow and the girdle of Jonathan. The robe was too long for him. The girdle of the sword would need tightening.

Jonathan stood with empty hands and bare shoulders. The arms of David were full. Neither of them moved, and the court was still.
