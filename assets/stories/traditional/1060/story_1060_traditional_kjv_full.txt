Somewhat came to pass on the day David returned from slaying the giant, and it came to pass more swiftly than any man looked for.

David was yet standing before King Saul, yet dusty from the valley, yet drawing breath from the greatest thing he had ever done. He was speaking unto the king — answering questions, standing upright, striving to look as though he belonged in a palace, albeit he was but a shepherd lad from Bethlehem that bore the savour of sheep and wind and grass.

Jonathan beheld him. Jonathan was the son of King Saul — the prince of Israel, the heir of the throne. He wore goodly robes and bare a true sword and a bow wrought for him alone. He had himself gone forth to battle. He knew what courage looked like.

And when David had made an end of speaking, somewhat came to pass within Jonathan that the scripture describeth in the most beautiful manner: the soul of Jonathan was knit with the soul of David. That is to say, they were bound together — deep within, in a manner that was real and sudden and strong, even as two threads being woven together into one piece of cloth. Jonathan loved him as his own soul.

It made no sense by the manner of the world. Jonathan was a prince. David was no man of renown. Jonathan had all things. David had a sling and a shepherd's scrip. But friendship followeth not such rules. It followeth somewhat deeper.

And then Jonathan did a thing that no man in that palace had ever seen a prince do.

He reached up and loosed the robe wherewith he was clothed. This was no common robe — it was the robe of the prince, the garment that told every man in every chamber, "This is the king's son. This is he that shall reign after." The cloth was dyed deep blue and purple, woven close and fine, with hems that brushed the stone floor. Jonathan drew it off his shoulders and held it forth.

He gave it unto David.

David took it. The cloth was heavier than aught he had ever worn. He wist not, peradventure, what to say.

Then Jonathan took off his garments. He loosed the straps one by one — the breastplate of leather that had been fitted unto his own chest, the brazen guard at his side that yet bare the dents of true battles. He lifted it over his head and gave it unto David. The leather was yet warm from the body of Jonathan.

Then his sword. His own sword — the blade that hung at his hip, sharp and balanced, the weapon wherewith he had trained since he was of years to hold one. The handle was smooth from years of use. He laid it in David's hands, and David felt the weight thereof settle against his palms.

Then his bow. The weapon wherewith he had learned to shoot as a child, the one that felt as part of his own arm. The wood was dark and polished, curved aright. He gave that unto David also.

And at the last, his girdle. The wide leathern strap that held all things together — sword, quiver, all the tools of a prince and a man of war. He loosed the brazen clasp, drew it free, and laid it across the arms of David.

Jonathan stood in the court of his father's palace. No robe. No armour. No sword. No bow. No girdle.

David stood there bearing all of it. The robe was too long for him and gathered upon the floor near his dusty sandals. The girdle of the sword would need tightening. The armour sat otherwise upon his shoulders than it had upon Jonathan's — looser in some places, tighter in others.

Jonathan stood over against him with empty hands and bare shoulders, the cool palace air upon his skin. The arms of David were full. Neither of them moved. Light from the high windows fell upon the stone floor between them, and the court was still.
