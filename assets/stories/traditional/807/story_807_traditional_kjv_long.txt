In the quiet of a growing city, the stones of new buildings rose day by day. Hammers rang through the early morning. The people came and went, with hope in their hearts and dreams in their hands. Men set the foundation stones in the cool of the dawn, and lifted up the timbers in the bright glare of midday. They measured and cut, joining wood unto stone, and fastening beams above their heads. They bowed themselves beneath the weight of their labour, sweat gathering upon their brows, and they wrought until their arms ached and their bodies grew weary. Yet the city, great as it seemed, stood quiet in the evenings, still unfinished, yearning for something greater than the tools and the devices of men.

There were they that builded houses of great beauty, and the skill flowed from their hands, yet however careful their labour, the houses sometimes creaked and trembled in the storms. The builders set their eyes unto the sky, beholding the clouds grow dark and the winds rise up, and they were troubled at every shadow and every shaking beam. The strongest stones sometimes shifted. The handywork of men, though clever and patient, could not always shelter every corner, nor guard against every trouble that might enter unseen.

Along the wall of the city, the watchmen drew their cold cloaks close about them as the sun sank down beneath the horizon. They climbed unto their watch as the moon arose, and the stars pricked through the sky. Their eyes narrowed as they beheld the gates and searched the open fields beyond, looking for the least hint of any danger that might come with the night. Some watchmen paced restlessly while others spake quietly together, seeking to shake off the weight of their long and lonely vigil. Yet though they kept watch with all the skill and courage they could gather, at times they felt fear pressing upon them. A fear that their watching might not be enough. A fear that their strength alone could not keep the city from every threat that moved in the darkness. For all their diligence, the safety of the city rested not in their hands alone.

With every sunrise, the city awoke unto its accustomed labour. In the first blue light, bakers kindled their fires, merchants dusted off their stalls in the market, and parents hushed their drowsy children, lest they should disturb their neighbours. Yet beneath the daily speech and stir, many bore a deep weariness that clung unto them as tightly as a cloak. The builders and watchmen, the merchants and the keepers of sheep, sometimes found their countenances drawn and their eyes heavy, for their days began early and went on into long hours, ending only when the night pressed them down upon their beds.

There were they that laboured from the earliest light and finished not until the moon stood high above. They did eat their food in haste, chewing bread made with the salt of their sweat, and their minds were burdened with cares for their work and their houses. Their hearts grew anxious at every crack in a wall or shadow behind a gate. They counted the little they earned, and wondered if it should be enough to make their houses endure and their children grow strong. Their hands seemed never to be at rest, neither were their minds free from the troubles of every day.

Yet in this city, some began to perceive a mystery unfolding — one that was not written in the plans of the craftsmen, nor kept in the silent watches of the night. Even while the builders measured their walls and the watchmen sharpened their eyes, there was a quiet truth, gentle as the morning sun, that settled upon them. They beheld that without something greater guiding their hands and watching over the city, all their toil seemed to fade as smoke. However strong the stones or bright the watch fires, houses could not be safe, neither cities secure, by the striving of men alone.

One evening, as the sky turned dark and the first stars flickered into life, the builders washed the dust from their hands, and the watchmen shifted their weary feet, longing for sleep. A few children, tucked beneath their patchwork coverings, hearkened as the elders spake softly in the warm glow of the lamp. Stories were told — old stories whispered through the generations — reminding all that hearkened that except the Lord watch over them, their walls were but as a reed in the wind, and their toil would not bring forth the comfort and safety they craved.

As the night deepened, the city grew quiet. The last fires upon the hearths dimmed, and soft breaths of rest arose from the narrow alleys and the spacious houses alike. In that hush, a gentle peace settled upon the weary, as though an unseen hand soothed their brows and stilled their racing thoughts. It was not the weariness that finally claimed their minds, but a restful sleep given unto them that the Lord loved — a gift not earned by worry or strain, but granted in mercy and lovingkindness.

In the darkness, the words of wisdom hovered unseen above the rooftops and gathered the hearts together. They spake without sound: "Except the Lord build the house, they labour in vain that build it: except the Lord keep the city, the watchman waketh but in vain." These words wove through the dreams of men, pressing a gentle hope into the hearts of them that had striven so hard to hold their world together by their own strength.

Even the most diligent awoke every morning with the knowledge that it was not their own effort that made the sun rise or the walls stand. Bread tasted sweeter when they remembered that every good thing came down from above, and their sleep, when at last it overtook their tired limbs, was a gift sent by him that loved them. Without his blessing, their building and their watching and their toiling — however skilful or earnest — would never be enough to keep their houses safe or their hearts at rest.

So, day by day, the people grew wiser. Some yet arose before the dawn, going about their tasks with care, but they began to lay their burdens down at every nightfall. They trusted in the watchful hand of the Lord over their houses and their streets, and they taught their children to do likewise. Their labour became lighter, and their fears were diminished, gently exchanged for a humble trust and a spirit of thanksgiving.

And in this manner, though the city yet bustled and the walls sometimes shuddered in the storm, the people rested. They learned the beauty of holy dependence, the quiet strength of letting go. In the shadow of God's protection, they found that which their hearts had longed for most — not only safety and bread, but true peace, deep and abiding, that filled every corner of their days and every soft evening of their nights.

For it is written, "Except the Lord build the house, they labour in vain that build it: except the Lord keep the city, the watchman waketh but in vain. It is vain for you to rise up early, to sit up late, to eat the bread of sorrows: for so he giveth his beloved sleep."
