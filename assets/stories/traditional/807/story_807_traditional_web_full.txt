In the heart of the city, while morning clouds still clung to the rooftops, the builders gathered. Their tools hung heavy from their belts, and their hands bore the marks of long labor. They looked up to the half-finished house that stood before them, strong in timber and stone. Every worker remembered the day they first set foot on the patch of earth: the ground cool and brown beneath their feet, the promise of shelter shining in their hopes. Now, day after day, they raised the walls higher, piecing together what would become a home. Yet even as sweat beaded upon their brows and their muscles tired, something whispered in the quiet before dawn: unless the Lord Himself is building this house, all the work, every plank and nail, is just empty toil.

Down the lane, the city’s great gates stood open, watched by guardians. The watchmen there were weary. They had stood through long, moonless hours, their eyes scanning the road that wound through the hills and vanished into the darkness. Their duty was to protect the people within, to keep friends and strangers and wandering travelers safe from harm. It was a heavy burden, for they knew that much depended on their wakefulness. Yet, as night stretched into morning, they realized that their watching alone was not enough. Without the Lord standing guard over the city, all their careful watching, all their staying awake and alert, would be in vain.

As the pale sun stretched across the sky, more people hurried to their work. Farmers walked down furrowed fields, scattering seed into the dark, fresh earth. Merchants lifted crates and opened their stalls in the market, their voices rising in the early morning air. Daughters and sons helped sweep and clean, and everywhere, hands rushed from one task to another. Many people rose before the sun, rising while it was still dark and cool, barely letting themselves rest through the night. They ate bread earned with hard effort, feeling each bite settle heavy in their stomachs. Though they worked and worried, and sometimes felt swallowed by the tiredness of days upon days of labor, still the sense lingered that effort alone could not finish the work. Unless the Lord blessed the labor, all their rising early, all their going late to rest, would not give them the peace and joy they sought.

There were nights when the people would lie on their mats, stiff and restless. Mothers looked upon their children, worn from their day, and fathers listened to the soft sounds of sleep. The little ones, not knowing the struggles of grown-ups, slept peacefully, trusting in the care around them. That deep, gentle rest was a quiet gift, not something that could be bought with worry or lengthened with effort. The Lord, confident and kind, gave restful sleep to those He loved, sending calm where there had been exhaustion and soothing the hearts of those who trusted Him.

Morning after morning, the sun would rise over homes both grand and modest, its gold touching each one the same. It showed that houses stood not only by walls and beams, but by the blessing that held them. Cities were kept safe not just by the strong arms of their watchmen, but by the One who watched over all. Work was good, and effort worthwhile, but peace and safety and enduring joy could not be won by pushing harder or worrying longer.

So the people learned to trust. They gave their plans and their sleep, their labors and their hopes, into hands greater than their own. No matter how much they built, guarded, hurried, or endured, they remembered—unless the Lord builds the house, those who build it labor in vain; unless the Lord watches the city, the watchman stays awake in vain. It is vain for them to rise up early and stay up late, eating the bread of anxious toil, for the Lord gives sleep to His beloved.
