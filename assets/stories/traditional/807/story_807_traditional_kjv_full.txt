In the heart of the city, while the clouds of morning yet clung unto the rooftops, the builders gathered themselves together. Their tools hung heavy from their girdles, and their hands bore the marks of long labour. They lifted up their eyes unto the half-finished house that stood before them, strong in timber and in stone. Every workman remembered the day they first set their foot upon that patch of ground: the earth cool and brown beneath their sandals, the promise of shelter shining in their hopes. Now, day after day, they raised the walls higher, joining together that which should become a home. Yet even as the sweat stood upon their brows and their sinews grew weary, a voice whispered in the quiet before the dawn: except the Lord himself build this house, all their toil, every plank and every nail, is but vanity.

Down the lane, the great gates of the city stood open, and watchmen kept them. The watchmen were weary. They had stood through long and moonless hours, their eyes searching the road that wound through the hills and was lost in the darkness. Their charge was to keep the people that dwelt within, to guard friend and stranger and wayfaring man from harm. It was a heavy burden, for they knew that much hung upon their wakefulness. Yet, as the night drew on toward morning, they perceived that their watching alone was not enough. Except the Lord himself keep the city, all their careful watching, all their staying awake and their being alert, would be in vain.

As the pale sun stretched forth across the sky, more of the people went unto their labour. The husbandmen walked down the furrowed fields, scattering the seed into the dark, fresh earth. Merchants lifted their crates and opened their stalls in the market, their voices rising in the early morning air. Daughters and sons helped sweep and make clean, and on every side hands hastened from one task unto another. Many of the people arose before the sun, rising while it was yet dark and cool, scarce suffering themselves to rest through the night. They did eat bread earned with hard labour, feeling every morsel sit heavy upon them. Though they toiled and they were troubled, and sometimes felt swallowed up by the weariness of days upon days of labour, yet the sense lingered that effort alone could not finish the work. Except the Lord bless the labour, all their rising up early, all their sitting up late, would not give them the peace and joy they sought.

There were nights when the people would lie upon their mats, stiff and restless. Mothers looked upon their children, worn from their day, and fathers hearkened unto the soft sounds of sleep. The little ones, knowing not the troubles of grown men, slept peacefully, trusting in the care round about them. That deep and gentle rest was a quiet gift, neither to be bought with worry nor lengthened with toil. The Lord, faithful and kind, gave restful sleep unto them that he loved, sending calm where there had been weariness and stilling the hearts of them that trusted in him.

Morning by morning, the sun arose over houses both great and small, and its gold touched every one alike. It shewed that houses stood not by walls and beams alone, but by the blessing that held them. Cities were kept safe not only by the strong arms of their watchmen, but by him that watched over all. Labour was good, and effort worthy, but peace and safety and abiding joy could not be won by pressing harder nor by worrying longer.

So the people learned to trust. They gave their plans and their sleep, their labour and their hopes, into hands greater than their own. No matter how much they builded, how much they kept watch, how much they hastened, or how much they endured, they remembered — except the Lord build the house, they labour in vain that build it: except the Lord keep the city, the watchman waketh but in vain. It is vain for them to rise up early, to sit up late, to eat the bread of sorrows: for so he giveth his beloved sleep.
