Moses was keeping the flock of Jethro, his father-in-law, the priest of Midian. The sheep moved slowly across the dry ground, their hooves stirring dust. He had walked this country for a long time. The mornings began before the heat rose, and the evenings ended where water could be found. The work was steady. The sky was unchanging. The wilderness asked little of him beyond patience.

On this day he led the flock farther than usual. He took them past the familiar grazing slopes, past the cleft where a thin spring ran in the cool months, out toward the far side of the wilderness. The ground rose. The grass thinned. The stones grew larger and more pale. By the time the sun was high he had come to Horeb, the mountain of God.

The land was rough and quiet. Stones lay in heaps between sparse patches of brush. The air was still. Heat rose from the ground in visible waves, and the sky was a flat, pale blue from edge to edge. There was no sound but the bleating of sheep and the occasional clatter of loose rock underfoot.

Then he saw the fire.

The angel of the Lord appeared to him in a flame of fire out of the middle of a bush. The bush was small. It was a thorny shrub of the kind that grew in every gully of the wilderness, low to the ground, gnarled at the base. It was burning. The flames moved through the branches, bright and steady, orange at the edges and white at the core. Moses stopped where he was. He watched. He waited for the bush to blacken at the base, for the leaves to curl and fall, for the smoke to thicken and the wood to give way. None of that happened. The wood did not blacken. The leaves did not curl or fall. The fire burned, and the bush remained, alive in the middle of it.

Moses said, "I will turn aside now and see this great sight, why the bush is not burned."

He stepped off the path he had been keeping. He moved across the rocky ground toward the bush, slow and careful, the way a man moves toward something he does not yet understand. The flock drifted unattended behind him. His shadow shortened as he came closer to the flame. He could feel the warmth on his face now, no different from the warmth of any fire, and yet the bush was whole.

When the Lord saw that he turned aside to look, God called to him out of the middle of the bush.

"Moses, Moses."

The voice came from inside the fire. It was clear. It was near. It was a voice that knew his name.

He said, "Here I am."

"Do not come close." The words landed where he stood, and he did not take another step. "Take off your sandals, for the place where you are standing is holy ground."

Moses bent. He worked the strap loose from his right foot. The leather was warm from the long morning's walking. He set the sandal aside on the stones. He bent again and worked the second strap. He set that sandal beside the first. He straightened. The ground pressed against the bare soles of his feet — the same dry earth he had crossed a thousand times in a thousand pastures, but now it was named as something other than ordinary.

"I am the God of your father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob."

Moses hid his face. He brought his hands up and turned his head away from the fire and pressed his palms against his eyes. He was afraid to look at God. His breathing slowed. His shoulders bent. He stood there on the holy ground, barefoot and covered, while the voice continued.

"I have surely seen the affliction of my people who are in Egypt. I have heard their cry because of their taskmasters, for I know their sufferings. I have come down to deliver them out of the hand of the Egyptians, and to bring them up out of that land to a good and large land, a land flowing with milk and honey — to the place of the Canaanite, the Hittite, the Amorite, the Perizzite, the Hivite, and the Jebusite."

The names rolled out from the fire, one after another. Nations. Territories. A geography of promise spoken into a wilderness where no nation lived. God had seen. God had heard. God had come down.

"Now the cry of the children of Israel has come to me. I have also seen the oppression with which the Egyptians oppress them. Come now, therefore, and I will send you to Pharaoh, that you may bring my people, the children of Israel, out of Egypt."

Moses did not lower his hands. The word "Pharaoh" hung in the air. He had not heard that name spoken aloud in many years. He had left it behind. He had built a life on the far side of it, with sheep and a wife and a son, in a country that did not measure men in chariots. Now the name was here again, in the fire, attached to him.

Moses said to God, "Who am I, that I should go to Pharaoh, and that I should bring the children of Israel out of Egypt?"

He spoke it small. He spoke it with his face still hidden. It was the honest question of a man standing barefoot on a slope, far from any throne.

God said, "Certainly I will be with you. And this will be the sign to you that I have sent you: when you have brought the people out of Egypt, you shall serve God on this mountain."

The sign pointed forward. It would not be given in advance. It would be confirmed only after the deed was done — worship on this same mountain, Horeb, where the bush burned without burning.

Moses asked one more question. He lowered his hands a little. He spoke toward the bush.

"When I come to the children of Israel and tell them, 'The God of your fathers has sent me to you,' and they ask me, 'What is his name?' — what shall I say to them?"

God said to Moses, "I AM WHO I AM."

The words landed in his ears. There was no title behind them. There was no comparison to other gods. There was no genealogy. There was only being, holding itself open, naming itself.

He said, "You shall tell the children of Israel, 'I AM has sent me to you.'" God said further, "The Lord, the God of your fathers, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, has sent me to you. This is my name forever, and this is my memorial to all generations."

The bush still burned. The fire still held. Moses stood barefoot on the holy ground, his face partly covered, the voice of God filling the air around him. Below him on the slope the sheep grazed on, unbothered, the soft tearing of grass continuing under the sound of the flame.
