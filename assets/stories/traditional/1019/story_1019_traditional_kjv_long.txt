Now Moses kept the flock of Jethro his father in law, the priest of Midian. The sheep moved slowly across the dry ground, their hooves stirring dust. He had walked this country a long while. The mornings began ere the heat rose, and the evenings ended where water could be found. The work was steady. The sky changed not. The wilderness asked little of him beyond patience.

On this day he led the flock farther than was his wont. He took them past the familiar grazing slopes, past the cleft where a thin spring ran in the cool months, out unto the backside of the desert. The ground rose. The grass thinned. The stones grew larger and more pale. By the time the sun was high he had come unto Horeb, the mountain of God.

The land was rough and quiet. Stones lay in heaps between sparse patches of thorn. The air stirred not. Heat rose from the ground in waves that one could see, and the sky was a flat, pale blue from one edge unto the other. There was no sound save the bleating of sheep and the occasional clatter of loose stone underfoot.

Then he saw the fire.

The angel of the LORD appeared unto him in a flame of fire out of the midst of a bush. The bush was small. It was a thorny shrub of the kind that grew in every gully of the wilderness, low to the ground, gnarled at the base. It burned. The flames moved through the branches, bright and steady, orange at the edges and white at the heart. Moses stayed where he stood. He looked. He waited for the bush to blacken at the base, for the leaves to curl and fall, for the smoke to thicken and the wood to give way. None of these things came to pass. The wood blackened not. The leaves curled not, nor fell. The fire burned, and the bush remained, alive in the midst of it.

Moses said, "I will now turn aside, and see this great sight, why the bush is not burnt."

He stepped off the path he had been keeping. He moved across the rocky ground toward the bush, slow and careful, as a man moveth toward something he understandeth not yet. The flock drifted unattended behind him. His shadow shortened as he drew nearer the flame. He could feel the warmth upon his face now, no different from the warmth of any fire, and yet the bush was whole.

When the LORD saw that he turned aside to see, God called unto him out of the midst of the bush.

"Moses, Moses."

The voice came from within the fire. It was clear. It was near. It was a voice that knew his name.

He said, "Here am I."

"Draw not nigh hither." The words landed where he stood, and he took not another step. "Put off thy shoes from off thy feet, for the place whereon thou standest is holy ground."

Moses stooped. He worked the strap loose from his right foot. The leather was warm from the long morning's walking. He set the sandal aside upon the stones. He stooped again and worked the second strap. He set that sandal beside the first. He stood up. The ground pressed against the bare soles of his feet — the same dry earth he had crossed a thousand times in a thousand pastures, but now it was named as other than common.

"I am the God of thy father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob."

Moses hid his face. He brought his hands up and turned his head away from the fire and pressed his palms against his eyes. He was afraid to look upon God. His breathing slowed. His shoulders bent. He stood there upon the holy ground, barefoot and covered, while the voice continued.

"I have surely seen the affliction of my people which are in Egypt. I have heard their cry by reason of their taskmasters, for I know their sorrows. I am come down to deliver them out of the hand of the Egyptians, and to bring them up out of that land unto a good land and a large, a land flowing with milk and honey — unto the place of the Canaanite, and the Hittite, and the Amorite, and the Perizzite, and the Hivite, and the Jebusite."

The names rolled forth from the fire, one after another. Nations. Territories. A geography of promise spoken into a wilderness where no nation dwelt. God had seen. God had heard. God was come down.

"Now therefore, behold, the cry of the children of Israel is come unto me. I have also seen the oppression wherewith the Egyptians oppress them. Come now therefore, and I will send thee unto Pharaoh, that thou mayest bring forth my people the children of Israel out of Egypt."

Moses lowered not his hands. The word "Pharaoh" hung in the air. He had not heard that name spoken aloud in many years. He had left it behind. He had built a life on the far side of it, with sheep and a wife and a son, in a country that measured not men in chariots. Now the name was here again, in the fire, fastened unto him.

Moses said unto God, "Who am I, that I should go unto Pharaoh, and that I should bring forth the children of Israel out of Egypt?"

He spoke it small. He spoke it with his face yet hidden. It was the honest question of a man standing barefoot upon a slope, far from any throne.

God said, "Certainly I will be with thee. And this shall be a token unto thee, that I have sent thee: when thou hast brought forth the people out of Egypt, ye shall serve God upon this mountain."

The token pointed forward. It would not be given beforehand. It would be confirmed only after the deed was done — worship upon this same mountain, Horeb, where the bush burned without burning.

Moses asked one question more. He lowered his hands a little. He spake toward the bush.

"When I come unto the children of Israel and shall say unto them, 'The God of your fathers hath sent me unto you,' and they shall say to me, 'What is his name?' — what shall I say unto them?"

God said unto Moses, "I AM THAT I AM."

The words landed in his ears. There was no title behind them. There was no likening unto other gods. There was no genealogy. There was only being, holding itself open, naming itself.

He said, "Thus shalt thou say unto the children of Israel, 'I AM hath sent me unto you.'" God said moreover, "The LORD God of your fathers, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, hath sent me unto you. This is my name for ever, and this is my memorial unto all generations."

The bush yet burned. The fire yet held. Moses stood barefoot upon the holy ground, his face partly covered, the voice of God filling the air about him. Below him upon the slope the sheep grazed on, undisturbed, the soft tearing of grass continuing beneath the sound of the flame.
