In the month of Nisan, in the twentieth year of King Artaxerxes, wine was before the king. Nehemiah lifted the wine and gave it to him, as he always did. He was the king's cupbearer. He had tasted the wine before the king drank, as he tasted every cup. His hands were steady. His face was trained to show nothing. For years he had carried out this small daily office in the palace at Susa, and for years his face had been smooth as polished stone.

But that day his face betrayed him.

He had received word from Judah weeks before. Men had come from the province with their report — the wall broken, the gates burned, the survivors in great trouble and shame. He had sat down and wept. He had mourned for days. He had fasted and prayed before the God of heaven. And though the mourning had quieted, it had not left him. It sat in him now as he stood before the king with the cup in his hand.

The king looked up at him.

"Why is your face sad, seeing you are not sick? This is nothing but sadness of heart."

Nehemiah was very much afraid. A cupbearer with a troubled face was a cupbearer who might not be trusted. A sad countenance in the king's presence could mean suspicion, or worse. He felt the eyes of the court turn toward him. He felt the silence settle over the table.

He spoke.

"Let the king live forever. Why should my face not be sad, when the city, the place of my fathers' graves, lies waste, and its gates have been destroyed by fire?"

The king did not speak at once. He looked at his cupbearer. The queen sat beside him. The wine cup was still in Nehemiah's hand.

"What are you requesting?" the king said.

Nehemiah prayed. Not aloud. There was no time for that, no place for it in the king's hall with the cup still on the table and the court watching. He prayed to the God of heaven in the space between the question and his answer. A breath. A silent lifting of the heart upward. Then he spoke.

"If it pleases the king, and if your servant has found favor in your sight, that you would send me to Judah, to the city of my fathers' graves, that I may rebuild it."

The king asked how long he would be gone, and when he would return. Nehemiah set a time. The king was pleased. Then Nehemiah pressed further — for letters to the governors beyond the River, that they would let him pass through; and a letter to Asaph, the keeper of the king's forest, for timber to make beams for the gates of the fortress by the temple, for the wall of the city, and for the house that he would occupy.

The king granted everything. The good hand of his God was upon him.

He came with captains and horsemen at his side, sent by the king. The road from Susa to Judah was long, and he carried the king's letters folded in his garments. When he came to the governors beyond the River, he gave them the letters. They read them. They let him pass.

But the letters were also heard of by other men. Sanballat the Horonite, and Tobiah the Ammonite servant, heard that a man had come to seek the welfare of the children of Israel. It grieved them exceedingly. The word of his arrival went out across the province ahead of him, and not every ear that heard it was glad.

Nehemiah came to Jerusalem.

He rested three days. The city smelled of dust and old fire. Weeds grew between the fallen stones. People moved through the streets without lifting their eyes to the broken places, as if the broken places had become invisible from long looking. He spoke to no one of why he had come.

On the third night, he rose. A few men rose with him. He told them only that they would come. He told no one else what God had put in his heart to do for Jerusalem. There was no animal with him except the one he rode.

They went out after dark, through the Valley Gate, toward the Jackal's Well, and on to the Dung Gate. The moon was up. The stones lay where they had fallen — great blocks half-buried in dirt, smaller stones scattered across the ground like broken teeth. The charred wood of the old gates stood in blackened heaps. Thorns grew through the rubble. Nehemiah said nothing. He rode slowly. He looked.

The men with him kept close. Their footsteps were soft on the broken ground. Once, a stone shifted beneath the animal's hoof and the sound carried in the dark, and they stopped, and waited, and listened. The city slept around them. They went on.

He continued to the Fountain Gate and to the King's Pool. There the rubble was so thick the animal could not pass. Nehemiah dismounted. He gave the reins to one of the men. He continued on foot, picking his way through the fallen masonry, his hand reaching out now and then to steady himself against a tumbled block. He went up the valley in the night. He inspected the wall. He laid his palm against the stone where it still stood. He felt the gaps where it did not. He measured the damage with his eyes, and with his fingers, and with the silent calculation behind his eyes that was already beginning to see what the wall might become.

He turned back. He re-entered by the Valley Gate. The few men returned with him. The east was beginning to grey.

The rulers did not know where he had gone, or what he had done. He had not yet told the Jews, nor the priests, nor the nobles, nor the officials, nor the rest who would do the work.

Then he gathered them.

They came at his word — first the nobles, then the priests in their robes, then the heads of the families, then the workers and the women who came behind them. They gathered in the open ground where the broken wall could be seen. They came in twos and threes, then in greater numbers. Word moved through the streets and they came. The broken stones lay all around them. The blackened gates stood where they had stood.

Nehemiah lifted his voice.

"You see the trouble we are in. Jerusalem lies in ruins. Its gates are burned with fire. Come, let us build the wall of Jerusalem, that we may no longer suffer derision."

He told them of the hand of his God which had been good upon him. He told them the words the king had spoken — the letters, the timber, the permission. The crowd listened. A young man at the front held his hands open at his sides. An old man near the back leaned on his staff. A woman with a child on her hip stepped closer. The faces in the gathering were not all the same. Some were lifted. Some were lowered. But the gathering held.

A voice in the crowd answered first. Then another. Then they were many.

"Let us rise up and build."

And they strengthened their hands for the good work. The morning light came over the broken wall, and the people stood together in the place where the wall would rise, and the gathering did not scatter.
