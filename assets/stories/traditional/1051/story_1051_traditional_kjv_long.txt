In the month Nisan, in the twentieth year of Artaxerxes the king, wine was before the king. Nehemiah took up the wine and gave it unto him, as he ever did. He was the king's cupbearer. He had tasted the wine ere the king drank, as he tasted every cup. His hands were steady. His countenance was trained to shew nothing. For years he had borne this small daily office in the palace at Shushan, and for years his face had been smooth as polished stone.

But that day his countenance betrayed him.

He had received word from Judah weeks before. Men had come from the province with their report — the wall broken down, the gates burned, the survivors in great affliction and reproach. He had sat him down and wept. He had mourned certain days. He had fasted and prayed before the God of heaven. And though the mourning had quieted, it had not departed from him. It sat in him now as he stood before the king with the cup in his hand.

The king looked up at him.

"Why is thy countenance sad, seeing thou art not sick? This is nothing else but sorrow of heart."

Then was Nehemiah very sore afraid. A cupbearer with a troubled countenance was a cupbearer who might not be trusted. A sad face in the king's presence could betoken suspicion, or worse. He felt the eyes of the court turn upon him. He felt the silence settle over the table.

He spake.

"Let the king live for ever. Why should not my countenance be sad, when the city, the place of my fathers' sepulchres, lieth waste, and the gates thereof are consumed with fire?"

The king answered not at once. He looked upon his cupbearer. The queen sat beside him. The wine cup was yet in Nehemiah's hand.

"For what dost thou make request?" said the king.

Then Nehemiah prayed. Not aloud. There was no time for it, no place for it in the king's hall with the cup yet upon the table and the court watching. He prayed unto the God of heaven in the space betwixt the question and his answer. A breath. A silent lifting up of the heart. Then he spake.

"If it please the king, and if thy servant have found favour in thy sight, that thou wouldest send me unto Judah, unto the city of my fathers' sepulchres, that I may build it."

The king asked how long his journey should be, and when he would return. Nehemiah set him a time. It pleased the king. Then Nehemiah pressed further — for letters unto the governors beyond the river, that they might let him pass through; and a letter unto Asaph the keeper of the king's forest, for timber to make beams for the gates of the palace by the temple, and for the wall of the city, and for the house that he should enter into.

The king granted him all. The good hand of his God was upon him.

He came with captains and horsemen at his side, sent by the king. The road from Shushan unto Judah was long, and he carried the king's letters folded within his garments. When he came unto the governors beyond the river, he gave them the letters. They read them. They let him pass.

But the letters were heard of also by other men. Sanballat the Horonite, and Tobiah the servant, the Ammonite, heard that a man was come to seek the welfare of the children of Israel. It grieved them exceedingly. The word of his coming went out across the province before him, and not every ear that heard it was glad.

Nehemiah came unto Jerusalem.

He rested there three days. The city smelled of dust and ancient fire. Weeds grew up betwixt the fallen stones. The people moved through the streets without lifting their eyes unto the broken places, as though the broken places had become invisible from long looking. He spake to no man wherefore he had come.

On the third night he arose. Some few men arose with him. He told them only that they should come. He told no man else what his God had put in his heart to do at Jerusalem. There was no beast with him save the one whereupon he rode.

They went out after dark, by the gate of the valley, toward the dragon well, and on unto the dung port. The moon was up. The stones lay where they had fallen — great blocks half buried in the earth, lesser stones scattered upon the ground as broken teeth. The charred timbers of the old gates stood in blackened heaps. Thorns grew through the ruin. Nehemiah spake not. He rode slowly. He looked.

The men with him kept close. Their footsteps were soft upon the broken ground. Once a stone shifted beneath the beast's hoof and the sound carried in the dark, and they stayed, and waited, and hearkened. The city slept around them. They went on.

He went on unto the gate of the fountain and to the king's pool. There the rubble was so thick that there was no place for the beast that was under him to pass. Nehemiah went down from the beast. He gave the reins unto one of the men. He continued on foot, picking his way among the fallen masonry, his hand reaching out now and again to steady himself against a tumbled block. He went up by the brook in the night. He viewed the wall. He laid his palm against the stone where it yet stood. He felt the gaps where it stood no longer. He measured the ruin with his eyes, and with his fingers, and with the silent reckoning behind his eyes that was already beginning to see what the wall might become.

He turned back. He entered in again by the gate of the valley. The few men returned with him. The east was beginning to grey.

The rulers knew not whither he went, or what he did. Neither had he as yet told it to the Jews, nor to the priests, nor to the nobles, nor to the rulers, nor to the rest that did the work.

Then he gathered them.

They came at his word — first the nobles, then the priests in their robes, then the heads of the families, then the workmen and the women who came after them. They gathered in the open ground where the broken wall could be seen. They came in twos and threes, then in greater numbers. Word moved through the streets and they came. The broken stones lay round about them. The blackened gates stood where they had stood.

Nehemiah lifted up his voice.

"Ye see the distress that we are in, how Jerusalem lieth waste, and the gates thereof are burned with fire. Come, and let us build up the wall of Jerusalem, that we be no more a reproach."

He told them of the hand of his God which was good upon him. He told them the words the king had spoken — the letters, the timber, the leave to go. The people hearkened. A young man at the front held his hands open at his sides. An old man near the back leaned upon his staff. A woman with a child upon her hip stepped nearer. The faces in the gathering were not all alike. Some were lifted. Some were lowered. But the gathering held.

A voice in the company answered first. Then another. Then they were many.

"Let us rise up and build."

And they strengthened their hands for this good work. The morning light came over the broken wall, and the people stood together in the place where the wall would rise, and the gathering did not scatter.
