In the month of Nisan, in the twentieth year of King Artaxerxes, wine was before the king. Nehemiah lifted the wine and gave it to him, as he always did. He was the king's cupbearer. He tasted the wine before the king drank. His hands were steady. His face was trained to show nothing.

But that day his face betrayed him.

The king looked at him. "Why is your face sad, seeing you are not sick? This is nothing but sadness of heart."

Nehemiah was very much afraid. A cupbearer with a troubled face was a cupbearer who might not be trusted. A sad countenance in the king's presence could mean suspicion or worse. But he spoke.

"Let the king live forever. Why should my face not be sad, when the city, the place of my fathers' graves, lies waste, and its gates have been destroyed by fire?"

The walls of Jerusalem had been broken for years. The gates burned. The stones scattered. Nehemiah had heard the reports from those who returned, and the weight of it sat behind his ribs like a stone he could not set down.

The king looked at him. "What are you requesting?"

Nehemiah prayed. Not aloud — there was no time for that, no place for it in the king's hall with the wine still on the table. He prayed to the God of heaven in the space between the question and his answer.

Then he said, "If it pleases the king, and if your servant has found favor in your sight, that you would send me to Judah, to the city of my fathers' graves, that I may rebuild it."

The queen sat beside the king. The court was quiet. Nehemiah stood with the empty wine cup still in his hand.

The king asked, "How long will you be gone, and when will you return?"

Nehemiah set a time. The king was pleased. Nehemiah pressed further — letters to the governors beyond the River for safe passage, and a letter to Asaph, the keeper of the king's forest, for timber to make beams for the gates of the fortress by the temple, for the city wall, and for the house that Nehemiah would occupy.

The king granted everything. The good hand of his God was upon him.

Nehemiah traveled to Jerusalem. He rested three days. The city smelled of dust and old fire. Weeds grew between the fallen stones.

Then he rose in the night. He told no one what God had put in his heart to do for Jerusalem. He took a few men and one animal — the one he rode — and went out after dark.

He rode through the Valley Gate, past the Dragon's Spring to the Dung Gate. He inspected the walls by moonlight. The stones lay where they had fallen — great blocks half-buried in dirt, smaller stones scattered across the ground like broken teeth. The charred remains of the gates stood in blackened heaps. Thorns grew through the rubble.

He continued to the Fountain Gate and to the King's Pool. The rubble was so thick the animal could not pass. Nehemiah dismounted and continued on foot, picking his way through the fallen masonry. He went up the valley in the dark, running his hand along the broken wall, measuring the damage with his eyes and his fingers.

Then he turned back and re-entered by the Valley Gate.

The officials did not know where he had gone or what he had done. He had not yet told anyone — not the Jews, not the priests, not the nobles, not the officials, not the rest who would do the work.

Then Nehemiah gathered them. He stood before the people of Jerusalem and spoke.

"You see the trouble we are in. Jerusalem lies in ruins. Its gates are burned with fire. Come, let us build the wall of Jerusalem, that we may no longer suffer derision."

He told them how the hand of God had been upon him. He told them the words the king had spoken and the provision that had been made — the letters, the timber, the permission.

They said, "Let us rise up and build."

And they strengthened their hands for the good work.
