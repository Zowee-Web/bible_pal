Then sang Moses and the children of Israel this song to the Lord on the morning of their deliverance.

The night before, the wind had blown from the east, and the sea had stood as walls on the right and the left, and the people had walked through on dry ground. Behind them the army of Pharaoh had gone down. The corridor had closed. The chariots and the horsemen had been covered.

Now the morning was bright over the eastern shore. The sea lay smooth and wide. The light fell upon the surface of the water and held there. The wreckage of chariots drifted at the tide line. Bodies of horses lay still upon the sand. The people of Israel stood on the shore in the new light. Their cloaks were heavy with salt. Their hair was wet against their necks. Their feet pressed into ground that did not move.

They had not spoken since they came up out of the sea.

Then Moses lifted up his voice and began the song.

He sang that he would sing to the Lord, for the Lord had triumphed gloriously. He sang of the horse and his rider, cast into the sea.

The words went out across the sand. The people gathered closer. Children stopped at the edge of the water and turned. Old men leaned upon their staffs and raised their faces toward the sound. The song was the shape of what had happened, given back to the people as praise.

Moses sang that the Lord was his strength and his song. The Lord had become his salvation. This was his God, and he would praise him. His father's God, and he would exalt him.

He sang that the Lord was a man of war. The Lord was his name.

He sang of Pharaoh's chariots, cast into the sea. He sang of the chosen captains, sunk in the Red Sea. The deeps had covered them. They had gone down into the depths like a stone.

The people heard the words and remembered the night. They remembered the wind in their cloaks, the long corridor between the walls of green water, the slow flicker of a fish suspended inside the wall, the firm cold ground beneath their feet. The song held it all and gave it back to them as praise.

Moses sang on. He sang of the right hand of the Lord, glorious in power, dashing the enemy in pieces. He sang of the greatness of the Lord's excellency, overthrowing those who had risen up against him. He sang of the wrath of the Lord, going forth and consuming them as stubble.

He sang of the blast of the Lord's nostrils. The waters had been piled up. The floods had stood upright as a heap. The deeps had been congealed in the heart of the sea.

The people sang the lines back to him. The song rose along the eastern shore. The wind moved across the water, soft now, carrying the singing outward.

Then Moses sang the boast of the enemy. He sang it the way it had been spoken, with the long pursuing breath of it. "The enemy said, I will pursue. I will overtake. I will divide the spoil. My desire shall be satisfied upon them. I will draw my sword. My hand shall destroy them."

The line hung in the morning. Every word of it had been answered.

Moses sang the answer. "You blew with your wind. The sea covered them. They sank as lead in the mighty waters."

The people sang it with him.

Then Moses lifted his voice on the great question, and the song reached its high place.

"Who is like you, O Lord, among the gods? Who is like you, glorious in holiness, fearful in praises, doing wonders?"

The question stood in the morning air. The water lay flat behind them. No army moved on the far shore. No voice answered from any direction. The question filled the open sky and did not ask for any answer that was not already given.

Moses sang on. He sang that the Lord had stretched out his right hand, and the earth had swallowed them.

Then the song turned toward the road ahead.

He sang of the loving kindness of the Lord, which had led the people he had redeemed. He sang that the Lord had guided them in his strength to his holy habitation. He sang of the peoples who had heard, and trembled. The inhabitants of Philistia were in pangs. The chiefs of Edom were dismayed. Trembling had taken hold of the mighty men of Moab. The inhabitants of Canaan had melted away. Terror and dread had fallen on them, by the greatness of the Lord's arm, until the people of the Lord should pass over, until the people he had purchased should pass over.

He sang that the Lord would bring them in, and plant them in the mountain of his inheritance, in the place the Lord had made for himself to dwell in, in the sanctuary which the Lord's hands had established.

Then he sang the last line of his song, low and steady, like a stone set into place.

"The Lord shall reign for ever and ever."

The line settled into the sand. The people of Israel stood quiet on the shore.

The song of Moses had ended.

Then Miriam came forward.

She was the prophetess, the sister of Aaron, and she took a tambourine in her hand. She lifted it. The morning light caught the small bright disks set into its frame. She struck the head of it once with the flat of her palm, and the ring of it went out over the wet sand.

The women of Israel came out after her. They had their tambourines also. They had their dances. They moved into a long line along the shore, women and girls, mothers carrying small children on their hips, grandmothers with grey braids loose at their shoulders. Their feet pressed into the wet sand. Their voices rose above the steady ring of the tambourines.

Miriam answered the song that Moses had begun.

"Sing to the Lord, for he has triumphed gloriously. The horse and his rider he has thrown into the sea."

The women sang it back. The tambourines beat through the morning. The line of dancing women moved along the shore in steady rhythm. The sound went out across the open water and rose into the bright air. The shore was a shore of singing.

The children of Israel watched. Some of them sang too, the older ones who knew the line. The smaller ones ran in and out among the dancing women, laughing, kicking up sand. The old men still leaned upon their staffs, but their faces were lifted, and one of them was weeping, and the salt of his tears mixed with the salt that the sea had left upon his beard.

The song of Miriam went up to the Lord. The tambourines beat on. The morning grew brighter. The line of the dance moved along the shore, and the shore held the rhythm of it, and the sea behind them lay still beneath the singing.

The song was not forgotten.
