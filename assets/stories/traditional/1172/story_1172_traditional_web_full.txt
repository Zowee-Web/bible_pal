The morning was bright over the eastern shore. The night wind had died. The sea behind them lay smooth and wide, holding the early light on its surface, holding nothing else that moved. The children of Israel stood together on the sand. Their cloaks were heavy with salt. Their hair was still wet. The shore was strewn with the wreckage of chariots and the bodies of horses, and the people stood and looked, and then they began to sing.

Moses lifted up his voice first.

He sang that he would sing to the Lord, for the Lord had triumphed gloriously. He sang of the horse and his rider, cast into the sea. The words rose in the cool morning air, and the people gathered closer to hear. Children stopped at the edge of the water and turned to listen. Old men leaned upon their staffs and let their faces lift toward the sound.

Moses sang that the Lord was his strength and his song. He sang that the Lord had become his salvation. This was his God, and he would praise him. His father's God, and he would exalt him.

The song carried along the shore. It did not break.

He sang that the Lord was a man of war. The Lord was his name. He had cast Pharaoh's chariots and his army into the sea. The chosen captains of Egypt were sunk in the Red Sea. The deeps had covered them. They had gone down into the depths like a stone.

The people heard the words and remembered. They remembered the chariot wheels caught in the floor of the sea, the trembling of the walls of water, the long crash of the closing. The song held it all and gave it back to them as praise.

Moses sang on. He sang of the right hand of the Lord, glorious in power, dashing the enemy in pieces. He sang of the blast of the Lord's nostrils, and how the waters had been piled up, and how the floods had stood upright as a heap, and how the deeps had been congealed in the heart of the sea.

He sang the boast of the enemy and the silence that had answered it. "The enemy said, I will pursue. I will overtake. I will divide the spoil. My desire shall be satisfied upon them." Then the singing turned. "You blew with your wind. The sea covered them. They sank as lead in the mighty waters."

The people sang with him. The line repeated and returned, and the song rose along the eastern shore.

Then Moses lifted his voice on the great question.

"Who is like you, O Lord, among the gods? Who is like you, glorious in holiness, fearful in praises, doing wonders?"

The question stood in the morning air. No one answered. The water lay flat behind them.

He sang of the loving kindness of the Lord, which had led the people he had redeemed. He sang that the Lord had guided them in his strength to his holy habitation. He sang that the peoples had heard, and they trembled. The inhabitants of Philistia were in pangs. The chiefs of Edom were dismayed. The mighty men of Moab trembled. The inhabitants of Canaan had melted away.

He sang the last line, low and steady. "The Lord shall reign for ever and ever."

The song settled into the sand and into the salt-wet cloaks of the people.

Then Miriam, the prophetess, the sister of Aaron, took a tambourine in her hand.

She lifted it. The morning light caught the small bright disks set into its frame. The women of Israel came out after her, and they had their tambourines also, and they had their dances. They moved out along the shore in a long line, women and girls, their feet pressing into the wet sand, their voices rising above the steady ring of the tambourines.

Miriam answered the song. "Sing to the Lord, for he has triumphed gloriously. The horse and his rider he has thrown into the sea."

The women sang back. The tambourines beat through the morning. The line of dancing women moved along the shore in steady rhythm, and the sound went out over the water and into the open air. Children ran in and out among them, laughing. The old men still leaned upon their staffs, but their faces were lifted.

The song of Miriam went up to the Lord, and the morning grew brighter still, and the shore where they stood was a shore of singing.
