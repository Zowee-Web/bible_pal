And the whole earth was of one language, and of one speech.

The generations after the flood had grown out across the country. The sons of Japheth had gone north, into the cool country and the isles. The sons of Shem had stayed in the midst, in the country where the rivers ran. The sons of Ham had spread south and west, into the dry country and the country of the great river. And in the midst, in the second and third generation after Noah, a great body of them journeyed from the east, and they had come together at last upon a plain in the land of Shinar, between two great rivers.

They dwelt there.

The land was wide. The clay was good. The grasses grew. There were waters in both rivers. There was room enough for all of them to plant fields and to keep their flocks and to set up tents and walls and the beginnings of houses. They had everything they needed. They had no cause to keep moving. And they desired not to keep moving. They had been moving too long already. The story of the flood was yet in the mouth of the grandfathers, and the children had heard it again and again. They had heard of the ark, and of the dove and the olive leaf, and of the bow that had been hung in the cloud for a token. They had heard of the long road from Ararat. They were weary of roads.

And they said one to another: go to, let us make brick, and burn them thoroughly. And they had brick for stone, and slime had they for morter. The kilns smoked along the river. The walls began to rise. The first walls were the walls of a city — a real city, not a camp.

Then one said the thing the others had already been thinking. He said it openly, where the others could hear.

Go to, let us build us a city and a tower, whose top may reach unto heaven. And let us make us a name, lest we be scattered abroad upon the face of the whole earth.

The word that ran through the camp was that word: name. They had not been given one. They desired one. They were afraid of going out and being forgotten. They were afraid of the world being too great for them. They had survived the flood. They were not going to be scattered again. They should build a place to be seen from. They should stand in it together. The place should bear their name forward into whatever came after them.

It was a reasonable fear. The world was wide. They had seen it. They had walked it. The story of the flood had ended with one family in one ark, and they desired not to be a family in an ark again. They desired to be a city that endured.

The work began in earnest.

The tower went up. The bricks burned hot. The plain filled with the noise of work. There were levels of scaffolding. There were ramps for the bricks. There were tools no man had thought of before, devised as they went. The plans grew. What had begun as a city became a city around a tower. What had been a tower became the highest tower any had builded. The base of it spread wider than the camp had been when they first came thither.

They could see the tower from a day's walk away. The wind moved at the top of it. The cloud sat upon it in the morning. The men climbing it could look down and see the river bend, and the kilns smoking, and the camp spread out below, and the women bearing water back from the river, and the children running between the houses. They could see all of it from on high. They felt very strong upon the top of the tower. They felt very strong in the city.

And the Lord came down to see the city and the tower, which the children of men builded.

He came down. The text saith it that way of purpose, with that small smile in it. The tower they had builded to reach unto heaven was small enough that he must needs come down to see it. He stood among them, though they knew not he stood among them. He looked at the work. He looked at the men. He looked at the future they were seeking to build for themselves.

He said: behold, the people is one, and they have all one language; and this they begin to do: and now nothing will be restrained from them, which they have imagined to do.

He was not wroth. The text saith not that he was wroth. It saith that he saw what was coming. He saw what one people with one language and one purpose could do, when that purpose was the wrong one. He saw what the next tower should be, and the next, and the next, and what they should build at the top of them.

Go to, he said, let us go down, and there confound their language, that they may not understand one another's speech.

And it was so.

It came not with thunder. It came not with fire. The men on the wall called down to the men below for more bricks, and the men below stood looking up at them with their hands open. The words had been their own words a moment before. They were not their own words now. The men with the morter shouted at the men with the buckets, and the men with the buckets shouted back, and neither side could tell what the other side was saying. The plans upon the table could not be read by the second man at the table. The order from the foreman could not be done by the workman who heard it. The mothers calling to their children from the doorways called in one tongue, and the children answered in another, and the mothers stood in the doorways with their mouths open.

By the end of the day, no man could give an order any other man could carry out.

The work ceased. The kilns went cold. The tower stood where it was, half builded, with the wind moving over the top of it. The men sat down where they were. They could yet see one another. They could yet know one another's faces. They had grown up together. They had eaten at the same fires. They simply could no longer speak to one another.

It was a terrible quiet that came over the city.

After a while, the men who could yet understand one another began to draw together. They that shared a tongue gathered up their tools, their families, what they could bear. The camp broke up into smaller camps. The smaller camps formed up into small companies. The small companies moved off in different directions, every one going toward a different horizon. Some went west. Some went north. Some went farther east. Some went south along the river. They had been one people in the morning. By the end of the next week they were many peoples, and the lines between them ran along the lines of speech.

So the Lord scattered them abroad from thence upon the face of all the earth: and they left off to build the city.

Therefore is the name of it called Babel; because the Lord did there confound the language of all the earth: and from thence did the Lord scatter them abroad upon the face of all the earth. The tower stood empty behind them upon the plain, with the wind moving over it, and the dust gathering at its base, and the kilns cold beside the river. The name they had desired to make for themselves was the name now given unto the place — and the name meant confusion.

And so it was in the days of the children of men on the plain of Shinar, when they sought to build a name into the heavens, and the building of it was the thing that brake the gathering.
