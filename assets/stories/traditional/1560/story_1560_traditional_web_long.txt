The whole earth was of one language and one speech.

The generations after the Flood had grown out across the country. The sons of Japheth had gone north, into the cool country and the islands. The sons of Shem had stayed in the middle, in the country where the rivers ran. The sons of Ham had spread south and west, into the dry country and the country of the great river. And in the middle, in the second and third generation after Noah, a great body of them traveled east, and they had come together at last on a plain in the land of Shinar, between two great rivers.

They settled there.

The land was wide. The clay was good. The grasses grew. There was water in both rivers. There was room enough for all of them to plant fields and raise their flocks and put up tents and walls and the beginnings of houses. They had everything they needed. They had no reason to keep moving. And they did not want to keep moving. They had been moving for too long already. The story of the Flood was still in the mouth of the grandfathers, and the children had heard it again and again. They had heard about the ark, and about the dove and the olive leaf, and about the rainbow that had been hung in the sky for a promise. They had heard about the long road from Ararat. They were tired of roads.

They began to say to one another: come, let us make bricks, and burn them thoroughly. So they had bricks for stone, and they used tar for mortar. The kilns smoked along the river. The walls began to rise. The first walls were the walls of a city — a real city, not a camp.

Then someone said the thing the others had already been thinking. He said it openly, where the others could hear.

Come, let us build ourselves a city, and a tower whose top reaches to the sky. And let us make a name for ourselves, lest we be scattered abroad on the surface of the whole earth.

The word that ran through the camp was that word: name. They had not been given one. They wanted one. They were afraid of going out and being forgotten. They were afraid of the world being too big for them. They had survived the Flood. They were not going to be scattered again. They would build a place to be seen from. They would stand in it together. The place would carry their name forward into whatever came after them.

It was a reasonable fear. The world was wide. They had seen it. They had walked it. The story of the Flood had ended with one family in one boat, and they did not want to be a family in a boat again. They wanted to be a city that endured.

The work began in earnest.

The tower went up. The bricks burned hot. The plain filled with the noise of work. There were levels of scaffolding. There were ramps for the bricks. There were tools no one had thought of before, invented as they went. The plans grew. What had begun as a city became a city around a tower. What had been a tower became the highest tower anyone had built. The base of it spread wider than the camp had been when they first arrived.

They could see the tower from a day's walk away. The wind moved at the top of it. The cloud sat on it in the morning. The men climbing it could look down and see the river bend, and the kilns smoking, and the camp spread out below, and the women carrying water back from the river, and the children running between the houses. They could see all of it from up there. They felt very strong on the top of the tower. They felt very strong in the city.

Yahweh came down to see the city and the tower that the children of men were building.

He came down. The text says it that way on purpose, with that small smile in it. The tower they had built to reach the sky was small enough that he had to come down to see it. He stood among them, though they did not know he was standing among them. He looked at the work. He looked at the men. He looked at the future they were trying to build for themselves.

He said: behold, they are one people, and they all have one language. And this is only the beginning of what they will do. Nothing they intend to do will be withheld from them.

He was not angry. The text does not say he was angry. It says he saw what was coming. He saw what one people with one language and one purpose could do, when that purpose was the wrong one. He saw what the next tower would be, and the next, and the next, and what they would build at the top of them.

Come, he said, let us go down, and confuse their language there, that they may not understand one another's speech.

And it was so.

It did not happen with thunder. It did not happen with fire. The men on the wall called down to the men below for more bricks, and the men below stood looking up at them with their hands open. The words had been their own words a minute before. They were not their own words now. The men with the mortar shouted at the men with the buckets, and the men with the buckets shouted back, and neither side could tell what the other side was saying. The plans on the table could not be read by the second man at the table. The order from the foreman could not be carried out by the workman who heard it. The mothers calling to their children from the doorways called in one language, and the children answered in another, and the mothers stood in the doorways with their mouths open.

By the end of the day, no one could give an order anyone else could carry out.

The work stopped. The kilns went cold. The tower stood where it was, half built, with the wind moving over the top of it. The men sat down where they were. They could still see each other. They could still recognize each other's faces. They had grown up together. They had eaten at the same fires. They simply could not speak to each other anymore.

It was a terrible quiet that came over the city.

After a while, the men who could still understand one another began to draw together. The ones who shared a tongue gathered up their tools, their families, what they could carry. The camp broke up into smaller camps. The smaller camps formed up into small companies. The small companies moved off in different directions, each one going toward a different horizon. Some went west. Some went north. Some went farther east. Some went south along the river. They had been one people in the morning. By the end of the next week they were many peoples, and the lines between them ran along the lines of speech.

So Yahweh scattered them abroad from there over the face of all the earth. They stopped building the city.

Therefore its name was called Babel, because there Yahweh confused the language of all the earth. From there, Yahweh scattered them abroad over the face of all the earth. The tower stood empty behind them on the plain, with the wind moving over it, and the dust gathering at its base, and the kilns cold beside the river. The name they had wanted to make for themselves was the name now given to the place — and the name meant confusion.

And so it was in the days of the children of men on the plain of Shinar, when they tried to build a name into the sky, and the building of it was the thing that broke the gathering.
