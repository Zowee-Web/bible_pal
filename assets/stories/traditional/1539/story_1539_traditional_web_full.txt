Jesus spoke this parable to certain people who trusted in themselves that they were righteous and who looked down on everyone else. They stood near him, listening, their faces sure and settled.

Two men went up to the temple to pray. One was a Pharisee. The other was a tax collector.

The temple courts were broad and open. The stone paving caught the light. Smoke drifted from the altar, thin and gray, curling upward past the columns. Worshipers moved through the courts in small groups, their sandals scraping the stones. Pigeons shifted on the ledges overhead.

The Pharisee walked in with his head up. His robes were long and well kept, the fringes hanging even at the hem. His phylacteries were broad on his forehead and bound tight on his left arm. He moved through the court without hurrying, passing others, and found a place to stand where his voice could carry.

He planted his feet. He straightened his back. He lifted his face toward the sky visible between the high walls. And he prayed with himself.

"God, I thank you that I am not like other men."

His voice was clear and unhesitant. People nearby could hear every word.

"Extortioners. The unjust. Adulterers."

He turned his head slightly, just enough to let his gaze pass over the edge of the court behind him.

"Or even like this tax collector."

He faced forward again.

"I fast twice every week," he said. "I give tithes of everything I possess."

He stood with his hands open at his sides. His chin was lifted. The light fell across the clean fabric of his outer garment. His prayer was done. He had listed what he was not and what he had done, and he was satisfied. He lowered his hands and remained standing in that place.

The tax collector stood far off.

He had not come forward into the court where the others gathered. He stayed near the outer edge, close to the wall where the shade was deep and the stone was cool. His clothes were plain. No one stood with him. No one spoke to him. He was alone at the margin of the place, and he kept himself there.

He would not lift so much as his eyes toward heaven.

His head was bowed. His shoulders curved forward. He stared at the paving stones beneath his feet, at the dust that had gathered in the cracks, at the worn surface where thousands of feet had walked before his.

Then his right hand came up and struck his own chest. The sound was sudden and dull, a fist against ribs. His body rocked with the force of it.

"God," he said.

His voice was low and rough. It did not carry. The people in the center of the court would not have heard him.

"Be merciful to me."

He struck his chest again. The same fist, the same spot over his heart.

"A sinner."

That was all. He did not list his deeds. He did not name what he had done or what he had failed to do. He stood with his fist against his chest and his eyes on the ground and the dust on his sandals and the shadow of the wall falling across his back.

The two men were both in the temple. Both had come to pray. The incense smoke drifted between them. The distance between where the Pharisee stood and where the tax collector stood was thirty paces at least, maybe more. The Pharisee did not look back again. The tax collector did not look up.

Jesus paused. The people around him were quiet.

"I tell you," Jesus said, "this man went down to his house justified rather than the other."

He looked at the faces in front of him. Some of them shifted. Some did not move at all.

"For everyone who exalts himself will be brought low," he said. "And the one who humbles himself will be lifted up."

The air was warm. The road dust hung in the light. No one in the crowd spoke. Jesus stood among them with his hands still, and the silence held.
