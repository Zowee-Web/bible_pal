Far unto the east, beyond the deserts and the rivers, wise men studied the night sky. They dwelt in a land of wide plains and ancient cities where scholars kept careful records of every star and every pattern above them. They knew the heavens the way a shepherd knoweth his flock.

One night, a thing was changed.

A star appeared which none of them had ever seen before. It was bright and steady, and it hung in the western sky like a lamp set upon a high shelf. These wise men believed it to be a sign. A king was born — a king of the Jews.

So they made ready for a journey. They laded their camels with provision and with treasures wrapped carefully in cloth. They filled bottles with water and packed dried fruit and bread for the long miles ahead. Then they set out, following the star westward across the desert.

The journey was not short. Day after day, their camels swayed beneath them across sand and stone. Night after night, the star burned in the sky before them.

At last they came unto the land of Israel. They were come to Jerusalem, the great city with its high walls and crowded markets and the gleaming temple upon the hill. Surely this was where a king should be born.

They began to enquire of the people in the streets. "Where is he that is born King of the Jews?" they said. "For we have seen his star in the east, and are come to worship him."

The people stared. They whispered one to another. Word travelled swiftly through the winding streets and open courts.

When Herod the king heard what these foreign visitors enquired, he was troubled. A new king? He had not heard of any new king. All Jerusalem was troubled with him.

Herod gathered all the chief priests and scribes of the people. These were the men who knew the scriptures best, who had spent their lives reading the ancient scrolls.

"Where should Christ be born?" Herod demanded of them.

They tarried not. "In Bethlehem of Judaea," they said. "For thus it is written by the prophet: 'And thou Bethlehem, in the land of Juda, art not the least among the princes of Juda: for out of thee shall come a Governor, that shall rule my people Israel.'"

Herod nodded slowly. Then he sent for the wise men. He called them unto a private chamber, away from the crowds and the priests.

"Tell me," he said, leaning forward. "What time did this star appear?"

The wise men told him. They described the night, the brightness, the place in the sky. Herod hearkened unto every word.

Then he said, "Go and search diligently for the young child in Bethlehem. And when ye have found him, bring me word again, that I may come and worship him also."

The wise men departed from Jerusalem. They passed through the city gate and out upon the road. The evening air was cool, and dust rose beneath their camels' feet.

And lo, there it was.

The star. The same star they had followed from the east. It went before them in the darkening sky, steady and bright. When they saw the star, they rejoiced with exceeding great joy. They had been right. They had not journeyed all those miles in vain.

The star led them southward, past olive groves and low stone walls, past fields where shepherds tended their flocks. The road was quiet. The air bore the savour of earth and dry grass.

Then the star stood still. It hung perfectly still over one particular place in the town of Bethlehem.

The wise men came down from their camels. Their robes were dusty from the journey, their sandals worn. They stepped toward the door.

When they were come into the house, they saw the young child with Mary his mother. The chamber was small and plain. Light fell across the floor. The child was there — quiet, small, breathing softly.

The wise men fell down. One by one, they bowed themselves down to the ground and laid their faces upon the floor.

Then they brought forth their treasures. They opened boxes and unwrapped bundles of fine cloth. Gold, heavy and gleaming, was presented unto the child. Frankincense, pale and sweet, filled the chamber with its rich savour. Myrrh, dark and fragrant, was placed beside the rest.

The gifts lay upon the floor in the lamplight — gold, and frankincense, and myrrh — the finest things these men had borne across the desert.

That night, being warned of God in a dream, the wise men were told that they should not return to Herod. So when morning came, they laded their camels, wrapped their robes against the cold, and departed into their own country another way. They turned eastward and wound through the low brown hills, following a new path back toward home.
