Far to the east, beyond the Jordan River, beyond the wide deserts and the slow-moving rivers of Mesopotamia, wise men studied the night sky. They lived in ancient cities made of brick and stone, cities where scholars had watched the heavens for hundreds and hundreds of years. They kept careful records. They drew maps of the stars. They knew the patterns the way a fisherman knows the currents in a river.

These were patient men. Careful men. Men who noticed when something changed.

One night, something changed.

A new star appeared. It had not been there before. It burned in the western sky, bright and steady, like a torch set on a distant hilltop. The wise men gathered on their rooftops and studied it. They compared it to their charts and their scrolls. This was not a star they recognized.

They believed it was a sign. A great king had been born — a king of the Jews, far to the west.

So they began to prepare. They were not the kind of men who rushed. They packed slowly, carefully. They chose their finest gifts and wrapped them in layers of cloth. They loaded their camels with waterskins, with dried dates and bread and grain. They checked and tightened every strap, every rope.

The road ahead would be long. Very long.

At last, everything was ready. The wise men set out. Their camels swayed beneath them as they crossed the open desert, heading west. The sand stretched in every direction, golden in the daylight, silver under the moon. The wind blew hot and dry during the day and turned cool at night.

Each evening the star appeared again, bright and clear, always in the same direction. The wise men followed it the way a sailor follows a lighthouse.

Days passed. Weeks. The landscape changed around them. Sandy flats gave way to rocky ground. Rocky ground gave way to green hills and terraced fields. They crossed rivers. They passed through small towns where children watched them from doorways and merchants called out from stalls.

At last, the walls of Jerusalem rose before them.

It was a great city — the greatest city in all of Judah. The temple of God stood high on a hill, its white stone catching the sunlight. Markets filled the streets with the smell of spices and roasting bread. Soldiers in armor walked the roads. Crowds pressed through the narrow gates.

Surely this was where a king would be born.

The wise men entered the city. They asked everyone they could find. They stopped people in the marketplace, near the wells, along the temple steps.

"Where is the one who has been born king of the Jews?" they asked. "We saw his star when it rose in the east, and we have come to worship him."

People stared at them. Some tilted their heads. Others pulled their cloaks tighter and walked away quickly. The question spread through the city like wind through dry grass. A new king? Born where? What star?

When King Herod heard about these visitors and their question, he was troubled. Deeply troubled.

Herod sat on his throne in his palace, surrounded by marble and gold, and the muscles in his jaw tightened. He had not heard of any new king being born. No one had told him. The whole city of Jerusalem felt the unease that rippled outward from the palace.

Herod called together the chief priests and the teachers of the law. These men spent their lives studying the scriptures, unrolling the ancient scrolls, reading and copying the words of the prophets. They gathered in the palace chamber, their sandals scuffing on the stone floor.

"Where is this Christ supposed to be born?" Herod asked them.

They answered without delay. "In Bethlehem, in the land of Judah," they said. "For this is what the prophet has written."

They recited the words: "'And you, Bethlehem, in the land of Judah, are by no means least among the rulers of Judah. For out of you will come a ruler who will shepherd my people Israel.'"

Herod dismissed the priests. The chamber emptied. Then he sent a quiet message to the wise men — a private invitation.

They came to him in a small room. The lamps flickered against the walls. Herod leaned forward in his chair and studied their faces.

"Tell me," he said. "When exactly did this star first appear?"

The wise men described what they had seen. The night it appeared. Its position in the sky. Its brightness. Herod listened carefully. He asked them to repeat certain details. He nodded slowly.

Then he sat back.

"Go to Bethlehem," he told them. His voice was smooth. "Search carefully for the child. And when you find him, report back to me, so that I too may go and worship him."

The wise men thanked him. They left the palace. They passed through the wide corridors, past the guards at the door, and out into the open air.

The sun was going down. The sky above Jerusalem was turning from blue to gold to deep orange. The rooftops of the city darkened against the fading light.

And then they saw it.

The star. Their star. The same star they had followed all those miles from the east. It hung in the sky ahead of them, bright and clear, moving slowly southward. It had been hard to see in the busy city, with all its torches and lanterns and noise. But here, outside the palace, there it was.

Joy. Overflowing, rushing joy.

When the wise men saw that star again, they were filled with a gladness so deep it showed on their faces and in their voices and in the way they climbed onto their camels. They had not been wrong. The star was still leading them.

They rode south out of Jerusalem. The road was narrow and dusty, lined with low stone walls. Olive trees stood in neat rows on the hillsides. The air smelled of earth and dry grass and the faint sweetness of wildflowers growing between the rocks. Crickets sang in the brush. Their camels' feet padded softly on the packed dirt.

The star moved ahead of them, steady and silent.

They passed small fields where the grain had been harvested and the stubble stood short and pale in the starlight. They passed a well where a clay jug sat on the rim, left by someone who would return in the morning. They passed a stone sheepfold where animals shifted and breathed in the darkness.

Then the star stopped.

It stopped right over one place in the small town of Bethlehem. It hung there in the sky, perfectly still, pouring its light down like water from a jar.

The wise men halted their camels. For a moment, no one moved. The animals snorted softly. The night was quiet. A dog barked somewhere far away and then fell silent.

One by one, the wise men climbed down. Their legs were stiff from riding. Their robes were dusty and creased from the long journey. Their sandals were worn thin. They steadied themselves and looked at each other.

Then they walked toward the place where the star had stopped.

They stepped inside. The room was small and plain. A lamp burned low on a ledge, casting warm light across the walls. Shadows moved gently as the flame flickered.

There was the child. He was with Mary, his mother. She sat near him. The child was small and quiet, his dark eyes catching the lamplight.

The wise men knelt. They lowered themselves all the way to the ground, pressing their foreheads to the dirt floor. Their fine robes spread out around them in the dust.

When they rose, they reached for their treasures. Carefully, slowly, they opened the boxes they had carried all the way from the east.

Gold came first. Heavy, gleaming, catching the warm lamplight. It was set on the floor near the child.

Then frankincense. Pale, golden-brown lumps of resin, giving off a rich, sweet smell that began to fill the small room. It was placed beside the gold.

Then myrrh. Dark, reddish-brown, with a deep fragrance all its own. It was set beside the frankincense.

The three gifts rested on the floor in the lamplight — gold, frankincense, and myrrh — the finest things these men had to give.

That night, God warned the wise men in a dream. He told them clearly: do not go back to Herod. Do not return to Jerusalem. Take a different road.

When morning came, the air was cool and gray. The wise men loaded their camels in the early light. They tightened the straps, checked the ropes, and wrapped their cloaks against the chill. They turned their camels east and took a different road — a road that wound through the low brown hills, away from Jerusalem, away from Herod's palace, back toward home.
