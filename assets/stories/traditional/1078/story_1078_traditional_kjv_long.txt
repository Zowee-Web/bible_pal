Far unto the east, beyond the river Jordan, beyond the wide deserts and the slow-moving rivers of Mesopotamia, wise men studied the night sky. They dwelt in ancient cities builded of brick and stone, cities where scholars had watched the heavens for hundreds upon hundreds of years. They kept careful records. They drew maps of the stars. They knew the patterns the way a fisherman knoweth the currents in a river.

These were patient men. Careful men. Men who took notice when a thing changed.

One night, a thing changed.

A new star appeared. It had not been there before. It burned in the western sky, bright and steady, like a torch set upon a distant hilltop. The wise men gathered upon their rooftops and studied it. They compared it with their charts and their scrolls. This was not a star they knew.

They believed it to be a sign. A great king was born — a king of the Jews, far unto the west.

So they began to make ready. They were not the kind of men who hasted. They packed slowly, carefully. They chose their finest gifts and wrapped them in layers of cloth. They laded their camels with bottles of water, with dried dates and bread and grain. They tried every strap, every cord.

The road before them was long. Very long.

At last, all was ready. The wise men set out. Their camels swayed beneath them as they crossed the open desert, going westward. The sand stretched in every direction, golden in the daylight, silver under the moon. The wind blew hot and dry by day and turned cool by night.

Every evening the star appeared again, bright and clear, ever in the same quarter. The wise men followed it the way a mariner followeth a beacon.

Days passed. Weeks. The land changed about them. Sandy flats gave way to rocky ground. Rocky ground gave way to green hills and terraced fields. They crossed rivers. They passed through small towns where children watched them from doorways and merchants called out from their stalls.

At last, the walls of Jerusalem rose before them.

It was a great city — the greatest city in all Judah. The temple of God stood high upon a hill, its white stone catching the sunlight. Markets filled the streets with the savour of spices and roasting bread. Soldiers in armour walked the ways. Multitudes pressed through the narrow gates.

Surely this was where a king should be born.

The wise men entered into the city. They enquired of every man they could find. They stayed people in the marketplace, near the wells, along the temple steps.

"Where is he that is born King of the Jews?" they said. "For we have seen his star in the east, and are come to worship him."

The people stared at them. Some turned their heads aside. Others drew their cloaks tighter and walked away in haste. The question spread through the city like wind through dry grass. A new king? Born where? What star?

When Herod the king heard tell of these strangers and their question, he was troubled. Sorely troubled.

Herod sat upon his throne in his palace, encompassed about with marble and gold, and the sinews in his jaw tightened. He had not heard of any new king being born. None had told him. All Jerusalem felt the unease that rippled outward from the palace.

Herod gathered together all the chief priests and scribes of the people. These men spent their lives studying the scriptures, unrolling the ancient scrolls, reading and copying the words of the prophets. They gathered in the palace chamber, their sandals scuffing upon the stone floor.

"Where should Christ be born?" Herod demanded of them.

They answered without delay. "In Bethlehem of Judaea," they said. "For thus it is written by the prophet."

They recited the words: "'And thou Bethlehem, in the land of Juda, art not the least among the princes of Juda: for out of thee shall come a Governor, that shall rule my people Israel.'"

Herod dismissed the priests. The chamber emptied. Then he sent a quiet message unto the wise men — a private summons.

They came unto him in a small chamber. The lamps flickered against the walls. Herod leaned forward in his seat and looked steadfastly upon their faces.

"Tell me," he said. "What time did this star first appear?"

The wise men told him what they had seen. The night it appeared. Its place in the sky. Its brightness. Herod hearkened carefully. He bade them repeat certain details. He nodded slowly.

Then he sat back.

"Go to Bethlehem," he said unto them. His voice was smooth. "And search diligently for the young child. And when ye have found him, bring me word again, that I may come and worship him also."

The wise men gave him thanks. They departed from the palace. They passed through the wide corridors, past the guards at the door, and forth into the open air.

The sun was going down. The sky above Jerusalem was turning from blue to gold to deep orange. The rooftops of the city darkened against the fading light.

And then they beheld it.

The star. Their star. The same star they had followed all those miles from the east. It hung in the sky before them, bright and clear, moving slowly southward. It had been hard to see within the busy city, with all its torches and lanterns and noise. But here, without the palace, there it was.

Joy. Overflowing, rushing joy.

When they saw that star, they rejoiced with exceeding great joy — a gladness so deep it showed upon their faces and in their voices and in the way they climbed upon their camels. They had not been wrong. The star was still leading them.

They rode southward out of Jerusalem. The road was narrow and dusty, lined with low stone walls. Olive trees stood in even rows upon the hillsides. The air bore the savour of earth and dry grass and the faint sweetness of wildflowers growing betwixt the rocks. Crickets sang in the brush. Their camels' feet padded softly upon the packed dirt.

The star went before them, steady and silent.

They passed small fields where the grain had been gathered in and the stubble stood short and pale in the starlight. They passed a well where a clay pitcher sat upon the rim, left by one who would return in the morning. They passed a stone sheepfold where animals shifted and breathed in the darkness.

Then the star stood still.

It stood right over one place in the small town of Bethlehem. It hung there in the sky, perfectly still, pouring its light downward like water from a vessel.

The wise men stayed their camels. For a moment, no man moved. The beasts snorted softly. The night was quiet. A dog barked somewhere afar off and then was still.

One by one, the wise men came down. Their legs were stiff from the ride. Their robes were dusty and creased from the long journey. Their sandals were worn thin. They steadied themselves and looked one upon another.

Then they walked toward the place over which the star stood still.

They came into the house. The chamber was small and plain. A lamp burned low upon a ledge, casting warm light across the walls. Shadows moved gently as the flame flickered.

There was the young child. He was with Mary his mother. She sat near him. The child was small and quiet, his dark eyes catching the lamplight.

The wise men fell down. They bowed themselves all the way to the ground, pressing their foreheads upon the dirt floor. Their fine robes spread out about them in the dust.

When they were risen up, they reached for their treasures. Carefully, slowly, they opened the boxes they had borne all the way from the east.

Gold came first. Heavy, gleaming, catching the warm lamplight. It was set upon the floor near the child.

Then frankincense. Pale, golden-brown lumps of resin, giving off a rich, sweet savour that began to fill the small chamber. It was placed beside the gold.

Then myrrh. Dark, reddish-brown, with a deep fragrance all its own. It was set beside the frankincense.

The three gifts rested upon the floor in the lamplight — gold, and frankincense, and myrrh — the finest things these men had to give.

That night, being warned of God in a dream, the wise men were told plainly: return not unto Herod. Go not back unto Jerusalem. Depart another way.

When morning came, the air was cool and gray. The wise men laded their camels in the early light. They tied the straps, tried the cords, and wrapped their cloaks against the chill. They turned their camels eastward and departed into their own country another way — a road that wound through the low brown hills, away from Jerusalem, away from Herod's palace, back toward home.
