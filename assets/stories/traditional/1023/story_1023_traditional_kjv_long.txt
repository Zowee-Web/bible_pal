The whole congregation of the children of Israel journeyed from Elim and came unto the wilderness of Sin, which is between Elim and Sinai. It was the fifteenth day of the second month after their departing out of the land of Egypt.

A month and a half. So long had they been free. Behind them lay the crossing of the sea, the waters of Marah made sweet, the twelve wells and threescore and ten palm trees of Elim. Before them lay Sinai, though they knew not yet what awaited there. About them, in every quarter, the wilderness lay open — dry ground beneath a vast sky.

The land gave forth nothing. No fields. No orchards. No storehouses. The provision they had borne out of Egypt was running low. What grain remained was measured with care. The beasts they had brought were too needful to slaughter freely. The people looked upon the land and saw it for what it was: empty.

They murmured.

The whole congregation murmured against Moses and Aaron in the wilderness.

"Would to God we had died by the hand of the Lord in the land of Egypt," they said, "when we sat by the flesh pots, and when we did eat bread to the full. For ye have brought us forth into this wilderness, to kill this whole assembly with hunger."

The complaint was not kept in silence. It passed through the camp as wind through dry grass — tent to tent, household to household, the same words taken up with mounting bitterness. They remembered Egypt. Not the bondage. Not the bricks wrought without straw. Not the stripes nor the cries of their children. They remembered the food. The pots of flesh. The bread eaten until they were filled.

Hunger doth rewrite what a man remembereth. It taketh what was past bearing and maketh it seem tolerable. It taketh liberty and maketh it appear a sentence of death. The people were not thankless by their nature. They were afraid. Their bellies were empty, and the wilderness gave no token of mercy.

The Lord spake unto Moses.

"Behold, I will rain bread from heaven for you."

The words were direct. No preamble. No reproach — not yet.

"And the people shall go out and gather a certain rate every day, that I may prove them, whether they will walk in my law, or no. And it shall come to pass, that on the sixth day they shall prepare that which they bring in, and it shall be twice as much as they gather daily."

There were conditions set. The bread would come, but it would come with commandment. A day's portion each day. Not two days' worth. Not a week's store laid up against doubt. One day at a time. Herein lay the proving — whether a people who had but lately learned to be free could learn also to trust.

Moses and Aaron gathered the people together. The congregation assembled, their faces drawn with hunger and vexation.

"At even," Moses and Aaron told them, "ye shall know that the Lord hath brought you out from the land of Egypt. And in the morning, then ye shall see the glory of the Lord, for that he heareth your murmurings against him."

Moses held. He looked upon the multitude.

"And what are we, that ye murmur against us? Your murmurings are not against us, but against the Lord."

The distinction was of weight. Moses and Aaron were but messengers. The people had turned their wrath upon the men who stood before them, but the grievance reached beyond those men. They murmured against the God who had divided the sea, who had made the bitter waters sweet, who had brought them forth from bondage with signs and wonders.

Moses said unto Aaron, "Say unto all the congregation of the children of Israel, Come near before the Lord, for he hath heard your murmurings."

Aaron spake unto the whole congregation. As he spake, they looked toward the wilderness. The glory of the Lord appeared in the cloud. It was visible — not fancied, not a stirring within, but a thing the eye could behold. The cloud shifted and shone with a presence that stilled the murmuring in their throats.

The Lord spake unto Moses again. "I have heard the murmurings of the children of Israel. Speak unto them, saying, At even ye shall eat flesh, and in the morning ye shall be filled with bread. And ye shall know that I am the Lord your God."

The afternoon wore on. The sun drew toward the edge of the sky. The camp waited.

At even the quails came up.

They came over the rim of the wilderness, wave upon wave, settling upon the ground. The birds covered the camp. They lighted between the tents, upon the open earth, in every clear place. The people had not gone forth to seek them. The quails came unto the people. Flesh — brought without labour, without hunting, without anything beyond the promise given that morning.

The people gathered the birds. Fires were kindled. The savour of roasting flesh moved through the camp for the first time in weeks. Households did eat. The murmuring grew quiet. Bellies that had been empty began to be filled.

Then came the night. The wilderness cooled. The stars appeared. The camp settled into the manner of stillness that followeth a meal after long want.

In the morning the dew lay round about the host.

It covered the ground in a thin coat, as it had done other mornings in the wilderness. But when the dew that lay was gone up — when the sun climbed and the moisture passed away — a thing remained. Upon the face of the wilderness there lay a small round thing, as small as the hoar frost on the ground. It was thin and pale, covering the earth in every quarter about the camp.

The children of Israel came forth from their tents. They looked upon it. They looked one upon another.

"What is it?" they said.

The Hebrew word was manna — what is it? They gave the substance the name of a question, for they had no other name. It was no grain. It was nothing they had planted or reaped or bought in any market. It lay upon the ground where the dew had been, strange past all reckoning.

Moses said unto them, "This is the bread which the Lord hath given you to eat."

The bread from heaven. Promised the day before. Given in the night, laid down with the dew, made known when the moisture departed. It was not what they had looked for bread to be. It was not what they had imagined provision to look like. But it was there.

Moses gave them the commandment. "This is the thing which the Lord hath commanded: gather of it every man according to his eating, an omer for every man, according to the number of your persons. Take ye every man for them which are in his tents."

The camp came to life. The people went forth from their tents with vessels and baskets. They stooped to the earth and gathered. Some laboured swiftly, taking up the flakes with both hands. Others gathered with more care, lifting the fine substance from the ground by degrees. Households spread abroad over the earth about the camp. Children gave aid. The aged moved at their own pace.

Some gathered much. Their vessels were full, heaped up, more than seemed needful. Others gathered little — perhaps they were slow, or perhaps they trusted the commandment and took only what seemed right for their household.

When they brought what they had gathered unto the camp and did mete it with an omer, the reckoning confounded all expectation. He that gathered much had nothing over. He that gathered little had no lack. Every man had precisely what he required. No more. No less.

The surplus was not. The shortage was not. The measure was perfect — not because the people had measured with perfection, but because the provision itself was sufficient. It met every man where he stood, with exactly what he wanted.

The bread had come. It lay upon the ground of a wilderness where nothing grew, and it was enough. The murmuring had been heard. The hunger had been answered. And the children of Israel stood in the morning light, bearing in their hands what they needed for the day — no more, no less — in a place where, the day before, there had been nothing at all.
