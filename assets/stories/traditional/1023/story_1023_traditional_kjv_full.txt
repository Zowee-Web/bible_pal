The whole congregation of the children of Israel journeyed from Elim and came unto the wilderness of Sin, between Elim and Sinai. It was the fifteenth day of the second month after their departing out of Egypt.

The wilderness stretched forth in every quarter — dry, open, and barren. The ground was hard earth and pale stone. There were no fields, no storehouses, no flesh pots. The people looked upon the land about them, and what they beheld offered naught.

They murmured against Moses and Aaron.

The voices rose from sundry parts of the camp — bitter, sharp, mingling one with another.

"Would to God we had died by the hand of the Lord in Egypt," they said, "when we sat by the flesh pots, and when we did eat bread to the full. For ye have brought us forth into this wilderness, to kill this whole assembly with hunger."

The complaint was particular. They remembered Egypt — not the bondage, not the taskmaster's rod. They remembered the food. Hunger hath a way of rewriting what a man remembereth. What was unbearable becometh tolerable when the belly is empty.

The Lord said unto Moses, "Behold, I will rain bread from heaven for you. And the people shall go out and gather a certain rate every day, that I may prove them, whether they will walk in my law, or no. And it shall come to pass, that on the sixth day they shall prepare that which they bring in, and it shall be twice as much as they gather daily."

Moses and Aaron gathered the people before them. They stood before the assembly and spake.

"At even," they said, "ye shall know that the Lord hath brought you out from the land of Egypt. And in the morning, then ye shall see the glory of the Lord, for that he heareth your murmurings against him. And what are we, that ye murmur against us?"

Moses spake further. "The Lord shall give you in the evening flesh to eat, and in the morning bread to the full, for that the Lord heareth your murmurings which ye murmur against him. Your murmurings are not against us, but against the Lord."

The people hearkened. The wilderness was still about them.

At even the quails came up and covered the camp. The birds came in great number, settling upon the ground between the tents, filling the open places. The people gathered them. Flesh — given without labour, without cause save the promise spoken that morning. The fires burned. The savour of roasting quail filled the camp as the sun went down.

Then came the night. And after the night, the morning.

When the dew that lay was gone up, behold, upon the face of the wilderness there lay a small round thing, as small as the hoar frost on the ground. It covered the earth about the camp in a thin, pale layer — fine and fragile, catching the early light. It lay upon the dirt and stones, as though the ground had brought forth in the night a thing that had not been there before.

The children of Israel came forth and beheld it. They looked one upon another.

"What is it?" they said.

They wist not what it was. It was no grain. It was nothing they had sown or reaped or purchased. It lay where the dew had been, pale and passing strange.

Moses said unto them, "This is the bread which the Lord hath given you to eat."

He gave them commandment. "Gather of it every man according to his eating, an omer for every man, according to the number of your persons. Take ye every man for them which are in his tents."

The people went forth. They gathered. Households moved through the camp in the early light, bending low, taking up the substance into baskets and jars. Some gathered swiftly, filling their vessels with broad sweeps. Others wrought more slowly. Children crouched beside their parents, their small hands scraping the pale flakes from the earth. The camp stirred with purpose.

Some gathered much. Others gathered little.

When they did mete it with an omer, the measure was the same for all. He that gathered much had nothing over. He that gathered little had no lack. Every man gathered according to his eating.

The bread had come. The murmuring had been answered. And in the wilderness, where there was naught, the ground itself gave forth — measured, sufficient, and new every morning.
