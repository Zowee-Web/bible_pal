The whole congregation of the people of Israel set out from Elim and came to the wilderness of Sin, which is between Elim and Sinai. It was the fifteenth day of the second month after they had departed from the land of Egypt.

A month and a half. That was how long they had been free. Behind them lay the Red Sea crossing, the waters of Marah made sweet, the twelve springs and seventy palm trees of Elim. Ahead lay Sinai, though they did not yet know what waited there. Around them, in every direction, the wilderness spread — dry, open ground under a wide sky.

The land offered nothing. No fields. No orchards. No storehouses. The provisions they had carried out of Egypt were running low. What grain remained was carefully rationed. The animals they had brought were too valuable to slaughter freely. The people looked at the land and saw what it was: empty.

They grumbled.

The whole congregation grumbled against Moses and Aaron in the wilderness.

"If only we had died by the Lord's hand in the land of Egypt," they said, "when we sat by the meat pots and ate bread to the full. You have brought us out into this wilderness to kill this whole assembly with hunger."

The complaint was not quiet. It moved through the camp like wind through dry grass — tent to tent, family to family, the same words repeated with growing bitterness. They remembered Egypt. Not the slavery. Not the bricks made without straw. Not the beatings or the cries of their children. They remembered the food. The pots of meat. The bread eaten until they were satisfied.

Hunger rewrites memory. It takes what was unbearable and makes it seem tolerable. It takes freedom and makes it feel like a death sentence. The people were not ungrateful by nature. They were afraid. Their bellies were empty, and the wilderness gave no sign of mercy.

The Lord spoke to Moses.

"I will rain bread from heaven for you."

The words were direct. No preamble. No rebuke — not yet.

"The people shall go out and gather a day's portion every day, that I may test them, whether they will walk in my law or not. On the sixth day, when they prepare what they bring in, it will be twice as much as they gather daily."

There were conditions. The bread would come, but it would come with instructions. A day's portion each day. Not two days' worth. Not a week's supply stored up against uncertainty. One day at a time. This was the test — whether a people who had just learned to be free could also learn to trust.

Moses and Aaron gathered the people together. The congregation assembled, faces tight with hunger and frustration.

"At evening," Moses and Aaron told them, "you will know that the Lord brought you out of the land of Egypt. And in the morning you will see the glory of the Lord, for he has heard your grumbling against him."

Moses paused. He looked at the crowd.

"What are we, that you grumble against us? Your grumbling is not against us but against the Lord."

The distinction mattered. Moses and Aaron were messengers. The people had aimed their anger at the men standing in front of them, but the complaint reached further than that. They were grumbling against the God who had parted the sea, who had turned bitter water sweet, who had brought them out of bondage with signs and wonders.

Moses said to Aaron, "Say to all the congregation of the people of Israel, 'Come near before the Lord, for he has heard your grumbling.'"

Aaron spoke to the whole congregation. As he spoke, they looked toward the wilderness. The glory of the Lord appeared in the cloud. It was visible — not imagined, not a feeling, but something the eye could see. The cloud shifted and shone with a presence that stopped the grumbling in their throats.

The Lord spoke to Moses again. "I have heard the grumbling of the people of Israel. Say to them, 'At twilight you shall eat meat, and in the morning you shall be filled with bread. Then you shall know that I am the Lord your God.'"

The afternoon wore on. The sun moved toward the horizon. The camp waited.

That evening, quail came.

They came up over the edge of the wilderness, wave after wave, settling on the ground. The birds covered the camp. They landed between the tents, on the open ground, in every clear space. The people had not gone looking for them. The quail came to the people. Meat — delivered without effort, without hunting, without anything but the promise spoken that morning.

The people gathered the birds. Fires were lit. The smell of roasting meat moved through the camp for the first time in weeks. Families ate. The grumbling quieted. Bellies that had been empty began to fill.

Then came the night. The wilderness cooled. The stars appeared. The camp settled into the kind of silence that follows a meal after long hunger.

In the morning, dew lay around the camp.

It covered the ground in a thin layer, as it had on other mornings in the wilderness. But when the dew lifted — when the sun climbed and the moisture burned away — something remained. On the surface of the wilderness, there lay a fine, flake-like thing. It was fine as frost on the ground. It was thin and pale, covering the earth in every direction around the camp.

The people of Israel came out of their tents. They looked at it. They looked at one another.

"What is it?" they said.

The Hebrew word was manna — "what is it?" They named the substance with a question, because they had no other name for it. It was not grain. It was not anything they had planted or harvested or bought in a market. It lay on the ground where dew had been, strange and unexplained.

Moses said to them, "It is the bread that the Lord has given you to eat."

The bread from heaven. Promised the day before. Delivered in the night, laid down with the dew, revealed when the moisture lifted. It was not what they expected bread to look like. It was not what they expected provision to look like. But it was there.

Moses gave them the instructions. "This is what the Lord has commanded: gather of it, each one of you, as much as he needs. Take an omer per person, according to the number of the persons that each of you has in his tent."

The camp came alive. People moved out from their tents with vessels and baskets. They bent to the ground and gathered. Some worked quickly, scooping the flakes into their containers with both hands. Others gathered more slowly, carefully lifting the fine substance from the earth. Families spread out across the ground around the camp. Children helped. The elderly moved at their own pace.

Some gathered much. Their baskets were full, heaped up, more than seemed necessary. Others gathered little — perhaps they were slow, or perhaps they trusted the instruction and took only what seemed right for their household.

When they brought what they had gathered back to the camp and measured it with an omer, the result confounded expectation. Whoever had gathered much had nothing left over. Whoever had gathered little had no lack. Each person had exactly what he needed. No more. No less.

The surplus did not exist. The shortage did not exist. The measure was perfect — not because the people had measured perfectly, but because the provision itself was sufficient. It met each person where they were, with exactly what they needed.

The bread had come. It lay on the ground of a wilderness where nothing grew, and it was enough. The grumbling had been heard. The hunger had been answered. And the people of Israel stood in the morning light, holding what they needed for the day — no more, no less — in a place where, the day before, there had been nothing at all.
