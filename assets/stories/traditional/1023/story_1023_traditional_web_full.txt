The whole congregation of the people of Israel set out from Elim and came to the wilderness of Sin, between Elim and Sinai. It was the fifteenth day of the second month after they had departed from Egypt.

The wilderness stretched in every direction — dry, open, and empty. The ground was hard dirt and pale stone. There were no fields, no storehouses, no cooking pots. The people looked at the land around them, and what they saw offered nothing.

They grumbled against Moses and Aaron.

The voices rose from different parts of the camp — bitter, sharp, overlapping.

"If only we had died by the Lord's hand in Egypt," they said, "when we sat by the meat pots and ate bread to the full. You have brought us out into this wilderness to kill this whole assembly with hunger."

The complaint was specific. They remembered Egypt — not the slavery, not the whip. They remembered the food. Hunger has a way of rewriting memory. What was unbearable becomes tolerable when the belly is empty.

The Lord said to Moses, "I will rain bread from heaven for you. The people shall go out and gather a day's portion every day, that I may test them, whether they will walk in my law or not. On the sixth day, when they prepare what they bring in, it will be twice as much as they gather daily."

Moses and Aaron gathered the people. They stood before the assembly and spoke.

"At evening," they said, "you will know that the Lord brought you out of the land of Egypt. And in the morning you will see the glory of the Lord, for he has heard your grumbling against him. What are we, that you grumble against us?"

Moses continued. "The Lord will give you meat to eat in the evening, and bread to the full in the morning. For the Lord has heard your grumbling that you grumble against him. Your grumbling is not against us but against the Lord."

The people listened. The wilderness was still around them.

That evening, quail came up and covered the camp. The birds arrived in great numbers, settling on the ground between the tents, filling the open spaces. The people gathered them. Meat — provided without labor, without explanation beyond the promise made that morning. The fires burned. The smell of roasting quail filled the camp as the sun went down.

Then came the night. And after the night, the morning.

When the dew had gone up, there lay on the surface of the wilderness a fine, flake-like thing, small as frost on the ground. It covered the earth around the camp in a thin, pale layer — delicate, catching the early light. It lay on the dirt and stones, as though the ground itself had produced something overnight that had never been there before.

The people came out and saw it. They looked at one another.

"What is it?" they said.

They did not know what it was. It was not grain. It was not anything they had planted or harvested or bought in a market. It lay where the dew had been, pale and strange.

Moses said, "It is the bread that the Lord has given you to eat."

He gave them instructions. "Gather of it, each one of you, as much as he needs. Take an omer per person, according to the number of persons in your tent."

The people went out. They gathered. Families moved through the camp in the early light, bending low, scooping the fine substance into baskets and jars. Some gathered quickly, filling their vessels with broad sweeps. Others worked slowly. Children crouched beside their parents, their small hands scraping the pale flakes from the ground. The camp stirred with purpose.

Some gathered much. Others gathered little.

When they measured what they had with an omer, the result was the same for all. Whoever had gathered much had nothing left over. Whoever had gathered little had no lack. Each had exactly what he needed.

The bread had come. The grumbling had been answered. And in the wilderness, where there was nothing, the ground itself provided — measured, sufficient, and new every morning.
