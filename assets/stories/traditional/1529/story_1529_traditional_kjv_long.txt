There dwelt in Bethlehem a man whose name was Boaz, a kinsman of Elimelech, who had been the husband of Naomi. A man of great substance was he. His fields stretched wide beyond the town, and his labourers were many. His name was spoken in the gates of Bethlehem, and his harvest each year was reckoned among the greatest in all the country round about.

Now Ruth, the woman of Moab, spake unto Naomi in the small dwelling wherein they had come to lodge. Dim was the room, and close. A single jar of clay stood near the door. And Ruth turned from the window, where she had been watching the road that led out toward the fields.

"Suffer me to go out into the field," she said, "that I may glean after him in whose sight I shall find grace."

Naomi looked upon her. Thin was her face and worn with the long way they had travelled. "Go, my daughter," said she.

So Ruth went forth out of the house and passed through the narrow ways of Bethlehem. Already the morning air was warm. Dust hung in the light where feet had troubled the road. Women bearing jars went by her toward the well. A boy drave a lean goat along the wall. And beyond the last stone walls the barley fields opened wide, row upon row, golden and heavy with grain ready for the sickle. The reapers moved in lines through the standing corn. The sound of their blades carried across the open ground — a steady cadence of iron and stalk.

Ruth came unto a field and began to gather. The loose ears that the reapers had let fall she took up, stooping low and drawing them from the stubble. She gathered them into the fold of her garment, pressed against her side. She knew not whose field it was. And it came to pass that the field was the field of Boaz.

The sun climbed higher. Heat pressed down upon the open land. Small clouds of insects rose where the reapers laboured. Ruth's hands moved without ceasing. She bent and gathered, and bent and gathered again. Chaff clung to the sweat upon her arms. Her shadow shrank beneath her as the noon drew near. From the early morning she had laboured, and scarce had she rested. Once she paused to wipe her brow with the back of her wrist, and bent again to the stubble.

Then came Boaz out of Bethlehem. He walked along the edge of the field, where the cut barley lay in sheaves, and as he drew near he lifted up his voice unto his reapers.

"The Lord be with you," said he.

And their voices rose together from the rows. "The Lord bless thee."

Boaz went among them. The labourers parted and gave way before him. He stayed to look upon a bound sheaf, lifted it once in his hand, and set it down again. And he lifted up his eyes over the field, and beheld a young woman whom he knew not. She laboured behind the reapers, gathering the stalks close to the ground. Plain was her raiment, and the hem of it stained with dust. She was none of his servants.

He turned unto the young man that was set over the reapers. "Whose damsel is this?" said he.

And the servant answered him. "She is the Moabitess that came back with Naomi out of the country of Moab. This morning came she unto me and besought leave to glean among the sheaves after the reapers. From that hour hath she been upon her feet. Scarce hath she sat down."

Boaz looked upon Ruth yet a little longer. Then went he across the cut rows to the place where she laboured. The stubble brake beneath his sandals. Some of the reapers lifted up their eyes, and bent again to their work. And Ruth saw him coming, and rose up. She put back the hair from her face with one hand. With the other she yet held the loose grain in her garment.

"Hearken unto me, my daughter," said Boaz. Steady was his voice, and clear. "Go not to glean in any other field. Depart not from this place. Abide here fast by my maidens. Mark where my reapers cut, and follow thou after them."

He paused. A warm wind passed through the standing grain beyond the line of the reapers. The stalks bowed in a long slow wave, and lifted up again.

"I have charged the young men," said he, "that they shall not touch thee. And when thou art athirst, go thou unto the vessels, and drink of that which the servants have drawn."

Ruth answered him not at once. She fell upon the ground. She fell upon her face and bowed herself low, her forehead near the dust, her palms flat against the rough stubble. Small splinters of stalk pressed into her skin. And the grain she had gathered spilled from her garment upon the ground beside her.

"Wherefore have I found grace in thine eyes?" said she from the ground. "Wherefore shouldest thou take knowledge of me, seeing I am a stranger?"

Boaz stood above her. Behind him the reapers laboured on, their sickles flashing in short arcs. Sweat darkened the cloth upon their shoulders. The bound sheaves lay along the rows. And further off, a boy bare a jar of water upon his shoulder toward the men.

"It hath fully been shewed me," said Boaz, "all that thou hast done unto thy mother in law since the death of her husband. How thou hast left thy father. How thou hast left thy mother. How thou hast left the land of thy birth, and art come unto a people which thou knewest not aforetime."

His shadow fell across her where she knelt. The wind passed again, and the standing grain bowed and rose. A bird called once from somewhere along the border of the field.

"The Lord recompense thy work," said he. "A full reward be given thee of the Lord God of Israel, under whose wings thou art come to take refuge."

Ruth lifted up her face. Dust was upon her brow, and in the creases of her hands. A streak of earth ran along one cheek. And she looked up unto Boaz.

"Let me find favour in thy sight, my lord," said she. "Thou hast comforted me. Thou hast spoken kindly unto thine handmaid, though I be not as one of thy maidens."

The reapers passed onward through the rows. The sound of their blades went not from the field. The boy with the jar came unto the line of labourers, and tilted the vessel that one of them might drink. And Ruth knelt in the stubble with her gathered grain about her, and Boaz stood before her in the bright open field.
