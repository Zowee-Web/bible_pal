Job had sat a great while upon the ash heap. Many days had he sat there. The ashes beneath him were grown cold in places, and in other places warm, where the sun had lain upon them. His flesh was smitten with boils from the crown of his head unto the soles of his feet. The dust of the ground had gathered itself into the furrows of his face. His robe was rent. His head was shaven.

And three men sat nigh unto him. Eliphaz. Bildad. Zophar. They had journeyed from far countries and had poured forth many words upon him. And he had answered them with many words of his own. Their contentions had endured for days, round upon round, voice upon voice, until the very air between them was spent. As a field that hath been turned by the plow too many times in a single season.

But now the speaking had ceased. And the silence lay upon them as a burden.

Then came the wind.

It did not rise after the manner of common winds, beginning softly and gathering strength. Nay, this wind fell upon them all at once from the east, a great wall of moving air that smote the ash heap and cast a veil of gray dust into their faces. The thorn bushes round about them were pressed flat against the earth. Loose stones rolled from their places. The hems of their garments snapped and twisted upon themselves.

And the sky was changed. Clouds drew together from quarters that bore no reason, gathering themselves overhead into a dark mass that swallowed up the sun. The warmth departed from the air. The light that remained was the colour of old bronze.

The wind drew tighter. It began to turn upon itself. It caught up ash and earth into a column that spiraled upward, and the column grew until it reached from the ground unto the belly of the clouds. And the sound thereof was exceeding great — a deep and unceasing roar, as the voice of a mighty river made a hundredfold greater. The whirlwind held its form and turned.

The three friends rose up upon their feet. They pressed their robes close against their bodies and lifted their forearms to shield their eyes. Their beards were driven sideways by the blast. They drew back.

But Job moved not. He sat upon the ash with his hands laid upon his knees, and the wind bore against his open sores.

Then a voice spake out of the whirlwind.

It was not thunder. Neither was it the sound of any man born of woman. It issued forth from the heart of the spinning column and filled all the space round about them, pressing into their ears, into their chests, into the very ground beneath them. The earth trembled.

"Who is this that covereth counsel in darkness, speaking words wherein there is no knowledge?"

The words were clear, notwithstanding the great roaring of the wind. They passed through it as a blade passeth through linen. No man gave answer. No man drew breath.

"Stand now upon thy feet as one who is a man," the voice said. "For I shall put questions unto thee, and thou shalt give me answer."

The whirlwind turned. Its foot scraped against the ground and sent forth new clouds of ash into the air. The column was dark at its edges and darker still at its centre, and the voice came forth from that centre as though the darkness itself had opened its mouth.

"Where wast thou when I set the foundations of the earth in their place? Speak, if thou hast wisdom to declare it."

The mouth of Job did not open. His hands remained upon his knees. The ash about him rose in small spirals.

"Who appointed the measures thereof, if thou knowest? Or who drew the measuring line upon it?"

The voice did not stay itself for Job to give answer. It pressed onward, question upon question, and each one fell as a stone cast into water that hath no motion.

"Upon what are the pillars thereof made fast? Or who set down the chief cornerstone of it?"

Then came a change in the wind. The roar softened for a moment, only so long as a breath, enough that the words which followed bore a different manner — not softer, but broader, as though the heavens themselves had parted to let them through.

"When the stars of the morning lifted their voices together, and all the sons of God cried aloud for gladness."

The words abode in the air. Then the wind rose again with great force, and the voice returned unto its full strength.

"Or who barred the sea behind its doors, when it burst forth as from the womb of the deep?"

The three friends had withdrawn themselves further off. Eliphaz had fallen upon his knees. Bildad stood with both his hands pressed over his face. Zophar had turned his back unto the wind and bowed himself low upon the ground. Not one among them uttered a word.

"When I appointed the cloud for a covering upon it," the voice continued, "and wrapped it round about in thick darkness as in a garment. When I established the boundary I had decreed for it, and set the bar and the doors thereof in their place."

The whirlwind drew tighter. The column of spinning air grew narrower and the roar sank deeper. The ash heap shifted beneath Job. Small stones tumbled past his legs.

"And declared unto it, Thus far shalt thou come, and no further. Here shall thy swelling waves be brought to nothing."

The voice held upon the last word. The whirlwind turned still. Dust and ash rose in broad spirals about the place where Job sat. His torn robe was pressed flat against his ribs. His hands lay open upon his knees. And the wind pulled at the edges of all things — the cloth, the ash, the dry thorn — and the dark column turned against the darkened sky, and the sound thereof filled the valley from ridge unto ridge.
