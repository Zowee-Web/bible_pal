Long had Job sat in his place. Days were gone by. The ash heap beneath him had grown cold in some places and warm in others, where the sun had touched it. His flesh was broken with sores from the crown of his head unto the soles of his feet. In some places the sores were crusted over; in others they were split and weeping. The dust had gathered in the lines of his face and lay packed in the corners of his eyes. His robe was rent at the breast where his own hand had torn it. His head was shaven, and the stubble was come back uneven upon him.

A shard of broken pottery lay near his right hand. With it had he scraped himself. He had laid it down a while since, and had not taken it up again.

Three men sat nigh him. Eliphaz. Bildad. Zophar. From far countries had they come, and many words had they spoken unto him. And many words had he spoken back unto them. The arguments had gone on days together, round upon round, voice after voice, until the air between them was as a thing worn out. Like a field plowed too oft in one season. The hems of their robes were grayed with dust from their sitting upon the ground. Their faces were drawn.

Now the speaking was ended. The silence lay upon them like a weight. A fly crept across the ash near Job's foot. Somewhere down the slope a goat cried out once, and then was still.

Then came the wind.

It rose not as a common wind ariseth, soft at first and growing. This one smote them all at once out of the east, a wall of moving air that struck the ash heap and flung a curtain of gray dust into their faces. The scrub brush round about was bent flat unto the ground. Loose stones went rolling down the slope. The hems of their robes snapped and twisted about their legs. A jar of clay was overturned and rolled until it caught against a stone.

And the heavens were changed. Clouds gathered from quarters that made no sense, drawing together overhead into a dark mass that shut out the sun. The air grew cold. The light became as the color of old bronze. The shadows beneath the men's eyes deepened. The dry grass on the ridge above lay flat, and remained flat.

The wind drew tighter. It began to wheel about. It pulled ash and dirt upward in a spiraling column, and the column climbed until it reached from the ground unto the underside of the clouds. The sound thereof was great — a deep and unceasing roar, as the voice of a river magnified a hundredfold. The whirlwind held its shape and turned. Fragments of dry brush spun in its lower edges. The smell of the ash arose with it, sharp and ancient.

The three friends stood up. They pressed their robes against their bodies and shielded their eyes with their forearms. Their beards were blown sideways. They stepped backward, one slow step and then another, until they were many paces from where they had been sitting.

Job moved not. He sat in the ash with his hands upon his knees, and the wind pressed against his open sores. His robe was drawn tight against his ribs. His eyes were open.

Then spake a voice out of the whirlwind.

It was not thunder. It was not the sound of any man. It came forth from the heart of the spinning column and filled the place round about them altogether, pressing into their ears, into their breasts, into the ground beneath them. The earth trembled. Small pebbles near the feet of Job moved as though they were shaken by an unseen hand.

"Who is this that darkeneth counsel with words without knowledge?"

Clear were the words despite the roaring of the wind. They cut through it as a blade cutteth through cloth. None answered. None drew breath. The mouth of Eliphaz hung open. The hands of Bildad were gone up to his ears, and were dropped again to his sides.

"Gird up thy loins like a man," said the voice. "I will demand of thee, and thou shalt answer me."

The whirlwind turned. Its base scraped against the ground and sent up fresh plumes of ash into the air. Dark were the edges of the column, and darker yet the center, and the voice came forth from that center as though the darkness itself were speaking. The clouds above moved on, low and swift, but the column beneath abode in its place.

"Where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the earth? Declare, if thou hast understanding."

The mouth of Job opened not. His hands remained upon his knees. The ash about him rose in small spirals, and settled, and rose again. A streak of blood from one of his sores had dried along his arm. The wind moved it not.

"Who set the measures thereof, if thou knowest? Or who hath stretched the line upon it?"

The voice tarried not for Job to make answer. It pressed onward, question upon question, each one falling as a stone dropped into still water. Steady was the rhythm thereof. The pressure of it did not relent.

"Whereunto are the foundations fastened? Or who laid the cornerstone thereof?"

A shifting of the wind. The roar gave way for a moment, just enough that the next words came through with another quality — not softer, but wider, as though the sky itself had opened to let them pass.

"When the morning stars sang together, and all the sons of God shouted for joy."

The words hung in the air. For one breath the column seemed to slow. Then the wind surged again, and the voice returned unto its full strength, and the roar climbed up again to where it had been.

"Or who shut up the sea with doors, when it brake forth as out of the womb?"

The three friends had drawn yet further back. Eliphaz was fallen upon his knees in the dirt, his palms pressed flat against the ground. Bildad stood with both hands over his face, his fingers spread, his shoulders bent forward. Zophar had turned his back unto the wind and crouched low, his forehead near unto his knees. None of them spake. None of them lifted up their eyes.

"When I made the cloud the garment thereof," the voice went on, "and thick darkness a swaddling band for it. And brake up for it my decreed place, and set bars and doors."

The whirlwind drew tighter. The column of spinning air grew narrower, and the roar deepened. The ash heap shifted beneath Job. Small stones tumbled past his legs and came to rest against his feet. The shard of broken pottery slid a hand's breadth and was still.

"And said, 'Hitherto shalt thou come, but no further. Here shall thy proud waves be stayed.'"

The voice held upon the last word. The whirlwind kept turning. Dust and ash arose in wide spirals about the place where Job sat. His torn robe was pressed flat against his ribs. His hands lay open upon his knees. The wind pulled at the edges of all things — the cloth, the ash, the dry brush, the loose hair at the back of his head — and the dark column spun against the dark heavens, and the sound thereof filled the valley from ridge to ridge.
