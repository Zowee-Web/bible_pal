David knew sheep. He had known them since he was a boy in Bethlehem, walking behind his father's flock across dry hills where the ground cracked in summer and the wind carried dust into his teeth. He had counted them at dusk. He had searched for them in the dark. He had pulled thorns from their wool and carried the smallest ones across his shoulders when their legs gave out.

He had stood in the field and watched. He had calluses on his hands from the staff and scars on his knuckles from the rod.

The Lord is my shepherd, he said. I shall not lack anything.

A good shepherd knew where the grass grew thick. David had found those places himself, walking ahead of the flock at dawn while the air was still cool, looking for ground that was green and level. When he found it, he brought the sheep there and they lay down. Not because he forced them. Because there was nothing to fear. No predator crouching in the rocks. No scent of lion on the wind. The pasture was safe, and the flock knew it, and their legs folded beneath them one by one until the whole field was quiet.

He made them lie down in green pastures.

He led them beside still waters. David had learned early that sheep would not drink from a fast current. The sound frightened them. The pull of the water against their legs made them brace and stumble. A good shepherd found quiet pools, fed by slow streams where the surface barely moved. The banks were low enough for the smallest lamb to reach. The flock stood at the edge and dipped their mouths and drank, and their reflections held steady beneath them.

He restored their souls. He led them in paths of righteousness for his name's sake.

There were paths through the hills that were safe if you knew them. Narrow trails along ridgelines where the footing was solid and the way was clear. A shepherd who walked those paths kept his flock from the loose rock, from the steep drop, from the places where a wrong step meant a broken leg and a slow death. The right paths. The straight paths. The shepherd chose them not for his own ease but for his name, because a shepherd's reputation was built on the condition of his flock.

David had walked through valleys where the shadows were so deep the sun disappeared completely. Rock walls rose on both sides, close enough to touch. The light thinned to a gray strip overhead. The air turned cold. Sounds bent and echoed off the stone so that a man could not tell whether footsteps came from ahead or behind. The shadow of death lay across everything in those valleys. A lion could wait behind any outcrop. A bear could block the path around any turn.

He feared no evil. The shepherd was with him.

The rod in the shepherd's hand was short and heavy, made of hard wood, sometimes studded with stone or iron. David had used his own rod to strike a lion across the face and a bear across the skull. It was a weapon. The staff was different. The staff was long, with a crook at the top, and a shepherd used it to guide the flock, to pull a lamb back from the edge of a cliff, to reach into a crevice where a sheep had wedged itself. One for fighting. One for reaching. Both were in the shepherd's hands.

Your rod and your staff, they comfort me.

A table was prepared. Not behind a wall. Not inside a fortress. The table was set in the open, in the presence of enemies, in plain sight of every threat. The food was laid out across the surface. Bread. Meat. The cup was filled to the rim and past it.

Oil was poured on his head. It ran down across his forehead and along the sides of his nose and caught in his eyebrows. It was heavy and warm. The scent of it rose into the still air.

His cup ran over. The wine spilled past the rim and traced a dark line down the outside of the cup and pooled on the wood of the table beneath it.

Goodness and mercy followed him. They followed him the way two sheepdogs follow a flock, one on each side, their feet steady in the dust.

The table stood in the open air. The bread sat untouched beside the meat. The oil on his forehead caught the light. The cup was full past the brim, and a thin line of wine ran slow down its side, reaching the wood below.
