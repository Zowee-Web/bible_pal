David knew sheep. He had known them from his youth in Bethlehem, walking behind his father's flock across dry hills where the earth split open in the heat of summer and the wind drove dust between his teeth. He had numbered them at eventide. He had sought them in the darkness. He had drawn thorns from their wool and borne the smallest upon his shoulders when their legs could carry them no further.

He had stood in the open field and watched over them. His hands bore calluses from the staff, and his knuckles bore scars from the rod.

The Lord is my shepherd, he said. I shall not want for any thing.

A good shepherd knoweth where the grass groweth thick. David himself had found such places, walking before the flock at the breaking of the day while the air yet held its coolness, searching for ground that was green and level. And when he found it, he brought the sheep thither, and they lay down. Not because he compelled them. Because there was nothing to make them afraid. No beast crouching among the stones. No scent of the lion upon the wind. The pasture was safe, and the flock perceived it, and one by one their legs folded beneath them until the whole of the field was still.

He maketh them to lie down in green pastures.

He leadeth them beside still waters. David had learned in his youth that sheep would not drink from a swift current. The sound affrighted them. The pull of the water against their legs caused them to brace and to stumble. A good shepherd sought out quiet pools, fed by slow streams where the surface scarce moved at all. The banks were low, that even the smallest lamb might reach. And the flock stood at the water's edge and lowered their mouths and drank, and their reflections held steady beneath them upon the face of the pool.

He restoreth their souls. He leadeth them in paths of righteousness for his name's sake.

There were paths through those hills that were safe, if a man knew them. Narrow trails along the ridges where the footing was sure and the way lay open before them. A shepherd who walked those paths kept his flock from the loose stone, from the steep falling-away, from the places where one wrong step meant a broken limb and a slow death. The right paths. The straight paths. And the shepherd chose them not for his own ease but for the sake of his name, for a shepherd's name was built upon the condition of his flock.

David had walked through valleys where the shadows lay so deep that the sun was swallowed altogether. Walls of rock rose up on either side, near enough to touch with outstretched hands. The light above thinned to a grey band. The air grew cold. And sounds bent and returned upon themselves against the stone, so that a man could not discern whether footsteps drew near from before him or from behind. The shadow of death lay upon all things in those valleys. A lion might wait behind any outcrop of rock. A bear might stand across the path at any turning.

He feared no evil. For the shepherd was with him.

The rod in the shepherd's hand was short and heavy, hewn of hard wood, and sometimes set with stone or iron. David had wielded his own rod, and had struck a lion across the face and a bear across the skull. It was a weapon for the striking. But the staff was another thing. The staff was long, with a crook at its head, and a shepherd used it to guide the flock, to draw a lamb back from the edge of a precipice, to reach down into a crevice where a sheep had wedged itself fast. The one was for the fighting. The other was for the reaching. And both were in the shepherd's hands.

Thy rod and thy staff, they comfort me.

A table was prepared. Not behind a wall. Not within a fortress. The table was set in the open, in the presence of enemies, in the plain sight of every threat that stood near. And the food was laid upon it. Bread, and meat. The cup was filled unto the brim, and beyond.

Oil was poured upon his head. It ran down across his brow and along the sides of his face and gathered in his eyebrows. It was heavy and warm. And the fragrance thereof rose into the still air.

His cup ran over. The wine spilled past the rim and traced a dark line down the outside of the vessel and gathered upon the wood of the table beneath it.

Goodness and mercy followed after him. They followed him as two sheepdogs follow a flock, one upon each side, their feet steady in the dust of the way.

The table stood in the open air. The bread sat untouched beside the meat. The oil upon his brow caught the light. The cup was full past the brim, and a thin line of wine ran slowly down its side, reaching the wood below.
